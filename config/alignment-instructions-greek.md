# Atomic verse alignment contract

This contract is `nt-greek-verse-v126`. Apply it independently to each prepared verse or cited footnote-reading file. A footnote packet contains only the quoted translation, with its Greek reading selected beforehand; editorial commentary and citation labels are not alignment targets. Its source provenance and any documented limitations belong to that supplementary reading, not to the chapter's main-text projection.

1. Partition every supplied `s###` source ID and `t###` target ID exactly once. Never invent an ID.
2. Prefer the smallest defensible semantic unit. Split only when every resulting correspondence is independently defensible and that source–target allocation is clearly preferable to materially plausible alternatives; an imaginable partition is not enough. Judge lexical and grammatical contributions in context: a correspondence need not be a dictionary-literal gloss or exclude ordinary English helpers. Where English expresses a construction jointly and its pieces cannot be assigned non-arbitrarily, retain the fused rendering as `noncompositional`. Source or target token count is diagnostic, never sufficient grounds to split. This tests ownership of the rendering, not alternative heads or helper placements already settled by a specific rule; explicit rulings below, including fused `aligned` renderings, retain precedence.
3. `aligned` requires nonempty source and target arrays. Both heads must be members of their arrays.
4. `unrepresented` is source-only, with a valid source head, an empty target, and a null target head.
5. `added` is target-only, with an empty source and null source head. Its target head may be null or a member of the target array.
6. Reserve `noncompositional` for a genuinely indivisible phrase. Both heads are required and must belong to their respective arrays. Use the lexical English anchor as target head; where the source phrase has no uniquely defensible internal head, use its first source token as the deterministic display anchor rather than leaving the head null.
7. English helpers—articles, auxiliaries, pronouns, *of*, *to*, and similar words—may belong to the source word or phrase that licenses them. Do not mark them `added` mechanically.
8. A Greek article may align to English *the*. If it has no English realization, mark it `unrepresented`.
9. An inflected Greek verb may align to an English subject, auxiliary, and main verb. Choose the lexical English verb rather than its subject or auxiliary as the target head.
10. A one-token source unit takes that token as its source head. In a multi-source unit choose the lexical or syntactic head.
11. ASV addition markup is evidence, not an infallible `added` label.
12. The prepared source projection already applies selected textual readings and SBLGNT fallbacks for underdetermined units. Do not make new textual decisions inside the alignment file.
13. Audit preposition–article–noun and article–noun/name sequences. Split independently recoverable correspondences. If Greek `article + numeral + noun` maps cleanly to English `article + numeral + noun`, align each pair separately; a shared noun phrase is not sufficient reason to merge them. Do not group an unrepresented article with its noun merely for convenience.
14. A substantival article may correspond to an English pronoun or nominal expression such as *he*, *one*, or *who*. Decide from its function in context.
15. Reordered or discontinuous target tokens may occupy one unit when they realize one source item. Do not absorb unrelated intervening words into that unit.
16. Where materially different alignments remain defensible, say which one you took and why in the unit's note. There is no confidence field: it was dropped after `nt-greek-verse-v58`, because a note naming the competing reading is what a later reader can act on and a grade was not. Units aligned under an earlier contract still carry one. Where a rule below cites a corpus unit as `medium`, that is a historical reading of how hedged the precedent is — evidence about that unit, never an instruction to record anything.
17. A target head is the cross-language lexical display anchor: the English token that most directly realizes the source head's lexeme, with English syntax used only to break a genuine tie. Do not choose a word merely because it is first, finite, or the phrase's formal syntactic head. Choose the lexical predicate over subjects and auxiliaries (`was given` heads on `given`), and the meaning-bearing complement in support/light-verb or resultative renderings (`make straight` heads on `straight`; `bear witness` on `witness`). When a substantival Greek adjective or demonstrative is expanded with a generic English noun, choose the adjective or demonstrative (`all things` heads on `All`; `good thing` on `good`). That clause is scoped to **anarthrous** Greek, where no article stands to take the supplied noun. Where a Greek article governs the phrase, rule 102 gives the article the supplied noun and leaves the adjective or demonstrative its own bare word, so no shared head is in question: `τὰ πάντα` → *all things* is the article's *things* and the quantifier's *all*, as `τὸ τρίτον` → *the third part* is under rule 49, and heading the whole phrase on *all* is the error rule 102 records JAS.1.11 correcting. When a case-inflected Greek article becomes `on the`, choose `the`; the preposition realizes case. For a source noun rendered by `of + noun`, choose the noun. Review this choice independently, and split separately recoverable names rather than assigning an arbitrary shared head.
18. This verse is the whole unit of work. Nothing carries to another verse except what a chapter has recorded in its conventions log.

## The article decides most of the work

Epistolary prose is article-heavy, and the recurring failure is deciding an article by its dictionary label instead of by what it does in English. It can:

19. Surface as English *the*. Align it to that word.
20. Be displaced by a possessive: `τοῦ θελήματος αὐτοῦ` → *of his will*. The article is `unrepresented`, the possessive belongs to `αὐτοῦ`, and *of* stays with the noun. Displacement is what a genitive pronoun does, so where there is no such pronoun the article is not displaced and is not `unrepresented`: it is what the supplied possessive realizes. `ἐν τῇ καρδίᾳ` → *in my heart*, `τὴν ἀπολύτρωσιν` → *our redemption*, `τὸν ἄνδρα` → *her husband* align the article to *my*, *our*, *her*, headed there, with any case preposition riding along as in rule 21. Do not mark such a possessive `added`: rule 11 makes the ASV's italics evidence, not a verdict. The test is whether some other word is present to take the possessive; if one is, rule 20 proper applies and the article realizes no determiner. A genitive pronoun is the ordinary case, but not the only one: a possessive dative does it (`εἰς τὴν χρείαν μοι` → *unto my need*, with *my* on `μοι`), a genitive demonstrative does it (`τῇ τούτων ἀπειθείᾳ` → *by their disobedience*, with *their* on `τούτων`), and so does a genitive noun or name the ASV renders with *'s* (`τῆς Καίσαρος οἰκίας` → *Caesar's household*, `ὁ θρόνος τοῦ Σατανᾶ` → *Satan's throne*). What matters is that the English possessive already has a source word to belong to, not the case that word is in. What displacement empties is the **determiner slot** and nothing else, so an article whose determiner another word has taken is not thereby emptied of an English word of some other kind the ASV supplies for it. Where the ASV writes such an article a separate **antecedent pronoun** under rule 92, or an **address word** under rule 89, the article takes that word and the possessive still belongs to the possessor: `μου τὴν σάρκα` → *them that are my flesh* gives *my* to `μου`, *them* to the article, and the discontinuous *that are … flesh* to `σάρκα` under rule 15, exactly as rule 89's REV.19.5 gives *ye* to an article whose possessive slot `αὐτοῦ` already occupies. An antecedent pronoun is not a determiner, and a possessor cannot displace what is not one. The contrast is an antecedent some other source token already spells: where the Greek expresses the word the relative clause is predicated of, the article has nothing left to take and is `unrepresented` under rule 23, as `Ὑμῖν … τοῖς ἔθνεσιν` → *to you that are Gentiles* leaves it empty because `ὑμῖν` has written the *you*. So leave a displaced article `unrepresented` only where the ASV supplies it nothing else, which is the ordinary case and the one the examples above record.
21. Carry a case preposition: `τοῦ πληρώματος` → *of the fulness*. Then *of* and *the* form one unit headed on *the*, and the noun stands alone. The English *the* is what anchors that unit, so where the ASV gives the noun no determiner the article carries nothing: it is `unrepresented` under rule 23 and the case preposition stays with the noun. `τῇ προσευχῇ` → *by prayer*, `τῆς εἰρήνης` → *of peace*, `τῷ θεῷ` → *to God*, `τοῖς ἀνθρώποις` → *unto men* are all one unit on the noun. Do not read this rule as licensing a bare *of* or *by* on the article; a determinerless English phrase is rule 23's case, not this one. This rule speaks throughout of a preposition realizing the governed word's **own case**, and an English preposition licensed by something else does not move to that word merely because it stands in front of it. Where the ASV builds a support-verb frame around the Greek verb and the frame's own supplied noun is what licenses the preposition, the preposition belongs to the frame and stays inside the verb's unit, the governed word keeping a bare noun: `οὐ λογίζεται τὸ κακόν` → *taketh not account of evil* gives *taketh … account of* to `λογίζεται` headed on *account* and a bare *evil* to `κακόν`, the article standing `unrepresented` under rule 23 as it would in any event, and `ἀναμνήσει … τὰς ὁδούς μου` → *shall put you in remembrance of my ways* gives *shall put … in remembrance of* to `ἀναμνήσει` headed on *remembrance* and a bare *ways* to `ὁδούς`. Rule 27 is what forces that, the supplied *account* or *remembrance* being what licenses the *of*, and rule 106's accusative arm says the same from the other side: an accusative has no case for an English preposition to realize, so a preposition standing over an accusative object is the verb's or the frame's and never the object's. That reaches the occurrence the frame **licenses** and no further. Where the ASV **repeats** that same preposition over a second complement of the frame, the repetition is decided by rule 103 and not here: it is a rule 7 helper on the member it introduces, whatever that member's case, and it is neither `added` nor stretched back over the frame under rule 15. `Οὕτως ἡμᾶς λογιζέσθω ἄνθρωπος ὡς ὑπηρέτας Χριστοῦ` → *Let a man so account of us as of ministers of Christ* gives *Let … account of* to `λογιζέσθω`, a bare *us* to the accusative `ἡμᾶς`, and the second *of* to `ὑπηρέτας` though that word is accusative too, rule 103 already forbidding both the stretch and the `added` for a repeated word from rule 7's own list and scoping rule 106's accusative arm the same way. That rule's own clause speaks of a Greek preposition or conjunction governing two coordinate members, and a supplied frame noun licensing two complements is the same case: what makes a repetition a helper is that the word it repeats has already been spent, not what licensed it. So ask which occurrence is in question before asking what case the word in front of it carries. A genitive or a dative is the contrast and is this rule's ordinary case, its own case being what the English preposition answers to, which is why `τῶν ἀνθρώπων` → *of men* and `τῇ ἀληθείᾳ` → *with the truth* are decided here and not there.
22. Substantivize with no English determiner: `τὰ` → *things*, `τοῦ` → *of him who*, `τοὺς` → *we who*, `τῷ` → *that which*. Head such a unit on the nominal word, not on the relativizer. The English expression may bring its own determiner — `τὰ` → *the things*, `τὸ` → *the part*, `οἱ` → *the one* and *the other* — and the head is still the nominal word and never the determiner. Rule 17's *on the* → *the* is the contrast: there the article's own case is what the preposition realizes, so nothing nominal competes with *the*. Where a participle follows, rule 32 splits this instead and the article keeps only the pronoun — where the ASV writes one. Where the ASV writes for such an article a determiner and a **supplied nominal** in place of a pronoun, rules 102 and 103 give the article both words, headed on the nominal, and the participle keeps the relativizer, any supplied copula and its own verb: `τὴν ἀποκεκρυμμένην` → *even the wisdom that hath been hidden* gives *the wisdom* to the article and *that hath been hidden* to the participle. Rule 32's *and nothing else* limits what the article may absorb beyond the English the ASV wrote for it, as rule 90 already reads that phrase, and it does not deny it a nominal where no pronoun was written. Rule 103's article clause is what folds that nominal rather than marking it `added`, and it folds it however plainly the nominal echoes a noun the same verse expresses elsewhere, no noun being expressed inside the article's own phrase; GAL.3.20 is the contrast, its article governing an expressed noun so that the article's phrase is already complete and the repeat stands in a slot no token governs. A supplied appositional *even* standing in front of such a phrase is untouched by this and remains its own `added` unit under rule 105, realizing no part of the article.
23. Have no English realization at all, including a second attributive article (`τῷ πνεύματι τῷ ἁγίῳ`) and an article before a name. Then it is its own `unrepresented` unit.

## Two habits that prevent inconsistency

24. When the ASV renders one construction two ways in the same chapter — `εἰς τὸ` + infinitive as *to the end that* and as a bare *that* — analyse both the same way rather than following the surface.
25. When the ASV's word order contradicts the usual glosses, as in `τοῦ κράτους τῆς ἰσχύος αὐτοῦ` → *the strength of his might*, follow the ASV's own consistent rendering of that collocation instead of crossing the nouns.

## The recurring failure is under-atomization

26. Split adjacent source words whenever their English realizations remain independently recoverable. Do not bundle a compositionally transparent noun phrase: `ὁ ἀμνὸς` → *the Lamb* and `οἱ δύο μαθηταὶ` → *the two disciples* are recoverable token pairs. Phrase membership alone is never a reason for a many-to-many unit.
27. Conversely, do not split an English helper away from what licenses it. Reordering and discontinuity are not reasons to merge intervening words into one broad unit.
28. Review heads separately from edges. The common mistakes are choosing an auxiliary instead of the main verb, and giving one head to two names that should have been split.
29. Inside a compound numeral the English juncture *and* has no Greek counterpart but is obligatory idiom, so it is a helper and not `added`: attach each *and* to the numeral token it introduces, and keep the numerals themselves separate even when the ASV reorders them. `ἑκατὸν τεσσεράκοντα τέσσαρες χιλιάδες` → *a hundred*, *and forty*, *and four*, *thousand*, each headed on its numeral; `εἴκοσι τέσσαρες` → *four* for `τέσσαρες` and *and twenty* for `εἴκοσι`, headed on *twenty*. That separation is scoped to where the ASV writes a word for each numeral, and it is not a licence to split one English word across them. Where the ASV compresses a compound numeral into a **single** English word, the numeral tokens and any `καί` standing between them are one `aligned` unit taking that word, headed on the token the word's stem realizes: `δέκα καὶ ὀκτὼ ἔτη` → *eighteen years* gives *eighteen* to the three tokens headed on `ὀκτώ`, and *years* to the noun. Rule 94's count test is what decides it, the count of English words the ASV wrote being the test there as here, and rule 39's test keeps the unit `aligned` rather than `noncompositional`, *eight-* answering to `ὀκτώ` and *-teen* to `δέκα`. The intervening `καί` is neither `added` nor `unrepresented`: this rule's own reason holds, an obligatory English juncture inside one numeral idiom belonging to the idiom, and here the idiom is spelt solid. Do not leave any member of such a compound empty in order to give the single word to one of them alone. Where the Greek writes the compound as one token the question does not arise and the unit is an ordinary one-to-one alignment, as `δεκαοκτώ` → *eighteen* is.
30. In the duration idiom `εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας τῶν αἰώνων` → *for ever and ever*, neither article has a distinct English realization, so each is its own `unrepresented` unit; the first `αἰῶνας` takes *ever* and the second takes *and ever* headed on *ever*, the juncture being a helper as in rule 29. Where the ASV compresses the phrase into *for evermore*, the two nouns share that one target instead.
31. A supplied English relative clause does not realize the article that governs it. Where the ASV renders an attributive participle as a finite clause — `τὴν δύναμιν τὴν ἐνεργουμένην` → *the power that worketh*, `τὸν καλούμενον` → *which is called* — the relativizer and any copula are helpers on the participle under rules 7 and 9, and the second attributive article stays its own `unrepresented` unit under rule 23. The test against rule 32 is whether the article governs an expressed head noun. `τῷ θεῷ τῷ κτίσαντι` → *God who created* has one, so the second article realizes nothing and *who* is the participle's helper; a bare `Τῷ δυναμένῳ` → *unto him that is able* has none, so the article is substantival and takes the pronoun itself. That test decides only the cases where a participle follows, because the participle is what carries the relativizer. It decides the **relativizer** alone and never the determiner, the two being separate questions: an article that **precedes** an expressed head noun of its own is that noun's determiner however the modifier standing between them is rendered, so where a participle intervenes the article takes the English *the* with any case preposition riding along under rule 21, and the participle still keeps the relativizer, any supplied copula and its own verb. `τοῖς κατοικοῦσιν ἐν τῇ Ἰουδαίᾳ ἀδελφοῖς` → *unto the brethren that dwelt in Judaea* gives *unto the* to the article, *that dwelt* to the participle and a bare *brethren* to the noun, exactly as rule 22's `τὴν ἀποκεκρυμμένην` → *even the wisdom that hath been hidden* divides a supplied nominal from the participle's own clause. The `unrepresented` article of this branch is the **second** attributive article, whose noun has already taken the determiner standing in front of it; an article with an English determiner of its own to carry is emptied neither by rule 23 nor by this rule. Where the article's modifier carries **no verb of its own** — a **prepositional phrase**, a bare **adverb**, or a **predicate adjective** — there is nothing there to carry the relativizer, so the article keeps the supplied relativizer whether or not it governs an expressed head noun, and the copula goes with the preposition, with the adverb or with the adjective: `τὰ στρατεύματα τὰ ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ` → *the armies which are in heaven* gives *which* to the second article and *are in* to `ἐν`, as `τὰ ἄνω` → *the things that are above* gives *the things that* to the article and *are above* to `ἄνω`. An adverb is no different from a prepositional phrase here, rule 53 already giving an adverbial predicate the supplied copula; and a **predicate adjective** is no different either, rule 53 naming an adjectival predicate beside those two, so a substantival article whose complement is an adjective keeps the antecedent pronoun and the relativizer together and the adjective keeps only the supplied copula and its own word: `Ὁ πιστὸς ἐν ἐλαχίστῳ` → *He that is faithful in a very little* gives *He that* to the article and *is faithful* to the adjective, exactly as `τῷ ἀλλοτρίῳ` → *that which is another's* and `τὸ ἐν ἀνθρώποις ὑψηλόν` → *that which is exalted among men* give *that which* to the article and the copula to the adjective. Rule 32's split, and rule 90's *He* / *that would*, are scoped in terms to a **participle**, which has a verb of its own to carry the relativizer, and are not a licence to strip the relativizer from an article whose complement is an adjective; where some other source token has already written the antecedent, rule 32's apposition clause empties the article and the relativizer falls to the adjective along with the copula, as `αὐτοῖς δὲ τοῖς κλητοῖς` → *unto them that are called* does. Where the article is substantival it takes the supplied nominal along with the relativizer under rule 22, headed on the nominal word and never on the determiner or on the relativizer. Which side takes the English *the* is decided by where the article stands, not by the relative clause. An article that **follows** its noun leaves the determiner on the noun, supplying one there when the noun is anarthrous: `καρπὸν δικαιοσύνης τὸν διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ` → *the fruits of righteousness which are through Jesus Christ* gives *the fruits* to `καρπόν` and only *which* to `τόν`, as `κατὰ δικαιοσύνην τὴν ἐν νόμῳ` → *as touching the righteousness which is in the law* gives *the righteousness* to `δικαιοσύνην`. An article that **precedes** the noun it governs is that noun's own determiner and takes *the* along with the relativizer, headed on *the*, leaving the noun bare: `τὴν ἐκ θεοῦ δικαιοσύνην` → *the righteousness which is from God* gives the discontinuous *the … which* to `τήν` and *righteousness* to `δικαιοσύνην`, as `τὴν καθ' ὑμᾶς πίστιν` → *the faith … which is among you* does. That is rule 22's determiner clause holding where a head noun is expressed: the article carries the English determiner unless its own noun has already stood in front of it. An article left `unrepresented` in front of such a verbless modifier is right only where the ASV supplies no relative clause at all.
32. A substantival article before a participle splits; it does not absorb the clause. `οἱ κατοικοῦντες` → *they that dwell* is `οἱ` → *they* and `κατοικοῦντες` → *that dwell* headed on *dwell*, never one unit taking *they that*. The article supplies the pronoun and nothing else, because the participle is what the relative clause is predicated of. Where **another source token has already written that pronoun** — an expressed pronoun or noun standing in apposition to the article's phrase — nothing is left for the article to supply and it is its own `unrepresented` unit, the participle keeping the supplied relativizer along with any copula and its own verb: `τοῖς δὲ σῳζομένοις ἡμῖν` → *unto us who are saved* gives *unto us* to `ἡμῖν` under rule 21, nothing to the article, and *who are saved* to the participle headed on *saved*. That is rule 20's stated contrast holding where a participle rather than a noun follows the article, `Ὑμῖν … τοῖς ἔθνεσιν` → *to you that are Gentiles* leaving it empty because `ὑμῖν` has written the *you*, and it is the same reading an articular **adjective** in that apposition takes under rules 20 and 92, `αὐτοῖς δὲ τοῖς κλητοῖς` → *unto them that are called* giving *unto them* to `αὐτοῖς` and *that are called* to `κλητοῖς`. Rule 76's availability test does not reach this: an antecedent is the very word the relative clause is predicated of rather than an English word of some other role, so a pronoun that has written it is spent for this purpose and not available to the article. Rule 90 is the near contrast and is untouched: where the ASV writes only one English word for the pair that word is the article's, being pronoun and relativizer at once, and this clause reaches only a phrase for which the ASV writes more. Rule 22 is the contrast wherever no participle follows, and this rule's split is scoped in terms to a participle: what carries a supplied relativizer is a modifier with a verb of its own, so where the article's complement instead carries none — a **prepositional phrase**, a bare **adverb**, or a **predicate adjective** — the article carries *of him who* or *that which* whole and the modifier keeps only the supplied copula and its own word, as rule 31 has it. `οἱ μετ' αὐτοῦ` → *they … that are with him* keeps the discontinuous *they … that* on the article, since a prepositional phrase has no verb to carry a relativizer, and the copula goes with `μετά`; `Ὁ πιστός` → *He that is faithful* keeps *He that* on the article and gives *is faithful* to the adjective, and `τῷ ἀλλοτρίῳ` → *that which is another's* does the same. Where such an article is emptied by the apposition clause above, the relativizer falls to the adjective with the copula exactly as it falls to a participle. A reference comparison will report such an `unrepresented` article as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction.
33. The emphatic negation `οὐ μή` is one `noncompositional` unit taking the whole English negation, headed on `οὐ` and on the English *no* where there is one. `οὐ μὴ εὑρεθῇ` → *shall … in no wise be found* keeps *in no wise* whole; `οὐ μὴ ἀκουσθῇ … ἔτι` → *shall be heard no more at all* keeps *no … at all* on the pair and leaves *more* to `ἔτι`. Do not split *at all* or *in no wise* across the two particles, and do not treat either particle as `unrepresented`.
34. In `ἀπὸ μακρόθεν` → *afar off*, the preposition is pleonastic and English realizes it nowhere separately, so `μακρόθεν` takes the whole idiom headed on *afar* and `ἀπό` is its own `unrepresented` unit. Do not put *off* on `ἀπό`: it is the adverb's own particle, not the preposition's. What empties the preposition is that rendering and not the Greek phrase, so where the ASV writes a preposition word of its own the pair splits under rule 2 and each token is `aligned` to what answers to it: `ἀπὸ μακρόθεν` → *from afar* gives *from* to `ἀπό` and *afar* to `μακρόθεν`. Rule 36 states the test this rule is an instance of — a preposition is left `unrepresented` only where English realizes it nowhere at all — and rules 42, 48 and 93 already let the ASV's phrasing decide which token a word answers to, so a chapter rendering the phrase both ways is writing rule 24's habit and not two analyses. Do not empty a preposition the ASV has written a word for merely because the same Greek phrase is elsewhere rendered as a single idiom.
35. Where the ASV fuses a preposition and its pronoun into one word — `ἐν ᾧ` → *wherewith*, `ἐν πᾶσιν` → *withal*, `εἰς αὐτό` → *thereunto* — both source tokens take that one target as a single `aligned` unit, headed on the pronominal. Rule 34 is the contrast, not the model: there the preposition has no English counterpart anywhere, whereas a portmanteau realizes it in the *-with* or *-unto* it carries. So do not leave the preposition `unrepresented`, and do not reach for `noncompositional`, which records a Greek idiom English cannot take apart rather than an English word that happens to be spelled solid. Where the single English word carries no part of either token, rule 45 is the case instead, and the two rules divide on exactly that test.
36. An English preposition that governs a prepositional phrase realizes the Greek preposition, not the verb it depends on, even where English idiom makes the verb phrasal. `τὰ περὶ ὑμῶν … μεριμνήσει` → *will care truly for your state* gives *for* to `περί` and *will care* to `μεριμνήσει`; so do *hear of your state*, *bear witness of the light*, *supplication for all*, and `περιεζωσμένοι … περὶ τὰ στήθη` → *girt about their breasts*, where *girt with* has already taken its own particle. Rule 27's warning against splitting a helper from what licenses it points the other way here, because the preposition is what licenses it. Leave a preposition `unrepresented` only where English realizes it nowhere at all — pleonastically as in rule 34, compressed into a possessive as in `τὰ περὶ ἡμῶν` → *our state*, or absorbed into a predicate — and never merely because the verb could be read as phrasal.
37. A difference between source and ASV verse boundaries is resolved in the prepared projection, before a verse is aligned. The selected Greek span is assigned to the ASV verse that translates it, retaining its original source identity and provenance. Align the supplied tokens normally, even when their original reference names another verse. Do not mark Greek `unrepresented` or English `added` merely because the editions number their corresponding words differently, and do not invent cross-packet IDs or choose a new source reading. If a packet still leaves corresponding text in a neighbouring verse, report an input defect for correction before accepting the pass; a note about the other verse does not complete the alignment. This rule supersedes older chapter conventions that prescribed source-only and target-only units for that mismatch. Genuine untranslated Greek and genuinely supplied English retain rules 4 and 5; a source reading absent from the selected projection remains rule 97's separate case.
38. An attributive demonstrative supplies the English determiner, so the article governing that noun is its own `unrepresented` unit. `τὸν ναὸν τοῦτον` → *this temple* and `ὁ ναὸς οὗτος` → *this temple* both give *this* to the demonstrative and nothing to the article, as `τὴν ἡμέραν ἐκείνην` → *that day*, `ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ὥρᾳ` → *in that hour*, and `ἐν ταῖς ἡμέραις ἐκείναις` → *in those days* do. Whether the demonstrative precedes or follows the article makes no difference. This is rule 20's displacement holding for a demonstrative rather than a possessive: another source word already occupies the English determiner slot, so the article realizes nothing. Do not supply the article a *the* the ASV never wrote, and do not split *this* or *that* across the demonstrative and the article. Where the ASV writes a **multiword** determiner expression for the demonstrative, that expression is the demonstrative's whole and the article still realizes nothing: `ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ὥρᾳ` → *the same hour* gives *the same* to `ἐκείνῃ`, headed on *same* and never on *the*, and leaves the article `unrepresented`, exactly as the substantival `αὕτη` → *The same* takes both words under rule 49. That is rule 49's clause holding for an attributive demonstrative — a demonstrative takes the whole English expression the ASV builds for it — so a written *the* is not evidence that the article is realized, and the count of English words is not the test here that rules 50 and 94 make it elsewhere. What the article may not be given is a determiner that some other source word's own English already spells. Rule 60 is the contrast and its test is unchanged: where no Greek demonstrative competes, a supplied demonstrative determiner is the article's own to take, which is why `αὐτῇ τῇ ὥρᾳ` → *that very hour* gives *that* to the article and *very* to the intensive pronoun. Rule 22 is not the contrast, because a substantival article governs no head noun; where the demonstrative stands alone, as `ταῦτα` → *these things*, no article is in question at all.
39. A conditional or purpose particle fused with the negative `μή` and rendered by one English word is one `noncompositional` unit, headed on that word and on the first particle. `ἐὰν μή` → *except*, `εἰ μή` → *but*, *save*, *but only*, `ἵνα μή` → *lest*, and `εἰ δὲ μή` → *or else* all join `οὐ μή` under rule 33: the English conjunction realizes neither particle separately, so do not give it to one and mark the other `unrepresented`, and do not split it across the two. Rule 35 is the contrast and it is a narrow one. It applies where the single English word visibly carries both source elements — `οὐ δύναται` → *cannot* spells the negation and the modal solid, `οὐδὲ ἕν` → *nothing* spells the negation and the numeral solid — and such a unit stays `aligned`. So does a cluster whose second member is a contentful indefinite rather than a particle: `εἴ τις` → *whoso*. The test is whether some part of the English word answers to a source token, not whether the phrase is idiomatic. The optative formula `μὴ γένοιτο` is this rule's case where the second member is a **verb** rather than a particle, and it is one `noncompositional` unit taking the whole English formula, headed on the **verb** and on the English word that carries the prohibition: `μὴ γένοιτο` → *God forbid* is headed on `γένοιτο` and on *forbid*, neither English word answering to either token separately, as the ASV's own margin shows in giving the literal *Be it not so*. The count of English words is not the test here that rule 94 makes it for a prepositional phrase, rule 33 already taking a multiword English negation whole onto a particle pair. So do not mark the supplied *God* `added` under rule 5 — no token of the formula is spent, and it is the second word of one indivisible English idiom rather than a word standing in a slot no token governs — do not give *forbid* to the verb and leave `μή` its own `unrepresented` unit under rules 41 or 59, which empty a negator only before a second **negative** word able to carry the English negation alone, and do not split the two English words across the two tokens. Every corpus unit whose English is *God forbid* reads it this way: seven of them in six chapters before Romans 3, and three more in that chapter at 3:4, 3:6 and 3:31, with none split and none headed on *God*.
40. A subordinating conjunction the ASV renders by the infinitival *to* is aligned to that *to*, and the verb stands alone. `ἵνα … ἀγοράσωσιν` → *to buy* gives *to* to `ἵνα` and *buy* to the subjunctive, as `ἵνα ποιήσω` → *to do* does. Rule 24 is what forces this: the ASV renders purpose `ἵνα` sometimes as *that* and sometimes as a bare infinitive, and both are the conjunction realized by whatever word introduces the English clause — John 3:17 does both in one verse, giving *to* to the first `ἵνα` and *that* to the second. So this is not rule 7's infinitive marker, which belongs to its verb wherever no conjunction governs the clause (`ἀντλῆσαι` → *to draw*, `προσκυνεῖν` → *to worship*). Where one conjunction governs two coordinated subjunctives the English repeats the marker, and the repetition is an ordinary helper on the second verb: `ἵνα ποιήσω … καὶ τελειώσω` → *to do … and to accomplish* gives the first *to* to `ἵνα` and *to accomplish* whole to `τελειώσω`. Do not read the unmatched second *to* as evidence that the first belongs to its verb. A conjunction translated across an edition's verse boundary must arrive in the ASV packet under rule 37; that boundary does not license `unrepresented`.
41. Where a clause carries a redundant Greek negative — `οὐ` with a following negative word, whether a negative indefinite such as `οὐδέν` or `οὐδὲ ἕν` or a negative adverb such as `οὐκέτι` — and the ASV realizes the negation once, in that following word, that word takes the whole English negation and `οὐ` is its own `unrepresented` unit. `οὐ δύναται ὁ υἱὸς ποιεῖν … οὐδέν` → *The Son can do nothing* gives *nothing* to `οὐδέν` and nothing to `οὐ`, as `Οὐ δύναμαι ἐγὼ ποιεῖν … οὐδέν` → *I can of myself do nothing* and `Οὐ δύναται ἄνθρωπος λαμβάνειν οὐδὲ ἕν` → *A man can receive nothing* do, and as `καὶ οὐκ εἶδεν αὐτὸν οὐκέτι ὁ εὐνοῦχος` → *and the eunuch saw him no more* gives *no more* to `οὐκέτι` and nothing to `οὐκ`. Rule 59 carries this to `μή` and states the licensing test, which is the whole of this rule: the following word must itself be a negative able to carry the English negation alone, which is why an adverb serves as well as an indefinite and why a plain `τις` or `τι` does not. Do not split the English negation across the two, and do not join them into one unit: the two negatives are not adjacent, and what stands between them is the verb and its complements. Where the ASV writes an English word for content of the negator's own that the negation does not absorb, the negator is not emptied but keeps that word as its own `aligned` unit: `οὐδέπω γὰρ ἦν ἐπ᾽ οὐδενὶ αὐτῶν ἐπιπεπτωκός` → *for as yet it was fallen upon none of them* gives *as yet* to `οὐδέπω` and *none* to `οὐδενί`, the temporal element being no part of what *none* carries. Rule 33's `οὐ μή` is the contrast and it is a narrow one — there the particles stand side by side and the English negation answers to the pair, so they are one `noncompositional` unit. Where the ASV instead spells the negation into the modal, `οὐ δύναται` → *cannot* stays one `aligned` unit under rule 39 and the question does not arise. Rule 69 is untouched: where the ASV renders the following negative non-negatively, as *any* or *any man*, it cannot carry the negation alone and the negator keeps the English word that does. A reference comparison will report the `unrepresented` `οὐ` as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction.
42. An improper preposition — an adverb such as `πέραν` or `ἐγγύς` that governs a case — keeps only the English phrase that renders the adverb itself, and where the ASV supplies a determiner for the governed noun the accompanying English preposition realizes the case and rides with the article under rule 21. `πέραν τῆς θαλάσσης` → *on the other side of the sea* gives *on the other side* to the adverb and *of the* to the article, as `ἐγγὺς τοῦ τόπου` → *nigh unto the place* gives *nigh* to the adverb and *unto the* to the article. Do not read the supplied preposition as part of the adverb's own idiom merely because *nigh unto* reads as one phrase in English; the case is what it answers to, which is why an article after an adverb takes *unto the* in `ὡς τῷ κυρίῳ` → *as unto the Lord*. Where the adverb is itself rendered by a single English preposition the question does not arise and the article takes a bare *the*: `πέραν τοῦ Ἰορδάνου` → *beyond the Jordan*, `ὑποκάτω τῆς συκῆς` → *under the fig tree*, and `πέραν τῆς θαλάσσης` → *over the sea*, which is the same Greek as the first example and shows that the ASV's phrasing decides. Rule 34 is the contrast and a narrow one: there the preposition has no English realization anywhere, whereas here the English preposition answers to the governed case. Where the ASV gives the noun no determiner the article has no *the* to anchor and is `unrepresented` under rule 23, and since rule 21 forbids leaving a bare *of* or *unto* on it, the supplied preposition stays where the ASV's phrasing puts it: `ἐγγὺς τοῦ Σαλείμ` → *near to Salim* keeps *near to* whole.
43. Recitative `ὅτι` — the conjunction introducing direct speech, which English marks by quotation rather than by a word — has no English realization and is its own `unrepresented` unit. `ἔλεγον ὅτι Οὗτός ἐστιν` → *they said, This is* and `λέγει ὅτι Ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ καταβέβηκα` → *doth he say, I am come down out of heaven* leave the conjunction empty, as `ὡμολόγησεν ὅτι Ἐγὼ οὐκ εἰμὶ ὁ χριστός` → *he confessed, I am not the Christ* does. The test is the English, not the Greek: where the ASV renders the clause as reported speech and supplies *that*, the conjunction is aligned to that word in the ordinary way, so rule 24 applies to a chapter that does both. Do not reach for `added` on the opening word of the quotation, and do not stretch the conjunction over it. Reported speech is not a guarantee that a word is there to align to, because English also drops the complementizer: `ὃν ὑμεῖς λέγετε ὅτι τυφλὸς ἐγεννήθη` → *who ye say was born blind* is indirect, is punctuated as indirect, and still leaves `ὅτι` its own `unrepresented` unit, because no English word answers to it. So the reported-versus-recitative distinction is not what decides the status; the presence of an English word is. Do not manufacture one by stretching the conjunction onto the clause's subject or verb, and do not relabel such a clause recitative merely to reach the right status by the wrong route.
44. Interrogative `μή` — the particle marking a question that expects a negative answer, which English marks by word order rather than by a word — has no English realization and is its own `unrepresented` unit. `Μὴ καὶ ὑμεῖς πεπλάνησθε;` → *Are ye also led astray?*, `Μὴ καὶ σὺ ἐκ τῆς Γαλιλαίας εἶ;` → *Art thou also of Galilee?* and `μὴ πλείονα σημεῖα ποιήσει;` → *will he do more signs?* all leave the particle empty, as `μὴ σὺ μείζων εἶ τοῦ πατρὸς ἡμῶν` → *Art thou greater than our father* and `Μὴ καὶ ὑμεῖς θέλετε ὑπάγειν;` → *Would ye also go away?* do. This is rule 43's case for a particle rather than a conjunction, and the test is the same one: the English, not the Greek. Where the ASV supplies an interrogative word, the particle is aligned to that word in the ordinary way — `Μὴ γὰρ ἐκ τῆς Γαλιλαίας ὁ χριστὸς ἔρχεται;` → *What, doth the Christ come out of Galilee?* gives *What* to `μή`, and `μήποτε ἀληθῶς ἔγνωσαν` → *Can it be that … indeed know* gives the whole supplied frame *Can it be that* to `μήποτε`. Do not reach for `added` on that supplied word: rule 11 makes the ASV's italics evidence rather than a verdict, and the particle is what the word answers to. An ordinary negative `μή` is untouched by this rule and is aligned to *not* like any other. A reference comparison will report every `unrepresented` interrogative `μή` as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction.
45. A preposition and the word it governs that the ASV renders by one English word answering to neither token separately are one `noncompositional` unit, headed on the governed word. `διὰ τί` → *why*, `διὰ τοῦτο` → *wherefore*, `μετὰ ταῦτα` → *hereafter*, `καθ᾿ ἕνα` → *severally*, `ἐν ᾧ` → *while*, and `ἐπὶ τόπον` → *any whither* are each one unit. Do not leave the preposition `unrepresented` and give the English word to its complement alone, and do not split that word across the two. Rule 35 is the contrast and the test is the one rule 35 already states: whether some part of the English word answers to a source token. Where it does, the unit stays `aligned` — `ἐν ᾧ` → *wherewith* spells the preposition into *-with* and `εἰς αὐτό` → *thereunto* into *-unto* — which is why the same `ἐν ᾧ` is `aligned` as *wherewith* and `noncompositional` as *while*. Where the ASV instead spreads the phrase over words that do answer to each token, neither rule is in question and the ordinary analysis applies: `διὰ τοῦτο` → *For this cause* gives *for … cause* to `διά` and *this* to the demonstrative. Rule 39 is this same test for a particle cluster, and this rule is it for a prepositional phrase.
46. The postposition `χάριν` takes the whole discontinuous English frame the ASV builds around it — `Τούτου χάριν` → *For this cause*, `αἰσχροῦ κέρδους χάριν` → *for filthy lucre's sake* — as one `aligned` unit headed on the noun, *cause* or *sake*, and the genitive it governs stands alone on its own English word. The English preposition is supplied because no **proper** Greek preposition is present for it to answer to, and the noun is what carries the postposition's sense, so rule 17's meaning-bearing complement decides the head. Rule 45's `διὰ τοῦτο` → *For this cause* is the contrast: there a proper Greek preposition governs the phrase, so *for … cause* belongs to `διά` and is headed on *for* while the demonstrative takes *this*. The test is whether a **proper** Greek preposition is present, not whether the English reads alike, which is why the identical *For this cause* is headed on *cause* for `χάριν` and on *for* for `διά`. An **improper** preposition is not that contrast and reads with `χάριν` rather than with `διά`. Rule 95 fixes the class from the lexeme and never from the morph tag, which marks `ἕνεκα` and `ἕνεκεν` `PREP` exactly as it marks `διά`: a preposition governing one case only and forming no verbal compound is improper, and rule 71 heads its frame on the frame's lexical noun, the English preposition standing in front of it belonging to the idiom rather than answering to the governed case, as rule 42 has it. So `Ἕνεκα τούτου` → *For this cause* is one `aligned` unit headed on *cause*, the demonstrative keeping a bare *this*, exactly as `ἕνεκεν τοῦ ὀνόματός μου` → *for my name's sake* is headed on *sake* under rule 78 and `ἕνεκεν τῆς βασιλείας τοῦ θεοῦ` → *for the kingdom of God's sake* is there. Every corpus unit heading a *for … sake* wrap on the prepositional word carries a proper `διά` or `ὑπέρ`, so not one of them is precedent for an adverb governing a case. Do not read the mere presence of a preposition, or the English reading alike, as licensing a head on *for*: ask first whether the preposition is proper.
47. The possessive adjective `ἴδιος` is a source word able to take an English possessive, so where it stands attributively between an article and an expressed head noun it carries the supplied possessive and the article is `unrepresented` under rule 20. `τοῖς ἰδίοις ἀνδράσιν` → *to their own husbands* gives *their own* to `ἰδίοις` and the discontinuous *to … husbands* to `ἀνδράσιν`, as `ἐν τῇ ἰδίᾳ πατρίδι` → *in his own country* and `τὸν ἀδελφὸν τὸν ἴδιον` → *his own brother* do. This is rule 20's displacement for a lexical possessor rather than a case-marked one: rule 20 already says what matters is that the English possessive has a source word to belong to, not the case that word is in, and an adjective meaning *one's own* is such a word. So do not give the article the possessive merely because it is there, and do not read the article's presence as licensing a different analysis from the anarthrous `ἰδίοις δεσπόταις` → *their own masters*, where no article competes and the possessive plainly belongs to the adjective. Rule 22 is the contrast and the configuration decides it: where `ἴδιος` is substantival and governs no head noun the article is what supplies the pronoun, so `ἐκ τῶν ἰδίων` → *of his own* gives *his* to the article and *own* to the adjective. The test is whether a head noun is expressed, which is the same test rule 31 uses to tell a substantival article from an attributive one.
48. Where the ASV compresses a negator with the duration phrase `εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα` into the single negation *never*, that one English word stays whole on the negator and every token of the duration phrase is its own `unrepresented` unit. Where the negator is `οὐ μή`, rule 33 keeps the word on the particle pair: `οὐ μὴ ἀπόλωνται εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα` → *shall never perish*, `οὐ μὴ διψήσει εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα` → *shall never thirst*, and `οὐ μὴ θεωρήσῃ θάνατον εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα` → *shall never see death* leave the preposition, the article and the noun empty, because the single English word answers to the negation and nothing is left over for them. A **plain** negator does the same and is its own `aligned` unit headed on *never*, the particle pair being no part of what licenses this: `οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα` → *hath never forgiveness* gives *never* to `οὐκ` and leaves `εἰς`, `τόν` and `αἰῶνα` empty. What decides it is that one English word carries the negation and the duration together, not which negator the Greek wrote. Rule 30 is the contrast and the ASV's phrasing decides it: where the ASV writes *for ever* the ordinary analysis applies, `εἰς` taking *for* and `αἰῶνα` taking *ever*, as `ζήσεται εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα` → *shall live for ever* and `ὁ υἱὸς μένει εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα` → *the son abideth for ever* do. John 8 does both in one chapter, so this is rule 24's habit and not two analyses. Do not stretch *never* over the phrase, do not split it away from the negator in order to give the noun a word, and do not reach for `noncompositional` over the whole clause. A reference comparison will report all three duration tokens as disagreements, and that report is not a correction.
49. An article governing a **substantival** demonstrative is its own `unrepresented` unit, and the demonstrative takes the whole English expression, including any generic noun the ASV supplies for it. `ὁ τοιοῦτος` → *such a one* gives *such a one* to the demonstrative headed on *such* and nothing to the article, as `τοὺς τοιούτους` → *such* and `τὰς τοιαύτας` → *such* do, and as `τοῖς τοιούτοις` → *unto such* does, the case preposition staying with the demonstrative under rule 21's determinerless clause because no determiner stands on the article to anchor it. This is rule 38's displacement holding where no head noun is expressed: what empties the article there is that another source word already occupies the English determiner slot, and a demonstrative does that whether or not it modifies a noun. Rule 38's closing clause speaks of `ταῦτα` standing with no article at all, and is not a licence to read the opposite where an article is present. What a demonstrative displaces is the **determiner slot** and nothing else, exactly as rule 20 has it, so an article whose determiner the demonstrative has taken is not thereby emptied of an English word of some other kind the ASV supplies for it. Where the ASV recasts the phrase as a finite relative clause and writes the article a separate **antecedent pronoun**, the article takes that pronoun and the demonstrative keeps the relativizer, any supplied copula and its own word: `τοὺς τοιούτους` → *them that are such* gives *them* to the article and *that are such* to the demonstrative, headed on *such*, exactly as rule 92 gives an antecedent pronoun to an article whose noun keeps the relativizer and rule 32 splits the same way before a participle. An antecedent pronoun is not a determiner, and a demonstrative cannot displace what is not one. The test is the ASV's and never the Greek's, the two constructions being identical in the source: where the ASV writes no such pronoun the article realizes nothing and the whole expression is the demonstrative's, which is why 1 Corinthians 16 leaves the article empty at 16:16 and aligns it at 16:18 while writing one analysis and not two. Do not manufacture an antecedent pronoun by reading one off a supplied generic noun — *such a one* is rule 17's completion of the demonstrative and not a pronoun the article may claim — and do not split *that are such* or *such a one* across the two tokens. Rule 22 is the contrast, and the test is whether a demonstrative is present: where the article's complement is an adjective or a participle nothing else can carry the determiner, so a supplied nominal is the article's own — `τὸ τρίτον` → *the third part* gives *the part* to the article and *third* to the adjective, and `οἱ ἡμέτεροι` → *our people* gives *people* to the article and *our* to the possessive adjective. Rule 17 then decides the head inside the demonstrative's unit exactly as it does for `ταῦτα` → *these things*: the generic noun expands the demonstrative, and the demonstrative stays the target head.
50. The modal particle `ἄν` is `aligned`, and leaving it `unrepresented` is the recurring failure. In a counterfactual apodosis the English modal auxiliary is what answers to it, and the lexical verb stands alone: `οὐκ ἂν ἀπέθανεν ὁ ἀδελφός μου` → *my brother had not died* gives *had* to `ἄν` and *died* to the verb, as `σὺ ἂν ᾔτησας αὐτόν` → *thou wouldest have asked him* gives *wouldest* to `ἄν` and *have asked* to the verb, and `οὐκ ἂν εἴχετε ἁμαρτίαν` → *ye would have no sin* does. The Greek is an aorist or imperfect indicative carrying no pluperfect or modal sense of its own, so the English *had* or *would* is not the verb's own auxiliary under rule 9 but the word the particle licenses under rule 7. Where the ASV compresses `ἄν` with the relative, indefinite or subordinating word standing in front of it into a **single English word**, the two tokens are one `aligned` unit under rule 39's test, headed on that word. The `-soever` compressions are the ordinary case — `ὃ ἂν` and `Ὅ τι ἂν` → *whatsoever*, `ὃς ἂν` → *whosoever*, `ὃν ἂν` → *whomsoever*, `ὅπου ἂν` → *whithersoever*, `ὅσα ἂν` → *whatsoever* — because the *-soever* is what answers to the particle, and that the ASV can also spell it out separately is what proves it: `ἃ … ἂν … ποιῇ` → *what things soever he doeth* gives *what things* to the relative and *soever* to `ἄν` alone. But the `-soever` spelling is the ordinary case and not the test. The test is the count of English words the ASV wrote, as rule 94 has it for a prepositional phrase, so `ἕως ἂν` → *till* is one `aligned` unit headed on *till*, as MAT.10.11 and MAT.10.23 read it — rule 39 asks whether some part of the English answers to a source token and *till* answers to `ἕως`, and this rule's opening sentence leaves the particle nowhere else to go. So do not split a single English word across the two tokens, do not reach for `noncompositional` where one of them answers to it, and do not read the absence of a `-soever` as licence to leave the particle empty. Where the ASV instead writes a **separate** word for the particle, or a modal that stays with its verb under rule 9, the ordinary split applies and no compound unit is built. Rule 72's enumeration of the arms in which the corpus aligns `ἄν` is a description of what the corpus contained and not a closed list, and the generalizing `ἐάν` that rule decides is a different lexeme, untouched here. A reference comparison will report an `unrepresented` `ἄν` as a disagreement, and there that report is a correction.
51. A generic Greek article the ASV renders by the **indefinite** determiner is `aligned` to that determiner, headed on it. Rule 19 names only *the* because that is the ordinary case, and rule 23's `unrepresented` is for an article English realizes nowhere — not for one the ASV realizes with *a* or *an*. `ὁ κόκκος τοῦ σίτου` → *a grain of wheat*, `τὸν ἄνθρωπον` → *a man*, `τὸ ψεῦδος` → *a lie*, and `τὸ πρόσωπον` → *a face* all give the indefinite to the article. The test is whether the determiner is the article's own to take. Where the Greek is anarthrous there is no article to compete and the supplied determiner is an ordinary rule 7 helper on the word it determines — `φῶς` → *a light*, `κλέπτης` → *a thief*, `δεῖπνον` → *a supper*, and the substantival `νεκρῶν` → *the dead* — and where another source word already occupies the determiner slot, rules 20, 38 and 49 empty the article exactly as they do before *the*. A **distributive `κατά`** is such a word. The distribution is what the preposition contributes and no other token carries it, so where the ASV renders the distributive by *every* that determiner is the preposition's own and an article governing the noun is its own `unrepresented` unit under rule 23: `κατὰ τὰς συναγωγάς` → *in every synagogue* gives *in every* to `κατά` and nothing to the article, as `κατὰ τοὺς οἴκους` → *every house* does at ACT.8.3. Rule 17 heads such a unit on the word realizing the preposition where the ASV writes one — *in every* on *in* at ACT.14.23, ACT.15.21, ACT.20.23 and TIT.1.5, and *of every* on *of* at LUK.8.4 — and on *every* itself where it writes none, as ACT.8.3, LUK.2.41 and JHN.21.25 do. The corpus is eight units to none and not one of them reads the determiner off the article, so this is not a judgment call; do not split *in every* or *of every* across the preposition and the article, and do not leave the preposition `unrepresented` in order to give *every* to the noun's article. Where no preposition competes the ordinary reading holds and a supplied *every* is the article's own to take, as `τὰς ἡμέρας` → *every day* and `τὰς νύκτας` → *every night* are at LUK.21.37. So a definite-to-indefinite shift in the ASV is not evidence that the article realizes nothing, and it is not a licence to read a determiner off the article where the Greek never had one.
52. A genitive name of relationship takes the whole English expression the ASV builds around it, including the relational noun the ASV supplies, as one `aligned` unit headed on the name. `Ἰούδα Σίμωνος Ἰσκαριώτου` → *of Judas Iscariot, Simon's son* gives *Simon's son* to `Σίμωνος` headed on *Simon's*, and the same construction at John 13:26 and John 6:71 → *the son of Simon Iscariot* gives the whole *the son of* to that word headed on *Simon*. No Greek token answers to *son*: the bare genitive is all that carries the relation, so the supplied noun and its determiner are rule 7 helpers on the name and rule 11 keeps the ASV's italics from making them `added`. Rule 17 then holds the head on the name rather than on the supplied generic noun, exactly as it does for `ταῦτα` → *these things*, and the case preposition *of* rides along as it does for any source noun rendered `of + noun`. Which of the two phrasings the ASV chooses changes nothing, which is rule 24's habit. Rule 20 is not the contrast: there the Greek expresses the head noun, as `ὁ θρόνος τοῦ Σατανᾶ` → *Satan's throne* does, and the genitive name only displaces the article's determiner, whereas here no head noun is expressed and the genitive is the only word the supplied one can belong to. That last clause is this rule's whole scope and is to be read strictly, because an **article** is also a word a supplied noun can belong to: this rule reaches an **anarthrous** genitive alone. Where an article stands in front of the genitive and no head noun is expressed — the `τοῦ` of a genealogical link — rule 102 takes the supplied noun into that article's own phrase instead and this rule does not apply: the article takes the supplied nominal together with its determiner and the case preposition, headed on the supplied nominal, and the genitive name stands bare. `τοῦ Ἰωσὴφ τοῦ Ἠλί` → *the son of Joseph, the son of Heli* gives *the son of* to each article, headed on *son*, and a bare *Joseph* and *Heli* to the names, as LUK.3.23–38 reads its whole genealogy, and as the chain's closing `τοῦ θεοῦ` → *the son of God* reads a member that is a noun rather than a name. Rule 102's own test is what decides it, and it is the test rules 51 and 58 already use — whether a Greek article stands to take the determiner — so the presence of the article and not the shape of the English is what chooses between the two readings: a link the Greek writes **anarthrously** and the ASV still renders *the son of* is this rule's case and takes the expression whole, as `Ἰούδας Ἰακώβου` → *Judas the son of James* does. Do not split the supplied *son* from its determiner or from the case preposition, and do not mark a repeated one `added`: rule 103 folds a supplied nominal standing inside a phrase a Greek article governs into that article's own English however plainly it echoes a noun the same verse or the neighbouring verse expresses.
53. Where a Greek clause is verbless and the ASV supplies a copula, the copula is a rule 7 helper on the **predicate**, not on the subject. Where the predicate is a prepositional phrase the preposition takes it: `ἐγὼ ἐν τῷ πατρί` → *I am in the Father* gives *I* to the pronoun and *am in* to `ἐν`, as `ἐν ἐμοὶ ὁ πατήρ` → *the Father is in me*, `ὧν ἡ δόξα ἐν τῇ αἰσχύνῃ αὐτῶν` → *whose glory is in their shame*, and `ὧν τὰ ὀνόματα ἐν βίβλῳ ζωῆς` → *whose names are in the book of life* do. This is rules 31 and 32's copula clause holding outside a relative clause. Those rules give *are in* to `ἐν` and the copula to `μετά` where an article supplies the relativizer, and nothing in that reasoning depends on the article: a prepositional phrase has no verb to carry a supplied copula, so the preposition carries it whether or not an article stands in front. The same holds where the predicate is an adjective or an adverb, which likewise take the supplied copula themselves — `ὅμοια` → *were like*, `ἐγγύς` → *were nigh*. The recurring failure is reading the copula off the English word order and giving it to the subject pronoun, because the ASV writes *I am* together; word order is not what licenses a helper, and rule 27's warning points the other way here, since the predicate is what licenses the copula. This rule speaks only of a prepositional, adjectival or adverbial predicate. Where the predicate is a noun phrase, rules 7 and 17 decide it in the ordinary way, and there the subject pronoun is what takes the supplied copula: `ὑμεῖς τὰ κλήματα` → *ye are the branches* gives *ye are* to the pronoun, *the* to the article and *branches* to the noun, as `ἐγὼ τὸ Ἄλφα καὶ τὸ Ὦ` → *I am the Alpha and the Omega* does. What decides this is not the word order the rule has just refused, but what is left to carry the copula: a prepositional, adjectival or adverbial predicate is a source token whose own English is what the copula predicates, whereas a noun phrase spells its article and its noun out in words that already answer to those tokens and leaves nothing inside it for the copula, so the copula stays with the subject the ASV supplies its person and number from. A **verbless predication embedded under an expressed finite Greek verb** is such a clause, and the matrix verb's being expressed does not exempt it. Where the ASV renders a Greek object-complement or secondary-predicate construction by an English frame whose copula is written as an infinitival ***to be*** — *found … to be*, *shown to be* — that *to be* is the embedded predication's own supplied copula and is decided here rather than by rule 7 on the matrix frame: the embedded predicate carries it under this rule and rules 63 and 68, and the matrix verb keeps only the English word rendering its own lexeme. `εὑρέθη μοι ἡ ἐντολὴ … αὕτη εἰς θάνατον` → *this I found to be unto death* gives a bare *found* to `εὑρέθη`, a bare *this* to the demonstrative, and *to be unto* to `εἰς` headed on *unto*, exactly as the same verse's `ἡ εἰς ζωήν` → *which was unto life* gives *was unto* to its own `εἰς`; and `ἵνα φανῇ ἁμαρτία` → *that it might be shown to be sin* gives *it might be shown* to `φανῇ` and *to be sin* to the predicate noun under rule 68, that embedded clause expressing no subject of its own. The frame licenses the word but does not carry it, and the recurring failure is reading a *found … to be* or *shown to be* as one matrix idiom because English writes the two parts together. Rule 61 is not the contrast: its infinitival marker stands in front of a verb the Greek expresses, whereas here no source token answers to *be* at all. Rule 96 is untouched, the frame it keeps whole on a negator being an English cleft, which predicates nothing of a token of its own.
54. A first- or second-person possessive adjective — `ἐμός`, `σός`, `ἡμέτερος`, `ὑμέτερος` — names the possessor itself, so it carries the English possessive and the article governing it is `unrepresented` under rule 20. `τῇ ἀγάπῃ τῇ ἐμῇ` → *in my love* gives *my* to `ἐμῇ` and nothing to either article, as `ἡ χαρὰ ἡ ἐμή` → *my joy*, `ἡ ἐντολὴ ἡ ἐμή` → *my commandment* and `ὁ καιρὸς ὁ ὑμέτερος` → *your time* do; where the Greek is anarthrous nothing competes and the analysis is unchanged, `ἐμοὶ μαθηταί` → *my disciples*. It holds equally where the adjective is substantival and the ASV renders it by an absolute possessive, because that single word carries its determiner with it and leaves the article nothing: `τὰ ἐμά` → *mine own* and `τὸν ὑμέτερον` → *yours* give the whole word to the adjective and leave the article `unrepresented`. Rule 47's `ἴδιος` is the contrast, and the difference is lexical rather than configurational: `ἴδιος` means *one's own* and cannot say whose, so where it is substantival the article is what supplies the pronoun — `τὰ ἴδια` → *his own* and `τὸ ἴδιον` → *its own* give *his* and *its* to the article and *own* to the adjective, as rule 47's `ἐκ τῶν ἰδίων` → *of his own* does. John 10 writes both, giving the article *his* before `ἴδια` and nothing before `ἐμά`, which is what shows that the lexeme and not the construction decides. The article recovers a word only where the ASV supplies one that no other source token can take, which is rule 49's `οἱ ἡμέτεροι` → *our people*: there *people* is the article's and *our* is still the adjective's.
55. A crasis token — `κἀγώ`, `κἀμοί`, `κἀκεῖνος` — is one source word that spells a conjunction and a pronoun together, so it takes both English words as one `aligned` unit headed on the pronominal. `κἀγὼ ἔρχομαι πρὸς σέ` → *and I come to thee* gives *and I* to the crasis, as `κἀμοὶ αὐτοὺς ἔδωκας` → *and thou gavest them to me* gives the discontinuous *and … to me* to `κἀμοί`, and `κἀκεῖνοι` → *they also* does. Whatever English word answers to the `καί` is the crasis token's and belongs to no other, whether the ASV writes it *and*, *also*, *both* — `Κἀμὲ οἴδατε` → *Ye both know me* gives *both … me* — or as the correlative of a preceding `καθώς`: `καθὼς ἐμὲ ἀπέστειλας εἰς τὸν κόσμον, κἀγὼ ἀπέστειλα αὐτούς` → *As thou didst send me into the world, even so sent I them into the world* gives the discontinuous *even so … I* to the crasis and only *As* to `καθώς`, because the comparative adverb has its own English word already and the correlative is what the `καί` writes. Rule 15 is what permits the discontinuity, and rule 35 is the mirror image on the English side: there one English word carries two source tokens, here one source token carries two English words. So do not reach for `noncompositional`, which rules 39 and 45 reserve for an English word answering to neither element separately, and do not mark the conjunction's English word `added`. A crasis translated across an edition's verse boundary must arrive in the ASV packet under rule 37; that boundary does not license `unrepresented`.
56. Rule 53 decides a supplied copula by what is left to carry it, so where the Greek clause expresses no predicate at all the subject is all there is and the copula rides with it. An elliptical comparative is the ordinary case: `ἵνα ὦσιν ἓν καθὼς ἡμεῖς` → *that they may be one, even as we are* gives *we are* to `ἡμεῖς`, because the `ἕν` the comparison borrows stands in the main clause and the `καθώς` clause has no token of its own left. Where the same comparison spells its predicate out, that predicate takes the copula exactly as rule 53's adjectival case requires: `καθὼς ἡμεῖς ἕν` → *even as we are one* gives *we* to the pronoun and *are one* to `ἕν`. John 17 writes both, in 17:11 and 17:22, so this is rule 24's habit and not two analyses — and what differs there is the Greek rather than the ASV's phrasing, which is why the difference is licensed. Do not stretch the main clause's predicate across the comparative clause in order to give the copula a predicate there, and do not read the subject-pronoun case as contradicting rule 53's refusal of English word order: the pronoun takes the copula because nothing else in its clause exists to take it, not because the ASV writes *we are* together.
57. An article in attributive position governs its head noun across whatever stands between them, so an interposed genitive does not turn it into something else. `τὴν τοῦ θεοῦ ἡμῶν χάριτα` → *the grace of our God* and `περὶ τοῦ Μωϋσέως σώματος` → *about the body of Moses* give the English *the* to the outer article under rules 19 and 21, and the interposed genitive stands on its own English word, as `ὁ τοῦ κόσμου ἄρχων` → *the prince of the world* and `τῆς τοῦ κόσμου ζωῆς` → *the life of the world* do. The head noun is then bare, and the supplied-determiner clause of rule 51 does not apply to it, because an article is present to take that determiner. Rule 23's article before a name is the contrast and it is a narrow one: it applies where the name **is** the article's head noun, as `τοῦ Ἰησοῦ` → *of Jesus* is. So do not read an article as standing before a name merely because a name follows it, and do not decide the question from a morph string — a form-ambiguous `τοῦ`, `τῷ` or `τῶν` can be tagged for the interposed word rather than for the noun it actually governs, and the construction decides. Rules 20, 38, 47 and 49 are untouched: where a possessive, a demonstrative or an English *'s* already occupies the determiner slot the article is `unrepresented` as they say, which is why `τῆς Καίσαρος οἰκίας` → *Caesar's household* leaves it empty, and where the ASV supplies no determiner at all rule 23 does. An article that follows its noun is rule 31's case, not this one. Where the genitive carries **an article of its own**, that inner article is decided separately and on its own phrase, and the ASV's placement of the possessor decides both. Where the possessor stands **before** the head — the English prenominal possessive with *'s* that rule 20 already names as a displacer — the inner article takes the determiner the ASV writes on the possessor and the genitive takes only the possessive word: `τὸ τοῦ ἑτέρου` → *his neighbor's good* gives *his* to the inner article and *neighbor's* to the genitive, and `τὴν τοῦ ἑτέρου` → *the other's* gives *the* to the inner article and *other's* to the genitive. The outer article is then `unrepresented` under rule 20, its determiner slot being occupied, unless the ASV supplies a nominal for it, which it takes under rule 102 — which is why the same `τὸ τοῦ ἑτέρου` keeps *good* on the outer article and nothing else. Where the possessor stands **after** the head nothing is displaced and each article takes its own determiner in the ordinary way, so `τὸ τῶν πολλῶν` → *the profit of the many* gives *the profit* to the outer article under rule 102 and *of the* to the inner under rule 21. Do not empty the inner article in order to hand the outer one the *the* that the English writes on the possessor: a possessive phrase's determiner determines the possessor, and rule 20 has already emptied the article that possessive displaced. A determinerless English possessor is untouched by this and is rule 21's case, so `τὰ τοῦ θεοῦ` → *the things of God* leaves the inner article `unrepresented` and keeps *of* with the noun.
58. Where a Greek adjective and an expressed Greek noun are both anarthrous and the ASV supplies a determiner for the phrase, the determiner belongs to the **noun**, and any supplied case preposition goes with it. `Ἐπ᾽ ἐσχάτου χρόνου` → *In the last time* gives *last* to the adjective and the discontinuous *the … time* to the noun, as `ὥραν … ἑβδόμην` → *at the seventh hour* gives *seventh* to the ordinal and *at the … hour* to the noun, and `μόνῳ θεῷ` → *to the only God* gives *only* to the adjective and *to the … God* to the noun. Rule 15 is what permits the discontinuity, and rule 51's *the word it determines* is what chooses the noun: a determiner determines the noun phrase, and an attributive adjective is only a modifier inside it. Rule 22 and rule 49 are the contrast, and the test is the one rules 31 and 47 already use — whether a head noun is expressed. Where the adjective is substantival it **is** the nominal word and takes the determiner itself, so `νεκρῶν` → *the dead*, `τυφλῶν` → *of the blind* and `ἕβδομος ἀπὸ Ἀδάμ` → *the seventh from Adam* are its own; and where a Greek article is present, rules 19 and 21 give it the determiner instead, so `τὸν μόνον ἀληθινὸν θεόν` → *the only true God* gives *the* to the article and a bare *only* to the adjective. Do not read a supplied determiner off English word order merely because it stands next to the adjective. A supplied generic **noun** is decided the same way and by the same test, this rule's reasoning reaching it unchanged: a supplied nominal completes the noun phrase, and an attributive adjective is only a modifier inside it. So where the ASV supplies a nominal for such a phrase — commonly because it has recast the expressed Greek noun as an English verb — that nominal belongs to the **expressed noun**, and the adjective keeps only the words that render it. `κριτηρίων ἐλαχίστων` → *to judge the smallest matters* gives the discontinuous *to judge the … matters* to the noun and a bare *smallest* to the adjective, and `βιωτικὰ … κριτήρια` → *to judge things pertaining to this life* gives *to judge things* to the noun and *pertaining to this life* to the adjective, the ASV's own gloss notes there — *Greek of the smallest tribunals*, *Greek tribunals pertaining to* — showing which English word answers to which token. Rule 66 then heads such a unit on whichever of the noun's English words glosses the lexeme and not on the supplied generic one, which is why both of those head on *judge*. Rule 17's generic-noun clause is not the contrast, being scoped in terms to a **substantival** adjective or demonstrative, where no noun stands to take the supplied nominal: the same `βιωτικά` standing alone → *things that pertain to this life* keeps *things* in the adjective's own unit, so a chapter writing both is following rule 24's habit and not two analyses. Rule 102 is this same principle where a Greek article governs the phrase, the article taking the supplied noun there for the same reason. Do not read a supplied nominal off English word order merely because it stands next to the adjective, and do not hand it to the adjective because the ASV renders that adjective by a phrase reading like a noun phrase elsewhere.
59. Rule 41's redundant negative is not peculiar to `οὐ`. `μή` does the same before a negative indefinite, so `μὴ ποιῆσαι ὑμᾶς κακὸν μηδέν` → *that ye do no evil* gives the whole English negation to `μηδέν` and leaves `μή` its own `unrepresented` unit, as `μὴ πτυρόμενοι ἐν μηδενί` → *in nothing affrighted* does, and as a preceding `οὐδέ` empties a following `μή`. What licenses this is that the second word is itself a negative and can carry the English negation alone. The contrast is a negator standing with a **plain** indefinite — `τις` or `τι` — which carries no negation of its own: there the single English negative word answers to both tokens, so they are one `aligned` unit headed on that word under rule 39's test, `οὐ … δυνάμεθά τι` → *we can do nothing* and `ἵνα μή τι ἀπόληται` → *that nothing be lost* each spelling the negation into *no-* and the indefinite into *-thing*. So the test is not whether the English is a single word but whether the second source word is a negative: where it is, rule 41 empties the negator and the indefinite takes the negation whole; where it is not, nothing is left for the indefinite to carry alone and the pair is joined. Rule 41's refusal to join is scoped to its own case — what it will not merge is two negatives, each able to carry the negation by itself — and the distance between them is a symptom of that rather than the test, since rule 15 already permits a discontinuous unit. 2 Corinthians 13 writes both, in 13:7 and 13:8, which is rule 24's habit and not two analyses. Rule 33's `οὐ μή` is untouched, being two negatives with no indefinite in question at all. A reference comparison will report the `unrepresented` negator as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction.
60. A Greek article the ASV renders by a supplied **demonstrative** determiner is `aligned` to that determiner, headed on it, with any case preposition riding along as in rule 21. `ἐν τῷ νῦν αἰῶνι` → *in this present world* gives *this* to the article, as `κατὰ τὴν ἐνέργειαν` → *according to that working*, `κατὰ τὸν νόμον` → *by that law*, `τὸ ὕδωρ τὸ ζῶν` → *that living water*, and `τῆς χάριτος` → *of that grace* do. Rule 51 is the pattern and rule 20 the older one: the contract already says that a supplied possessive and a supplied indefinite are the article's own where no other source word competes for the slot, and a supplied demonstrative is the third case of that same argument. So a definite-to-demonstrative shift in the ASV is no more evidence that the article realizes nothing than a definite-to-indefinite one, and rule 23's `unrepresented` is for an article English realizes nowhere — not for one the ASV realizes with *this* or *that*. Rules 38 and 49 are the contrast, and the test is the one rule 51 already states, whether the determiner is the article's own to take: where a Greek demonstrative is present it occupies the English determiner slot and empties the article, and where the Greek is anarthrous no article competes at all and the supplied demonstrative is an ordinary rule 7 helper on the word it determines, so `Μαριὰμ` → *that Mary* and `Πρεσβύτιδας` → *that aged women* belong to the noun. Do not read a demonstrative off an article the ASV left as *the*, and where the article supplies a nominal as well rule 22 still chooses the head, so `τῆς μελλούσης` → *that hour which* heads on *hour* and not on *that*.
61. The nominalizing article of an articular infinitive is `unrepresented` under rule 23, because English spells the construction with no word answering to the article itself. Where a preposition governs it, that preposition takes the whole English clause-introducer with the complementizer included: `εἰς τὸ … εἶναι` → *to the end that … should be* gives *to the end that* to `εἰς` and *should be* to the infinitive, as `πρὸ τοῦ … ἐλθεῖν` → *before that … came*, `Πρὸ τοῦ σε Φίλιππον φωνῆσαι` → *Before Philip called thee*, and `πρὸς τὸ δύνασθαι` → *that … may be able* do. That is rule 40's principle that a subordinating element is realized by whatever word introduces its English clause, and rule 24's point that `εἰς τὸ` as *to the end that* and as a bare *that* is one analysis and not two. Rule 17 heads such a unit on the word that realizes the preposition where the ASV writes one — *before that* on *before*, *to the end that* on *to* — and on the complementizer where it does not. Where no preposition governs the construction, the infinitival *to* is rule 7's ordinary helper on its verb, as `τὸ ζῆν` → *to live* and `τὸ ἀποθανεῖν` → *to die* are, the article still `unrepresented`. Align the article itself only where the ASV writes a determiner of its own, as `τὸ εἶναι` → *the being on an equality* does under rule 19. Do not read the archaic *that* of *before that* as a word to be split off from *before*: it is one clause-introducer, and the ASV never spells the article separately.
62. Rule 53 decides a supplied verb by what is left in the clause to carry it, and that test does not turn on the verb being a copula. Where a verbless Greek clause has a prepositional predicate and the ASV supplies a **lexical** verb, the preposition carries it exactly as it carries a supplied *is* or *are*: `τὴν παρὰ τοῦ μόνου θεοῦ` → *that cometh from the only God* gives *cometh from* to `παρά`, as `τὰ παρ᾽ ὑμῶν` → *the things that came from you* gives *came from* to `παρ᾽`, and `ἵνα ἡμεῖς εἰς τὰ ἔθνη` → *that we should go unto the Gentiles* gives a bare *we* to the pronoun and *should go unto* to `εἰς`. An expressed subject does not change this, as `εἴ τις εἰς αἰχμαλωσίαν` → *If any man is for captivity* gives *any man* to `τις` and *is for* to `εἰς`; and rule 17 heads such a unit on the word realizing the preposition, not on the supplied verb. Rule 56 is the contrast and it is the narrow one: it gives the supplied verb to the subject only where the clause expresses no predicate at all, so that the subject is the sole token left to carry it. A prepositional phrase standing in the clause is such a predicate, and the recurring failure is reading the supplied verb off the English word order because the ASV writes it next to the subject. This rule reaches only a supplied verb that the standing prepositional phrase is the **predicate of**, and the test is which way the predication runs. Where the ASV instead supplies a **whole predicate of its own** — a lexical verb together with its own subject and complement — and the standing preposition is that supplied verb's **adjunct**, whose own English the ASV has already written in full, the supplied words answer to no token at all and are their own `added` unit under rule 5, the preposition keeping only the word written for it. `εἴτε διακονίαν, ἐν τῇ διακονίᾳ` → *or ministry, let us give ourselves to our ministry* marks *let us give ourselves* `added` and leaves `ἐν` a bare *to*, as `ὁ μεταδιδοὺς ἐν ἁπλότητι` → *he that giveth, let him do it with liberality* and `εἴτε προφητείαν κατὰ τὴν ἀναλογίαν τῆς πίστεως` → *whether prophecy, let us prophesy according to the proportion of our faith* do. An elliptical list is where this ordinarily arises, the Greek writing only the arguments of a predicate it never wrote; and the noun of such an item is ordinarily spent on its own English already, so rule 103's reasoning reaches the same answer from the other side. That the list is the ordinary case is not a scope: the operational test between the two arms is the supplied verb's **English subject**, and it is asked of any elliptical clause whatever its shape. Where that subject is some standing token's own English, a subject stands for the prepositional phrase to be predicated of and this rule's main arm folds the verb onto the preposition; where the ASV supplies the subject as well — a noun answering to no token of the verse, or a resumptive pronoun of a token whose English is already spent — nothing stands for the phrase to predicate of, and the supplied subject and verb are one `added` unit together. Every example above divides on that test: *that*, *the things that*, *we* and *any man* are the English of `τήν`, `τά`, `ἡμεῖς` and `τις`, while the *us* of *let us give ourselves* answers to nothing and the *him* of *let him do it* resumes an article already spent on *he*. So the test is availability and not bare expression, exactly as rule 76 reads rule 20's *present*. A **comparative correlative** whose clauses the Greek writes as bare prepositional strings is this same case and not a list, which is why `δι᾽ ἑνὸς παραπτώματος εἰς πάντας ἀνθρώπους εἰς κατάκριμα` → *through one trespass the judgment came unto all men to condemnation* marks the supplied *the judgment came* `added` whole and leaves each preposition the word written for it, while the neighbouring `τὸ μὲν γὰρ κρίμα ἐξ ἑνὸς εἰς κατάκριμα` → *the judgment came of one unto condemnation* folds its identical supplied *came* onto `ἐξ`, `κρῖμα` standing there to be predicated of. Do not split the supplied verb from the supplied subject, folding the one onto a preposition and adding the other: a standing prepositional phrase can be the predicate of a subject the Greek wrote and not of one the ASV wrote. A preposition whose English the ASV has written in full is not a word a supplied predicate can be a helper on, rule 27 forbidding a helper to be split from what licenses it and nothing here licensing this one. Rule 11 does not decide it either way: the ASV italicizes such a predicate only sometimes, and where it does the italics corroborate rather than license. Rules 63, 68, 75 and 96 are untouched, each of them naming a standing token that the supplied verb predicates of, and so is rule 53, whose supplied copula or auxiliary still folds onto an expressed predicate adjective, as `Ἡ ἀγάπη ἀνυπόκριτος` → *Let love be without hypocrisy* gives *Let ... be* to the adjective.
63. Rule 53's noun-phrase-predicate exception is exactly as wide as it says: it is the subject **pronoun** that takes the supplied copula. Where the subject is a noun, that rule's main principle holds and the copula is the predicate nominal's helper, even against the ASV's word order. `ὅτι κύριος Ἰησοῦς Χριστὸς` → *that Jesus Christ is Lord* gives a bare *Jesus* and *Christ* to the subject and *is Lord* to `κύριος`, as `πνεῦμα ὁ θεός` → *God is a Spirit* gives *is a Spirit* to `πνεῦμα`, `ὁ Χριστὸς κεφαλὴ τῆς ἐκκλησίας` → *Christ also is the head of the church* gives *is the head* to `κεφαλή`, and `ὧν ὁ θεὸς ἡ κοιλία` → *whose god is the belly* gives *the* to the article and *is belly* to `κοιλία`. An interrogative is no different: `ἆρα Χριστὸς ἁμαρτίας διάκονος;` → *is Christ a minister of sin?* gives a bare *Christ* to the subject and the discontinuous *is … a minister* to `διάκονος` under rule 15, because inversion is English word order and rule 53 has already refused to read the copula off it. Whether the predicate is articular decides nothing — `ὑμεῖς τὰ κλήματα` → *ye are the branches* and `ὧν ὁ θεὸς ἡ κοιλία` → *whose god is the belly* differ in their subjects, not in their predicates — and rule 56 is untouched, since it speaks of a clause with no predicate at all.
64. Rule 44 is not peculiar to `μή`. A particle whose only work is to mark the sentence as a question — `μήτι`, `μήποτε`, `ἆρα`, `ἆρά γε` — falls under it, and rule 44's own test decides. Where the ASV marks the question by inversion alone the particle is its own `unrepresented` unit, as `Μήτι ἐγὼ Ἰουδαῖός εἰμι;` → *Am I a Jew?* and `ἆρα Χριστὸς ἁμαρτίας διάκονος;` → *is Christ a minister of sin?* do. Where the ASV supplies an interrogative word the particle is aligned to it, as `μήτι οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ χριστός;` → *Can this be the Christ?* gives *Can* to `μήτι` while the expressed `ἐστιν` keeps *be*, and `μήποτε ἀληθῶς ἔγνωσαν` → *Can it be that … indeed know* gives the whole supplied frame to `μήποτε`. A copula the verbless clause would have required anyway is not such a supplied word: it belongs to the clause under rules 53 and 63. A particle carrying content of its own is untouched by this rule and is aligned in the ordinary way — `οὐχί` → *not*, `πότε` → *when*, `Ἕως πότε` → *how long* — so the test is the particle's work and never its morph tag, which groups both kinds under `PRT-I`. A reference comparison will report every `unrepresented` interrogative particle as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction.

65. Rule 39's `noncompositional` cluster is scoped to a particle fused with the **negative** `μή`, and a concessive `εἰ καί` is not that cluster. The conditional particle takes whatever English word introduces the concessive clause, exactly as rule 40 has it for a subordinating conjunction, and the intensifying `καί` is decided on its own: it is `aligned` where the ASV gives it a word and `unrepresented` where it does not. `εἰ καὶ ἐλύπησα ὑμᾶς` → *though I made you sorry* and `εἰ καὶ ἔγραψα ὑμῖν` → *although I wrote unto you* give *though* and *although* to `εἰ` and nothing to `καί`, as `καὶ ἐὰν … κηρύσσῃ` → *though … preach* gives *though* to `ἐάν` and leaves `καί` empty; where the ASV realizes the particle, `εἰ καὶ πρὸς ὥραν` → *though but for a season* gives *but* to `καί` and `εἰ καὶ σπένδομαι` → *if … I am offered* gives *and* to it, each its own `aligned` unit under rule 2. Which member stands first makes no difference. So do not read the pair as one indivisible concessive, and do not reach for `noncompositional`: rule 39's test asks whether some part of the English answers to a source token, and the concessive force is the conditional's own, `καί` adding only an emphasis English usually leaves unwritten. Rule 45 is not the contrast either, since these are particles and no preposition governs anything.
66. Where **one Greek noun** is rendered by an English adjective-plus-noun phrase, rule 17's head turns on whether the English **noun** is itself a gloss of the lexeme or a supplied generic completion. Where the noun glosses it, the noun is the head: `εὐδοκία` → *good pleasure*, `εὐνοία` → *good will*, `εὐαγγέλιον` → *good tidings*, `ζῷον` → *living creature*, `ἀρχιερεύς` → *chief priest* and *high priest*, `κτίσμα` → *created thing*, `ἀποκαραδοκία` → *earnest expectation*. Where it is a generic completion and another word carries the sense, that word is the head: `πλεονέκτης` → *covetous man* heads on *covetous*, as `ἀγάπαις` → *love feasts* heads on *love*, `πραιτώριον` → *praetorian guard* on *praetorian*, and `σπουδή` → *earnest care* on *earnest*, the corpus glossing that lexeme *diligence* and never *care*. This is rule 17's generic-noun clause holding for a noun rather than a substantival adjective or demonstrative, and the same test it already applies to `all things` and `good thing`. Do not decide it from English syntax: rule 17 forbids choosing a word merely because it is the phrase's formal head, which is the only thing *care* or *man* could rely on. The phrase need not be adjective-plus-noun for this test, and the ASV's word order does not change it. Where the ASV renders one Greek **compound** word — a noun or a verb — by two English words **each of which glosses one of its elements**, the head is the word answering to the compound's **core** and not the one answering to its modifying, incorporated or prefixed element, even where English syntax makes that modifier the phrase's formal head and puts the core in a complement: `πιθανολογίᾳ` → *persuasiveness of speech* heads on *speech* and `ἐθελοθρησκίᾳ` → *will worship* on *worship*, as rule 91's `καταλαλιάς` → *evil speakings* heads on *speakings* and `δυσφημίας` → *evil report* on *report*, and as rule 104's `εὐπειθής` → *easy to be entreated* and `εὐάρεστος` → *well pleasing* head their stem words. A compound **verb** is decided the same way and its core is the **verbal** element, so `καρποφοροῦντες` → *bearing fruit* heads on *bearing* and `εἰρηνοποιήσας` → *having made peace* on *made*, the incorporated `καρπο-` and `εἰρηνο-` being the modifying element however plainly the English noun reads as the phrase's complement. Rule 17's *make straight* and rule 91's *make known*, *make request* and *bear witness* are untouched and are the contrast, decided by rule 91's own test: there no part of the English verb glosses the lexeme, `εὐθύνω`, `γνωρίζω`, `αἰτέω` and `μαρτυρέω` each carrying a single root whose sense the English complement alone spells. A **denominal** verb formed from one root with a causative or verbalizing suffix is that case and not this one, which is why `ἱκανώσαντι` → *made … meet* still heads on *meet*, and rule 104 is untouched for the same reason, `φλογίζω` → *setteth on fire* heading on *fire* because *setteth* answers to no element of it. That is this rule's own line held to and not an exception to it: `εὐδοκία`, `εὐνοία` and `εὐαγγέλιον` all head the core and never the `εὐ-`. So ask first whether **both** English words gloss something, and only then which of them is the core; where one of them glosses no element at all the generic-completion arm decides it instead, which is why *man* and *feasts* lose to *covetous* and *love*. Rule 46 is untouched, because `χάριν` is a postposition rather than a noun and the supplied *cause* or *sake* is what carries its sense, and rules 22, 49 and 58 are untouched, because there the supplied word answers to an article or determines a phrase rather than completing a noun's own English expression.
67. Where the ASV expands a Greek case or preposition into a **complex English prepositional frame** containing a supplied noun — *in the matter of*, *in regard of*, *by reason of*, *as touching the*, *in furtherance of* — the frame is never split across tokens. It rides undivided with whichever token would carry the plain case preposition under rules 21 and 23: with the **Greek preposition** where one is expressed, as `σὺν τῇ χάριτι ταύτῃ` → *in the matter of this grace* and `ἐν τῇ ἁδρότητι ταύτῃ` → *in the matter of this bounty* give the whole frame to `σύν` and `ἐν`, and as `εἰς τοῦτο` → *in regard of this* and `ἐξ ἀκοῆς πίστεως` → *doeth it by the hearing of faith* do; with the **article** where the article takes the English determiner, as `τὸ εὐαγγέλιον` → *as touching the gospel* and `τὴν χάριν` → *in regard of this grace* give *as touching the* and *in regard of this* to the article under rules 21 and 60; and with the **noun** where neither an expressed preposition nor a determiner-bearing article is available, as `ἀνέμου` → *by reason of a wind* and `πεποιθήσει πολλῇ` → *by reason of the great confidence* do. Rule 62 is the same principle for a verbless clause whose prepositional predicate the ASV completes with a supplied **nominal** rather than a supplied verb: `εἰς τὸ ἐκείνων ὑστέρημα` → *being a supply for their want* gives *being a supply for* to `εἰς`, exactly as a supplied *is* or *cometh* rides there. Reach for `added` only where no token licenses the frame at all. A licensing preposition or conjunction displaced by verse numbering must arrive in the ASV packet under rule 37. This rule fixes **where** the frame goes, and it fixes the head wherever the carrier is not a preposition. An **article** or a **noun** carrier heads its own English realization — the determiner the article supplies, or the noun the carrier is — and never the frame's supplied noun: `τὸ εὐαγγέλιον` → *as touching the gospel* heads on *the* and `τὴν χάριν` → *in regard of this grace* on *this*, as `ἀνέμου` → *by reason of a wind* and `πεποιθήσει πολλῇ` → *by reason of the great confidence* head on *wind* and *confidence*. That is rules 21 and 60 holding unchanged: a frame is a longer case preposition, and it does not move a head the plain one would have left alone. So do not carry a noun-headed reading across from a preposition carrier, because the analogy jumps carrier classes: EPH.5.32's head on *regard* is `εἰς`, and the article beside it would still have taken its own *the*. Where the carrier **is** an expressed Greek preposition the finished corpus is genuinely divided and this rule does not settle it: the same frame heads its lexical noun at EPH.5.32, PHP.1.5, PHP.2.22 and EPH.3.1 and heads the prepositional word at JHN.12.11, REV.9.2, 2CO.7.4 and 2CO.7.14. Rule 17 governs that choice as it stands, and a chapter that departs from the reading its neighbours took should say so in the note.
68. Rules 53 and 63 decide a verbless clause's supplied copula by what is left to carry it, and two configurations they do not name are settled in the corpus. Where the clause expresses **no subject at all** and the ASV supplies a subject pronoun along with the copula, rule 63's noun-phrase exception is switched off — no subject token stands for it to apply to — and the predicate carries both: `κοινωνὸς` → *he is partner*, `ἀπόστολοι` → *they are the messengers* and `δόξα` → *they are the glory* at 2CO.8.23, as `εὐπρόσδεκτος` → *it is acceptable*, `κρεῖσσον` → *it is better*, `καλὸν` → *it is good* and `ἀσφαλές` → *it is safe* do, and as `ἰδιώτης` → *I be rude* does for a Greek noun the ASV renders by a bare adjective. Discontinuity is not a reservation about this: `ἔξεστίν` → *it is lawful* and `γνώσει` → *am I … in knowledge* keep the supplied words with the predicate across intervening tokens under rule 15. Where the clause **does** express a subject and that subject is **not a lexical noun**, it falls on rule 53's pronoun side and takes the copula itself. A **substantival demonstrative** is the ordinary case: `αὕτη ἡ ἀνάστασις ἡ πρώτη` → *This is the first resurrection* and `τοῦτο … τρίτον` → *This is now the third time* give *This is* to the demonstrative, as `οἱ τοιοῦτοι ψευδαπόστολοι` → *such men are false apostles* gives *such men are* to `τοιοῦτοι` and leaves *false apostles* to the noun. Rule 63's *noun* means a lexical noun, and a demonstrative is no more one here than `ὑμεῖς` is. A **substantival quantifier or adjective** is no more one either and is decided the same way: `πάντα ὑμῶν` → *all are yours* gives *all are* to `πάντα`, headed on *all* under rule 17, and a bare *yours* to the genitive, exactly as the same frame gives *ye are* to `ὑμεῖς` at 1CO.3.23 while `Χριστὸς … θεοῦ` in that verse's second clause sends *is God's* to the predicate under rule 63, its subject being a lexical noun. A **predicate genitive** the ASV renders by an English possessive — *yours*, *Christ's*, *God's* — is a nominal predicate for rule 53's exception and is none of the prepositional, adjectival or adverbial predicates rule 53's main principle names, so it does not draw the copula to itself unless the subject is a lexical noun. An **attributive** demonstrative is the contrast, and rule 38's test tells them apart: it is part of the subject noun phrase rather than the subject itself, so `Οὗτοι οἱ λόγοι πιστοὶ καὶ ἀληθινοί` → *These words are faithful and true* leaves it a bare *These* and gives *are faithful* to the predicate adjective under rule 53. Rule 56 is untouched, since it speaks of a clause with no predicate at all.
69. Rule 41's test is whether the second word can carry the English negation alone, so where the ASV renders the negative indefinite **non-negatively** — *any*, *any man*, *any thing* — it cannot, and the negator keeps whatever English word carries the clause's negation as an ordinary `aligned` unit. `Ἡμῖν οὐκ ἔξεστιν ἀποκτεῖναι οὐδένα` → *It is not lawful for us to put any man to death* gives *not* to `οὐκ` and *any man* to `οὐδένα` headed on *any*, as `οὐδὲ γὰρ ὁ πατὴρ κρίνει οὐδένα` → *For neither doth the Father judge any man* gives *neither* to `οὐδέ`, and as `οὐ κατενάρκησα οὐθενός` → *I was not a burden on any man* gives *not* to `οὐ` and *on any man* to the indefinite, the case preposition riding along under rules 21 and 36. The negation-bearing word need not be the negator itself: `οὐδενὶ δεδουλεύκαμεν πώποτε` → *have never yet been in bondage to any man* puts it on `πώποτε`. Rule 17's generic-noun clause heads such a unit on *any* and not on the supplied *man*. This is a corollary of rules 41 and 59 rather than an exception to them: those rules empty the negator only where the ASV writes the negation **in** the indefinite — *nothing*, *no man*, *not one* — and a positive rendering is exactly the case where nothing is left there to carry it. Decide it from the English the ASV wrote, never from the indefinite's negative morph tag.

70. The correlative particle `μέν` is decided by the English alone, exactly as rule 65 decides an intensifying `καί`: where the ASV writes a word for it — *indeed* throughout the corpus, as `ὅτι τὴν μὲν παράκλησιν ἐδέξατο` → *he accepted indeed our exhortation* and `Ἰωάννης μὲν σημεῖον ἐποίησεν οὐδέν` → *John indeed did no sign* — it is its own `aligned` unit on that word, and where the ASV leaves the correlation unwritten it is its own `unrepresented` unit. Marking the correlation elsewhere is not writing it: the answering `δέ` keeps its own *but* or *and*, and the correlative pronoun or article keeps the *some*, *the one* or *the other* that carries it, so `μέν` is joined to neither, nor to a preceding `οὖν`, which keeps its own *therefore*. That last clause presumes the ASV wrote a word for each. Where it writes only **one** English word for an adjacent `μὲν οὖν`, that word belongs to whichever of the two particles the corpus already spends it on, and the other is its own `unrepresented` unit: `Τὴν μὲν οὖν βίωσίν μου` → *My manner of life then* gives *then* to `οὖν` and leaves `μέν` empty, while `Ἐγὼ μὲν οὖν ἔδοξα` → *I verily thought* gives *verily* to `μέν` and leaves `οὖν` empty. Rule 91's operational test is what decides which, a query on the lemma answering it, and the order in which the two stand decides nothing; a chapter writing both is following rule 24's habit and not two analyses. Do not split that one word across the pair, do not join them into one unit, and do not reach for `noncompositional`, which rules 39 and 45 reserve for a cluster fused with the negative `μή` and for a prepositional phrase. Rules 44 and 64 apply this same English-side test to an interrogative particle, and as there the test is the particle's work and never its morph tag: `μενοῦνγε` is a separate lexeme carrying content of its own and is aligned in the ordinary way, as *verily*. A reference comparison will report every `unrepresented` `μέν` as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction. The connective `τε` is decided by this same test, and by the English rather than by the Greek pairing. Where the ASV writes a word for it, `τε` takes that word as its own `aligned` unit — *both* in fourteen corpus units, and *and* or *whether* where the ASV writes those. Where `τε … καί` is spelled out with **two** English conjunctions each particle keeps its own: `ἄνδρας τε καὶ γυναῖκας` → *whether men or women* gives *whether* to `τε` and *or* to `καί`, as `ἐλάλει τε καὶ συνεζήτει` → *he spake and disputed* and `ἀνέβλεψέν τε καὶ ἀναστὰς ἐβαπτίσθη` → *he received his sight, and he arose* give an *and* to each. Where the ASV writes only **one** English conjunction for the pair, that word is the `καί`'s and `τε` is its own `unrepresented` unit: `ἐθνῶν τε καὶ βασιλέων` → *the Gentiles and kings* and `ἡμέρας τε καὶ νυκτός` → *day and night* leave the first particle empty, as twelve corpus units do, ACT.2.9 and ACT.2.10 among them. A `τε` with no answering `καί` keeps whatever connective the ASV writes for it, as `υἱῶν τε Ἰσραήλ` → *and the children of Israel* does. Do not split one English conjunction across the two particles, do not join them into one unit, and do not reach for `noncompositional`, which rules 39 and 45 reserve for a particle cluster fused with the negative `μή` and for a prepositional phrase. LUK.12.45 gives such an *and* to `τε` rather than to the `καί` and is the one corpus unit reading the pair that way, so follow the majority and say in the note that the other reading stands. A reference comparison will report every `unrepresented` `τε` as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction.

71. Rule 67 leaves the head open where a frame's carrier is an expressed Greek preposition, but that division is confined to **proper** prepositions. An **improper** preposition — an adverb governing a case, which rule 42 already treats apart — is settled in the corpus, and heads on the frame's lexical noun. `ἐνώπιον` → *in the sight of*, *in the sight* and *in sight* head on *sight* at 2CO.7.12, 2CO.8.21, REV.13.12, REV.13.13, REV.13.14, REV.16.19 and REV.19.20, and → *in the presence of* and *in the presence* head on *presence* at JHN.20.30 and REV.14.10, as `πέραν` → *on the other side* and *to the other side* head on *side* at JHN.6.1, JHN.6.22 and JHN.6.25, `ἐγγύς` → *at hand* and *is at hand* head on *hand* at JHN.2.13, JHN.6.4, JHN.7.2, JHN.11.55, PHP.4.5, REV.1.3 and REV.22.10, and `χωρίς` → *by itself* heads on *itself* at JHN.20.7. Not one such frame heads on the initial *in*, *on* or *at*. This is rule 42's own reasoning carried through to the head: the adverb keeps the English phrase that renders the adverb itself, and the noun is what renders it, the preposition in front of it belonging to the English idiom rather than answering to the governed case — which is why rule 42 gives *of the* and *unto the* to the article and leaves the adverb its own phrase whole. Where the ASV renders the adverb by a single word the question does not arise and that word is the head, as `πέραν` → *beyond* and *over*, `ἐγγύς` → *nigh* and *near*, and `χωρίς` → *without*, *apart* and *separate* are; and where the adverb has an English gloss of its own the frame is not a supplied one at all, so `ἐγγύς` → *nigh at hand* heads on *nigh* and `ἐγγύς` → *nigh unto* on *nigh*. Two of these units are `medium` and only one of them concedes anything against this rule — JHN.6.25 weighs *other* against *side*, inside the frame either way, while JHN.20.7 does concede *by* — so the precedent is one hedged unit against sixteen unmarked ones. Rule 67's divided case is untouched and remains exactly what it says: a **proper** preposition, where EPH and PHP head the frame's noun and the 2 Corinthians run heads the prepositional word, so a chapter should follow the reading its neighbours took and say in the note that the other reading stands in the corpus.

72. Rule 50's closing clause — that a reference comparison reporting an `unrepresented` particle is a correction — is scoped to `ἄν`, and a generalizing `ἐάν` is decided on the English alone. The corpus never leaves `ἄν` empty: it is `aligned` to the modal it licenses, to a bare *soever*, or compressed with a preceding relative into a single *-soever* word. `ἐάν` standing for `ἄν` after a relative or an indefinite has two arms, and rule 39's test chooses between them. Where the ASV compresses the pair into **one English word** some part of which answers to the particle, the two tokens are one `aligned` unit as rule 50 already has it, headed on that word and on the relative: `ὅστις ἐὰν` → *whosoever*, `ὃ ἐὰν` → *whatsoever*, and `ὃς ἐὰν` → *whoso*, whose *-so* answers to the particle exactly as a *-soever* does. The *-soever* spelling is the ordinary case and not the test, which is what rule 50 already says for `ἄν` in as many words: the test is the count of English words the ASV wrote, as rule 94 has it, and then rule 39's question whether some part of that word answers to a source token. So do not read the absence of a *-soever* as licence to empty the particle where the ASV wrote only one word for the pair, and analyse `ὃς ἐάν` and `ὃς ἄν` alike wherever the ASV renders them alike, which is rule 24's habit and not two analyses. Where the ASV instead writes a **separate** English word for the relative and none for the particle, nothing is left for it and it is its own `unrepresented` unit: `ἃ ἐὰν θέλητε` → *the things that ye would* leaves it empty, as `ὅσους ἐὰν` → *as many as*, `ὁσάκις ἐὰν` → *as often as*, `ὅσοι ἐὰν`, and `οὗ ἐὰν αἰτήσωνται` → *that they shall ask* do, the generalizing force being absorbed by the English phrase or by a modal that stays with its verb under rule 9. Do not manufacture a word for it by stretching the particle onto the relative or the verb, and do not fold the pair into one unit where the relative has an English word of its own and the particle has none: rule 2 keeps the relative's own English on the relative. An ordinary conditional `ἐάν` is untouched by this rule and is aligned to *if* like any other. A reference comparison will report every `unrepresented` `ἐάν` as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction — which is the contrast with rule 50, where the same report about `ἄν` is one.

73. Where a Greek **light-verb or support-verb pair expresses both its elements** — a verb of general sense such as `ἔχω` or `παρέχω` together with a noun complement carrying the lexical content — and the ASV collapses the pair into a **single English verb**, the two source tokens are one unit and the target head is that English verb. The finite verb's inflection-licensed helpers ride inside the unit under rules 7 and 9: `χρείαν εἶχεν` → *he needed* keeps the supplied subject, `ἔχει χρείαν` → *needeth* and `χρείαν ἔχεις` → *needest* keep nothing else, and `κόπους … μηδεὶς παρεχέτω` → *let no man trouble me* keeps the imperative auxiliary *let* inside the pair's own unit, discontinuously under rule 15. Do not split that auxiliary off as a bare *let* headed on itself: rule 17 forbids choosing a word merely because it is finite, and no English word realizes `παρέχω` or `ἔχω` separately. The status stays `aligned` wherever some part of the English answers to a source token under rule 39's test — *need* answers to `χρεία` — and `noncompositional` is for the pair whose English answers to neither lexeme, as `ἔχουσα βασιλείαν` → *which reigneth* is recorded. Rule 10 leaves the source head open and the corpus takes it both ways, the noun at JHN.13.10 and JHN.16.30 and the verb at JHN.2.25, so put it on whichever token the target head realizes. Rule 2 is the contrast and it decides the ordinary case: where the ASV spells **both** elements out the pair splits like any other, so `καιρὸν ἔχομεν` → *opportunity* and *we have* are two units. The test is whether the English collapsed the pair, not whether the Greek is idiomatic.

74. A **supplied subject** of a Greek infinitive — one Greek leaves unexpressed, which English must write — is a rule 7 helper on the **infinitive**, not on the finite verb governing it. `δοκεῖ … εἶναί τι` → *thinketh himself to be something* gives a bare *thinketh* to `δοκεῖ` and *himself to be* to the infinitive, as `ἵνα μὴ δόξω … ἐκφοβεῖν ὑμᾶς` → *that I may not seem … I would terrify you* gives *I terrify* to `ἐκφοβεῖν`, and `δοκεῖτε … ἔχειν` → *ye think that … ye have* gives *ye have* to `ἔχειν`. The infinitive is the verb whose subject it is, and rule 27 forbids splitting a helper from what licenses it. A supplied **object** is the contrast and it goes the other way, staying with the verb that requires it in English: `ἔχῃ … μεταδιδόναι` → *he may have whereof to give* and `ἔχωσιν … κατηγορεῖν` → *they might have whereof to accuse* keep *whereof* on `ἔχω`, both recorded `medium` conceding the infinitive. So the test is which verb the supplied word is an argument of. The **complementizer** is decided separately and belongs to the verb licensing the clause under rule 40, which is why JHN.5.39 gives *that* to `δοκεῖτε` while giving *ye* to `ἔχειν`; do not read the one placement off the other. Rule 11 does not bear on this: the ASV italicizes such a subject only sometimes, and its markup is evidence rather than a verdict. A **participle** is decided the same way and by the same test, the infinitive being named above only because it is the ordinary case and not because the rule is scoped to it. Where the participial clause leaves its subject unexpressed and the ASV writes one, that pronoun is a rule 7 helper on the **participle**, riding inside its unit along with any supplied subordinator under rule 86 and any supplied copula or auxiliary: `ἐλθόντων` → *when they were come*, `ἐλθόντα` → *when he came* and `εἰπόντος` → *when he said* each keep the supplied pronoun with the participle, as rule 86's own `ὄντες` → *when we were*, `ποιοῦντες` → *if ye do* and `ἐνεχθείσης` → *when there was borne* already read it. Where the clause **expresses** its subject — the genitive of a genitive absolute, or any noun or pronoun the Greek writes — that token takes the English subject word as its own `aligned` unit under rule 2, and the participle keeps only the subordinator, the copula and its own verb: `καταβαινόντων αὐτῶν` → *as they were coming down* gives *they* to `αὐτῶν`, as `Ἐλθόντων δὲ αὐτῶν` → *when they were come*, `Ἀναστρεφομένων δὲ αὐτῶν` → *while they abode* and `ἔτι αὐτοῦ λαλοῦντος` → *While he was yet speaking* do, that last leaving `ἔτι` its own *yet* besides. The English is often identical on both sides of that line — MAT.17.14 and MAT.17.24 both write *when they were come* — so decide it from whether the Greek expressed the subject and never from the English. That is rule 32's reasoning holding here: a word another source token has already written is spent, and nothing is left to supply.

75. Rules 53, 62, 63 and 68 decide a supplied verb by what is left in the clause to carry it, and a verbless clause built on a **relational noun** is that chain's case where the ASV supplies a **lexical** verb and inverts the clause. Where the Greek names a relation with a noun — `μετοχή`, `κοινωνία`, `συμφώνησις`, `μερίς`, `συγκατάθεσις` — and governs its terms by case, the ASV makes a governed term the English subject and writes the relation as the object of a supplied *have* or *hath*: `τίς μετοχὴ δικαιοσύνῃ καὶ ἀνομίᾳ` → *what fellowship have righteousness and iniquity*, `τίς κοινωνία φωτὶ πρὸς σκότος` → *what communion hath light with darkness*, `τίς συμφώνησις Χριστοῦ πρὸς Βελιάρ` → *what concord hath Christ with Belial*, and `τίς συγκατάθεσις ναῷ θεοῦ μετὰ εἰδώλων` → *what agreement hath a temple of God with idols*. That supplied verb rides with the relational noun and is headed on it, and the governed term stands bare, keeping only its own determiner — *light*, *Christ*, *a believer*, *a temple*. Rule 63 is what forces this, having already refused to read a supplied verb off English word order in an inverted interrogative, and rule 17 confirms the head: the supplied verb carries no sense of its own while the relational noun carries all of it. The governed term's case is not what licenses the verb, and 2 Corinthians 6 is what proves it, since the same supplied *hath* stands over a genitive at 6:15 and over a dative at 6:14, 6:15 and 6:16. Rule 68 is the contrast and it is the narrow one: where the clause names no relation for the supplied verb to verbalize, the verb goes to whatever token is left, so `Τί ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί` → *what have I to do with thee* gives *have I to do* to the dative pronoun. Rule 73 is untouched, since there the Greek expresses a verb of its own and the ASV collapses the pair rather than supplying anything.

76. Rule 20's test asks whether some other word is **present** to take a supplied possessive, and the corpus reads *present* as **available**: a source word the ASV has already spent on an English word of some other syntactic role is not a word the possessive can belong to, so the article takes it under rule 20's second arm. `ἐπέβαλεν ἐπ᾽ αὐτὸν τὴν χεῖρα` → *laid his hand on him* gives *him* to `αὐτόν` as the preposition's object and *his* to the article, while the same verse's `ἡ ὥρα αὐτοῦ` → *his hour* leaves the article `unrepresented` because there the genitive pronoun carries nothing else — one verse writing both, which is what shows that availability and not mere presence is the test. `οὐκ εἰς τὰ ἄμετρα καυχησόμεθα` → *beyond our measure* gives *our* to the article, the nominative `ἡμεῖς` being already realized by *we*; and `ἵνα ἀφῇ ἡμῖν τὰς ἁμαρτίας` → *to forgive us our sins* gives a bare *us* to the dative and *our* to the article, against the same verse's `τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν` → *our sins*, where the genitive is free and rule 20 proper empties the article. So the pronoun's case does not decide this, exactly as rule 20 already says; what decides it is whether the ASV has spent that pronoun elsewhere. A pronoun whose own English is merely discontinuous or periphrastic has not been spent: `τοῖς δεσμοῖς μου` → *for me in my bonds* gives both *for me* and *my* to `μου` and leaves the article `unrepresented`, because one word's English split across a phrase is still that word's. Do not read a nominative subject already written as *we* or *ye*, or a dative object already written as *us*, as displacing a determiner it never reaches.

77. Rule 22's head-on-the-nominal-word is scoped to a substantival **article**, and a relative **pronoun** expanded by a supplied word is decided by which of the two words carries content. Where the supplied word is a bare antecedent or determiner, the relativizer is the head, because the pronoun's own lexeme is what *which* or *whom* spells: `ἣν εἴχομεν ἀπ᾽ ἀρχῆς` → *that which we had from the beginning* heads on *which*, as *he whom*, *him whom*, *him of whom* and *of that which* do, fifteen units against three. Where the supplied word is a **generic noun**, it is the nominal the relative substantivizes and it takes the head: `ἃ εἰργασάμεθα` → *the things which we have wrought* heads on *things*, as JHN.8.26, JHN.8.38, JHN.11.46, JHN.12.50, JHN.15.14 and TIT.2.1 read *the things which* and GAL.1.20 reads *touching the things which* — fifteen units against four, of which REV.2.10 and REV.4.1 give no head reasoning at all, JHN.18.21 asserts rule 17 without argument, and TIT.1.11 is `medium` conceding the nominal. The substantival article is the contrast and it is unanimous the other way: `τὸ` → *that which* heads on *that* in all nine corpus units and on the relativizer in none, and every article expanded with *things* heads on *things*, because an article's own lexeme is a determiner and the relativizer is supplied for it. So the test is whether the supplied word has content of its own — *things* names what is referred to, *that* only points at it — which is rule 17's principle rather than an exception to it. Rule 17's generic-noun clause is not the contrast, being scoped to a substantival adjective or demonstrative whose own English word already carries the sense, as `ταῦτα` → *these things* and `Πολλά` → *many things* do; there the supplied noun is the empty completion, and here the relativizer is. That such a supplied word rides **inside the relative pronoun's own unit** is presupposed by every reading above and is not left open: an antecedent the Greek does not express is a rule 7 helper on the relative that licenses it, so do not mark it `added` under rule 5, and do not hand it to a neighbouring token whose own English the ASV has already written. `Οὗτος ἦν ὃν εἶπον` → *This was he of whom I said* gives *This* to the demonstrative, a bare *was* to the copula and *he of whom* to the relative headed on *whom*, as `ὑπὲρ οὗ ἐγὼ εἶπον` → *of whom I said* gives the discontinuous *he … whom* to the relative and *of* to the expressed preposition under rules 15 and 36, and as `Ὃν ἔγραψεν Μωϋσῆς` → *him of whom Moses … wrote* does. An **indefinite** antecedent is no different and is not evidence of anything else: `ὃν ὑμεῖς οὐκ οἴδατε` → *one whom ye know not* gives *one whom* to the relative. Rule 103 is the contrast and is narrow: its `added` reaches a **repeated** antecedent, a lexical noun the verse has already spent, as 2CO.3.7 has it in terms, and it does not reach a bare pronoun or indefinite the ASV supplies where the Greek expressed none, the relative standing there unspent to license it. Rule 11 does not decide this either way, the ASV italicizing such an antecedent only sometimes.

78. Rule 67 leaves the head open where a complex frame's carrier is an expressed proper preposition, and that opening is confined to a **contiguous** multiword preposition — *in regard of*, *in furtherance of*, *by reason of*, *in behalf of*. Where the ASV instead **wraps** its phrase in the discontinuous frame *for … sake* and the carrier is a **proper** preposition, the corpus is settled and heads on the prepositional word: `διὰ τὴν ἀλήθειαν` → *for the truth's sake* heads on *for*, as JHN.12.9, JHN.12.30, JHN.14.11, JHN.15.21, REV.2.3 and 2CO.12.10 head *for … sake* and JHN.11.15, JHN.12.30, JHN.17.19 and 2CO.8.9 head *for … sakes* — eleven units against none, only PHP.1.24 hedging. Rule 46 is what decides it and already states the test: the identical English heads on *sake* or *cause* for the postposition `χάριν`, which is the only word carrying the sense there, and on the prepositional word wherever a Greek preposition is expressed. So do not read rule 67's divided case as licensing a head on the frame's supplied noun merely because the frame is complex; what that case divides is where a **contiguous** frame's sense sits, and a discontinuous *for … sake* wrap is not that question. An **improper** preposition is untouched by this and heads on the frame's lexical noun as rule 71 requires. Every unit counted above carries a proper preposition, `διά` or `ὑπέρ`, so none of them is precedent for an adverb governing a case — exactly as rule 95 says of the *by reason of* units that head on *by*, every one of which carries a proper `διά` or `ἐκ`. `ἕνεκεν τῆς βασιλείας τοῦ θεοῦ` → *for the kingdom of God's sake* heads on *sake*, the noun being what renders the adverb's own sense while the *for* belongs to the English idiom rather than answering to the governed case, which is rule 42's reasoning and rule 95's own settlement of `εἵνεκεν` → *by reason of* on *reason*. Decide the class from the lexeme under rule 95 — a preposition governing one case only and forming no verbal compound is improper — and never from the morph tag, which marks `ἕνεκεν` `PREP` exactly as it marks `διά`.

79. A **pleonastic negative** in the complement clause of a verb of denial is its own `unrepresented` unit. `ὁ ἀρνούμενος ὅτι Ἰησοῦς οὐκ ἔστιν ὁ χριστός` → *he that denieth that Jesus is the Christ* leaves `οὐκ` empty, because Greek doubles the denial into the complement and English does not, so no part of any English word answers to the particle and rule 23 decides it on its own terms. Rules 41 and 59 are the shape but not the licence, and the difference is the whole of this rule: what licenses those is a second **negative word** able to carry the English negation alone, and here there is none — what absorbs the particle is the lexeme of `ἀρνέομαι` itself. So do not cite them, and do not manufacture a word for the particle by stretching it onto *denieth*, which answers wholly to the participle; the corpus renders `ἀρνέομαι` *deny*, *denied*, *denieth* and *denying* throughout and never with a negative component of its own. The same holds for the redundant `μή` Greek writes in the **infinitival complement of a verb of withholding, hindering or refusing**, which English likewise does not double: `οὐδὲν ὑπεστειλάμην … τοῦ μὴ ἀναγγεῖλαι ὑμῖν` → *I shrank not from declaring unto you anything* and `οὐ … ὑπεστειλάμην τοῦ μὴ ἀναγγεῖλαι ὑμῖν` → *I shrank not from declaring unto you* both leave `μή` empty, the verb's own lexeme absorbing it exactly as `ἀρνέομαι` absorbs the other, and the nominalizing article of the infinitive standing `unrepresented` beside it under rule 61. The clause's one English negation then belongs to whichever token carries it in the ordinary way — `οὐ` → *not*, or the discontinuous *not … anything* to `οὐδέν` under rules 15 and 69 where no plain negator stands. Do not move that *not* onto the `μή` merely because the particle stands next to the infinitive it negates. A negative the ASV does realize is untouched by this rule and is aligned in the ordinary way, as `καὶ οὐκ ἠρνήσατο` → *and he denied not* is. A reference comparison will report the `unrepresented` particle as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction.

80. Where a universal `πᾶς` governs a **substantival article** and the ASV compresses the pair into the single word *whosoever*, the quantifier takes that word and the article is its own `unrepresented` unit. Rule 32 is what decides it: the article supplies the pronoun **and nothing else**, so where `πᾶς` has already taken the one English word the ASV wrote, nothing is left for the article and rule 23 makes it its own unit. `πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων` → *whosoever believeth*, `πᾶς ὁ ἀποκτείνας ὑμᾶς` → *whosoever killeth you*, `πᾶς ὁ προάγων` → *whosoever goeth onward*, `πᾶς ὁ μισῶν` → *whosoever hateth* and `πᾶς ὁ ἁμαρτάνων` → *whosoever sinneth* all give the word to the quantifier and leave the article empty, the participle keeping its own verb under rule 32. Rule 39's test is the competing reading and it does not carry here: the English words that rule licenses — `οὐ δύναται` → *cannot*, `οὐδὲ ἕν` → *nothing*, `εἴ τις` → *whoso* — visibly spell **both** source elements, whereas *whosoever* is one word for a quantifier that by itself means *every one*, and rule 50 gives the `-soever` to `ἄν` where a particle stands there to answer to it. So do not read a *who-* off the article merely because the article is substantival. The corpus reads it this way at JHN.3.15, JHN.3.16, JHN.16.2 and 2JN.1.9, two of them unhedged, and joins the pair only at JHN.11.26 and JHN.12.46, both `medium` and the latter conceding this reading — one hedged pair against four, which is rule 71's shape and not rule 67's genuinely divided case. Where the ASV writes a **second** English word for the article the question does not arise and the ordinary split applies: `πᾶς ὁ ἔχων` → *every one that hath* and `πᾶς ὁ ποιῶν` → *every one that doeth* give *every* to the quantifier, *one* to the article and *that hath* or *that doeth* to the participle under rules 22 and 32. A reference comparison will report the `unrepresented` article as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction.

81. A preposition fused with a governed demonstrative into a single English *here-* or *there-* word — `ἐν τούτῳ` → *Hereby* and *Herein*, `ἐν αὐτῷ` → *therein*, `δι᾽ αὐτῆς` → *thereby* — is one `aligned` unit headed on the demonstrative, and reaching for `noncompositional` is the recurring failure. Rule 35's test decides it and the English spells both elements out: *here-* and *there-* answer to the demonstrative, and *-in*, *-by*, *-of* and *-with* answer to the preposition. That `ἐν` is instrumental as well as locative is what licenses *-by*, so `ἐν τούτῳ` → *Hereby* and → *Herein* are one analysis and not two — which is rule 24's habit, since the ASV writes both and 1 John 4 writes both in one chapter. The corpus reads it this way in all fourteen units of *hereby* and *herein*, at 1JN.2.3, 1JN.2.5, 1JN.3.16, 1JN.3.19, 1JN.3.24, 1JN.4.2, 1JN.4.9, 1JN.4.10, 1JN.4.13, 1JN.4.17, 2CO.8.10, JHN.4.37, JHN.9.30 and JHN.15.8, with none hedged and none headed on the preposition. Rule 45 is the contrast and it is a narrow one: it wants an English word answering to **neither** token, which is why `διὰ τοῦτο` → *therefore* and `μετὰ ταῦτα` → *hereafter* stay `noncompositional` — those compounds have lexicalized to a sense neither element spells — and why the same `ἐν ᾧ` is `aligned` as *wherewith* and `noncompositional` as *while*. So do not read the ASV's spelling as the test: that *-by* is a looser gloss of `ἐν` than *-in* is decides nothing, because rule 45 asks whether some part of the English answers to a source token and not whether it answers most literally. Where the ASV instead spreads the phrase over separate words the question does not arise and the ordinary analysis applies, `ἐκ τούτου` → *By this* giving *By* to the preposition and *this* to the demonstrative.

82. Rule 55's correlative clause is not peculiar to a crasis. Where a **separate** `καί` answers a preceding comparative `καθώς`, `καθάπερ` or `ὡς`, that `καί` takes the whole English correlative — *so* or *even so* — and the comparative adverb keeps only *as* or *even as*. `καθὼς … καὶ ὑμεῖς` → *as … so ye* gives *so* to the conjunction at JHN.6.57 and JHN.13.33, as `ὡς … καὶ` → *As … so* does at GAL.1.9 and 2CO.13.2, and as `καθὼς ἐκεῖνός ἐστιν καὶ ἡμεῖς ἐσμεν` → *as he is, even so are we* does at 1JN.4.17. Rule 55 already gives the reason and it does not depend on the crasis: the comparative adverb has its own English word already, and the correlative is what the `καί` writes. The corpus confirms it from the other side, `καθώς` being rendered *even as*, *as* or *according as* in all sixty-nine of its units and never taking *so*. Rule 17 heads such a unit on ***so***, the word that realizes the correlative; a supplied *even* is an intensifying helper on it under rules 7 and 11, exactly as it is where `οὕτως` takes *even so* at JHN.3.14, JHN.5.21, JHN.5.26 and JHN.14.31 and where `ναί` does at REV.1.7. So do not head such a unit on *even* merely because *even* is the more direct gloss of `καί`: that gloss is what a bare *even* answers to where no *so* competes, as at 2CO.11.12 and EPH.2.3, and it does not carry where the ASV writes the correlative out. Where the ASV writes **no** correlative and renders the comparative together with its answering `καί` by a bare *even as*, that is exactly where the gloss does carry: the `καί` is `aligned` to *even* and headed on it, and the comparative keeps a bare *as* as its own unit, as `καθὼς καὶ ἡμεῖς` → *even as we* does at 2CO.11.12, `ὡς καὶ οἱ λοιποί` → *even as the rest* at EPH.2.3, and `καθὼς καὶ ἐγένετο` → *even as it came to pass* at 1TH.3.4. Do not give *even as* whole to the comparative and leave the `καί` `unrepresented`: rule 65's English-side test empties an intensifying `καί` only where the ASV writes no word for it, and a written *even* is such a word. Where the ASV writes a word of the particle's **own** — ***also*** — the bare-*even* arm does not apply. That word is the καί's under rule 65, and the comparative keeps as its own unit, headed on *as*, whatever the ASV wrote for it and no more: *even as* whole where the ASV writes the *even*, as `καθάπερ καὶ ἡμεῖς` → *even as we also* does at 1TH.3.6 and 1TH.3.12, and a bare *as* where it writes none, as `καθὼς καὶ προείπαμεν ὑμῖν` → *as also we forewarned you* does at 1TH.4.6. A written *also* is evidence that the particle is realized, and never evidence that an *even* the ASV did not write is owed to the comparative. Rule 76's availability test is what forces this — a particle the ASV has already spent on *also* is not a word the supplied *even* can belong to — and rule 100 settles the identical division for `οὕτως … καί` → *even so … also* unanimously across thirteen units, the adverb keeping the whole correlative and the particle taking only *also*. So do not stretch the `καί` discontinuously over *even … also*, and do not read a written *also* as licence to strip *even* from the comparative. A comparative with **no** answering `καί` is the contrast and keeps *even as* whole under rules 7 and 11, no other source token standing to claim the supplied *even*, so a chapter writing both — `καθώς` alone as *even as* and `καθὼς καί` as *even* plus *as* — is following rule 24's habit and not two analyses. The test is not merely whether a `καί` stands in the construction but which English word the ASV wrote for it: a written *so* is the correlative arm and is the `καί`'s, a written *also* is the particle's and leaves the comparative whatever the ASV wrote beside it, *even as* or a bare *as*, a bare *even as* with no other word for the particle splits into the `καί`'s *even* and the comparative's *as*, and no answering `καί` at all leaves *even as* whole on the comparative. Never decide it from how the same comparative was rendered where a different word, or none, stood.

83. The reciprocal `ἀλλήλων` rendered by the two-word English idiom is headed on ***another*** — the word realizing the `ἄλλος` lexeme — and never on *one*, which is the supplied completion whose only claim is standing first. `ἀλλήλους` → *one another* heads on *another*, as `ἀλλήλοις` → *one to another* and `ἀλλήλων` → *one of another* and *one another's* head on *another* and *another's*, and as `ἀλλήλοις` → *the one to the other* heads on *other*. This is rule 17's generic-completion clause, and rule 17 forbids the competing reading in as many words, refusing a word chosen merely because it is first; the corpus glosses `ἄλλος` *another*, *other* and *others* and never *one*. The precedent is thirty units on the `ἄλλος`-word against four on *one* — 1JN.3.11, 1JN.3.23, EPH.4.2 and TIT.3.3 — and not one of those four gives any head reasoning, two carrying no note at all and the other two asserting only that the English is a fixed two-word idiom, which is true of both readings and selects neither. That is rule 77's shape rather than rule 67's genuinely divided case, so follow the majority reading and do not treat the four as licensing a choice.

84. A **privative** Greek adjective or adverb — an alpha-privative such as `ἄμωμος`, `ἄσπιλος`, `ἄθεος`, `ἄκαρπος`, `ἄλογος`, `ἄνυδρος`, `ἄραφος`, or an adverb such as `ἀφόβως` and `ἀπροσωπολήμπτως` — that the ASV renders by *without* plus a noun is one `aligned` unit headed on that **noun**, and the head is not in doubt. `ἀμώμου` → *without blemish* heads on *blemish* at EPH.1.4, EPH.5.27, JUD.1.24, PHP.2.15 and REV.14.5, as `ἄσπιλος` → *without spot*, `ἄθεοι` → *without God*, `ἄκαρπα` → *without fruit*, `ἄλογα` → *without reason*, `ἄνυδροι` → *without water* and `ἄραφος` → *without seam* do; an adverb is no different, `ἀφόβως` → *without fear* heading on *fear* at JUD.1.12 and PHP.1.14 and `δωρεάν` → *without a cause* on *cause* at JHN.15.25, the supplied determiner riding along under rule 7. Rule 17 is what decides it — the noun is the meaning-bearing complement and *without* only spells the privative prefix — and rule 66's generic-completion test agrees, the noun being a gloss of the lexeme rather than an empty completion. The corpus is thirteen units to none and not one of them is hedged, so it is settled: a head on *without* is not a materially different analysis under rule 16, and hedging such a unit against an unbroken line is the recurring failure. The contrast is a lexeme whose own sense **is** *without* — the adverbs `ἔξω` → *without are* and `ἔξωθεν` → *without were*, meaning *outside* — where the word is the gloss and heads itself. So the test is whether *without* translates the lexeme or only its privative prefix, and never that *without* stands first. Rule 71 is the same principle for an improper preposition's frame and rule 67 is untouched, since no Greek preposition is expressed here for a frame to ride on.

85. A supplied English complementizer belongs to the **non-finite complement** the ASV expands into a finite clause, not to the governing finite verb. `καλῶς ποιεῖτε προσέχοντες` → *ye do well that ye take heed* gives a bare *ye do* to `ποιεῖτε` and *that ye take heed* to the participle, as PHP.4.14's identical `καλῶς ἐποιήσατε συγκοινωνήσαντές` → *ye did well that ye had fellowship* does, and as `σπουδάσω … ἔχειν ὑμᾶς` → *I will give diligence that … ye may be able* gives the *that* to the infinitive. Rule 40 is what decides it: a subordinating element is realized by whatever word introduces its **English** clause, and the clause the complementizer introduces is the expanded complement's, not the matrix verb's. Rule 61 says the same from the other side, giving the infinitival marker to its own verb wherever no preposition governs the construction. The corpus reads it this way at TIT.1.16, 2CO.10.7, 2CO.10.2, 2CO.11.5, 2CO.13.7, JHN.16.2, JHN.12.18, TIT.3.8, EPH.4.17 and PHP.4.14, eleven unhedged units against three the other way, and three lemmas — `δοκέω`, `εὔχομαι`, `ὁμολογέω` — appear on both sides with the non-finite carrier unhedged each time. Rule 74's closing aside is the dissent and it is narrower than it reads: it exists only to stop a reader inferring the complementizer's placement from the supplied subject's, and the one row it cites, JHN.5.39, is itself `medium` and concedes the infinitive in its own note. So do not read that aside as a placement rule. An **expressed** Greek conjunction is untouched by this and is aligned under rule 40 in the ordinary way.

86. A supplied English clause-introducer that **no source token licenses** is `added`. Where the ASV subordinates a clause whose Greek is a finite indicative with no conjunction anywhere in the verse, nothing is present for rule 40 to realize, so `ἐγνωρίσαμεν` → *when we made known* gives *we made known* to the verb and marks *when* `added` under rule 5. The test is whether a source token carries the subordination itself. A **participle** does, which is why a supplied subordinator on one is an ordinary rule 7 helper — `ὑπάρχοντα` → *if are*, `ἐνεχθείσης` → *when there was borne*, `ὄντες` → *when we were*, `ποιοῦντες` → *if ye do* — and the corpus places a supplied *when* on a participle in sixty-six of its sixty-seven units and on no other finite indicative. A conjunction expressed elsewhere in the sentence does, where the ASV repeats its English in a coordinated clause. Where neither is present the word answers to nothing, and PHP.1.22 and PHP.1.20 already mark such a resumptive *if* and *that* `added` even with a conditional particle standing in the sentence. Rule 11 does not decide this either way: the ASV italicizes such a word only sometimes. Rule 37 is the contrast: a licensing token displaced by verse numbering is mapped into the ASV packet before alignment; here there is no licensing token at all.

87. Where a Greek clause carries a universal `πᾶς` together with a negator and the ASV writes the negation once, as a single *no*, the two tokens are **one unit** taking that word. `πᾶσα προφητεία … οὐ γίνεται` → *no prophecy … is* joins the pair, as 1JN.2.21, 1JN.3.15, EPH.5.5, GAL.2.16 and REV.22.3 do, six corpus units with none split. Rule 39's test is what joins them — the single English word spells the negation and the quantification together — and rule 15 permits the discontinuity, which is often wide. Rules 41 and 59 are not the licence and not the contrast: they empty a negator only where the second word is **itself a negative** able to carry the English negation alone, and a quantifier is not, so those rules settle emptying and have nothing to say about joining. Rule 80 is a different construction, `πᾶς` with a **substantival article**, which rule 32 can empty because an article supplies a pronoun and nothing else; 1JN.3.15 writes both in one verse, giving `πᾶς ὁ μισῶν` → *Whosoever* and joining `πᾶς … οὐκ` → *no*. Where the negator is `οὐ μή`, rule 33 takes the whole English negation and `πᾶς` stands on its own English word or on none, as REV.21.27 and REV.18.22 do. Where the ASV writes **two** English words the pair splits like any other, as 1JN.2.16 and 1JN.2.19 do. Say in the note that the reading is contested: every corpus instance is hedged, and giving *no* to the negator alone remains defensible.

88. Where the ASV converts a Greek circumstantial participle into a coordinate clause, the supplied English juncture *and* is a rule 7 helper on the verb of the clause it **introduces**, never on the participle it follows, and never `added`. `ταρταρώσας παρέδωκεν` → *cast them down to hell, and committed them* gives *and committed* to the finite verb, as `φθεγξάμενον … ἐκώλυσεν` → *spake with man's voice and stayed* gives *and stayed* to `ἐκώλυσεν` and leaves `φθεγξάμενον` a bare *spake*. Rule 29 is what the corpus cites for this, by name, at 1PE.3.19, EPH.2.17, 1PE.1.13, JHN.9.11 and JHN.21.12, and the principle carries unchanged from a compound numeral to a clause: an obligatory English juncture is a helper on what it introduces. The corpus places such a juncture on the verb it introduces in thirty-four units across twenty-one chapters — JHN.8.2, JHN.8.6, JHN.8.7, JHN.8.8, JHN.2.15, JHN.12.3, JHN.13.4, JHN.19.2, EPH.2.17, 1PE.3.19 and 1PE.1.13 among them — against REV.1.1 `ἀποστείλας` → *sent and* alone on the participle it follows. The coordinate clause need not be **finite**: the word *finite* names the ordinary case and is not a scope, what licenses the fold being that a participle standing in the coordination has had its subordination converted into coordination. So where the ASV renders such a participle as one bare complement of a governing verb and coordinates it with a second complement, the juncture is still a rule 7 helper on the verb of the clause it introduces, whatever that clause's English finiteness: `Θέλεις εἰς Ἱεροσόλυμα ἀναβὰς ἐκεῖ κριθῆναι` → *Wilt thou go up to Jerusalem and there be judged* gives the discontinuous *and … be judged* to `κριθῆναι` under rule 15, an adverb of the introduced clause standing between, and leaves `ἀναβάς` a bare *go up*. Rule 86 is not the contrast and must not be read as making such a word `added`: its test is whether a source token licenses the word, and a participle standing in the same clause is exactly such a token, which is why no corpus unit has ever marked a participle-to-finite conversion juncture `added`. Rule 98 is the near contrast and its own scope is what keeps the two apart: it reaches an asyndetic seam between two **like** constituents each rendered in its own class — two infinitives, two attributive participles, two adjectives — where nothing has been converted and only the coordination is supplied, and a participle coordinated with a constituent of another class is not that seam. So ask whether a participle stands in the coordination before asking how finite the English is, and never decide it from the English alone. The historical `added` bare *and* examples describe different cases: the two at 2CO.13.14 were verse-boundary defects now governed by rule 37's upstream mapping; JHN.9.28 has a connective its unit's excluded fallback dropped under rule 97; and REV.15.6 has a juncture between adjectives. An expressed Greek conjunction is untouched by this and is aligned in the ordinary way.

89. A Greek article governing an expressed vocative is `aligned` to the address word the ASV supplies for it — *ye*, *thou*, *O* — headed on that word, and the noun it governs stands bare. `Οἱ ἄνδρες` → *Ye husbands* gives *Ye* to the article and *husbands* to `ἄνδρες`, as EPH.6.4 → *ye fathers*, EPH.6.9 → *ye masters*, the three articles of REV.18.20 and REV.19.5 → *all ye his servants* do, and as an article taking a supplied *O* does at REV.6.10 and REV.12.12. This is rules 51 and 60's argument for a third kind of supplied word: a slot the ASV fills in front of the noun and no other source token competes for is the article's own to take, and an address word is such a filler. REV.19.5 is what shows that rule 20 is not the contrast — the possessive slot there is already occupied by `αὐτοῦ` → *his*, and the article still takes *ye* — because an address word is not a determiner a genitive pronoun can displace. Rule 23 is the contrast, and the test is the ASV's: where it writes a bare vocative and supplies no word at all the article realizes nothing, as `οἱ οἰκέται` → *Servants* at 1PE.2.18, `Οἱ ἄνδρες` → *Husbands* at EPH.5.25 and EPH.5.28, and EPH.6.1 and EPH.6.5 do. Where the Greek vocative is anarthrous no article competes and the supplied word is an ordinary rule 7 helper on the noun: `γυναῖκες` → *ye wives*, `Φιλιππήσιοι` → *ye Philippians*, `οὐρανέ` → *thou heaven*, `Κορίνθιοι` → *O Corinthians*. REV.18.20 writes both halves in one verse, which is what shows that the presence of the article and not English word order decides. The corpus has no unit anywhere in which an article stands before a noun whose English carries a supplied address word and is left `unrepresented`. Where a participle rather than a noun follows, rule 32 splits the unit as it always does and the article keeps only the address word. That closing clause reaches only an article the ASV writes the address word **for**, and which article that is is decided by the token the address word stands in front of, never by the mere presence of an article somewhere in the vocative phrase. An article standing in that phrase but governing something other than the addressed noun is not that noun's article and takes no address word. Where the ASV writes the address word on an **expressed anarthrous** vocative noun under this rule's anarthrous arm and a further article governs a participle or a phrase standing in **apposition** to that noun, the noun has already written the antecedent, so rule 32's apposition clause empties the article and the participle keeps the supplied relativizer along with its own verb: `ὁδηγοὶ τυφλοί, οἱ λέγοντες` → *ye blind guides, that say* gives the discontinuous *ye … guides* to `ὁδηγοί`, a bare *blind* to the adjective, nothing to the article, and *that say* to the participle, as `Ἰερουσαλὴμ Ἰερουσαλήμ, ἡ ἀποκτείνουσα` → *O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, that killeth* leaves its article empty with *O … Jerusalem* on the first name. Rule 31's expressed-head-noun test reaches the same answer, the addressed noun being an expressed head noun standing in front of the article, so the two tests agree and neither is a licence to hand such an article a second address word the ASV never wrote. A reference comparison will report such an `unrepresented` article as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction.

90. Where a **substantival** article stands before a participle and the ASV writes only **one** English word for the pair, that word is the pronoun rule 32 gives the article, and the participle keeps its own verb and any supplied copula. `οἱ ἐπηρεάζοντες` → *who revile* gives *who* to the article and *revile* to the participle, as EPH.1.19, EPH.2.11, EPH.2.14, GAL.1.4, JHN.6.33, REV.1.4, REV.1.8, REV.2.18, REV.2.20, REV.4.8, REV.7.10, REV.8.13, REV.11.17, REV.12.10, REV.15.7, REV.16.5 and REV.16.9 do — about twenty-eight units, of which only EPH.2.11 and EPH.2.14 are hedged and neither concedes the other reading. A single relativizer is pronoun and relativizer at once, so rule 32's *and nothing else* limits what the article may absorb beyond the pronoun rather than denying it one: `οἱ κατοικοῦντες` → *they that dwell* still splits, and where the ASV writes two words the ordinary split applies, as *He* / *that would* and *every man* / *that asketh* do. Rule 31 is the contrast and its test is unchanged: where the article governs an expressed head noun the relativizer is the participle's helper and the article is `unrepresented`, and this rule blesses no unit in which a head noun is expressed. JHN.1.18 stood in the enumeration above and no longer does: its `ὁ ὢν εἰς τὸν κόλπον τοῦ πατρός` follows the expressed head noun `υἱός`, so it falls on rule 31's side, and its chapter reconciliation reread it there with the article `unrepresented` and *who is* the participle's. Count the head noun before counting the English words, and do not read a citation list as a licence where the rule's own scope excludes the unit. Rule 80 is untouched, another source word having already taken the one English word there, and so is rule 22, which speaks of no participle following. EPH.4.10 alone reads it the other way and is `medium`, which is rule 71's shape rather than rule 67's genuinely divided case. A reference comparison will report such an article as represented, and that is not a correction.

91. Rule 66 decides the head of a one-word-to-phrase rendering by asking whether the English noun glosses the lexeme or completes it, and that test is operational: the head is the word the **corpus already spends on that lexeme**, never the phrase's formal English noun. Rule 17 forbids choosing a word merely because it is the phrase's formal head, and English syntax is the only thing such a choice could rest on. So `σωφρονήσατε` → *be ye of sound mind* heads on *sound*, the corpus glossing `σωφρ-` *sober* and *soberly* in every unit and *mind* in none — TIT.1.8, TIT.2.2, TIT.2.5 and TIT.2.6, the last of them the same lemma — and `βλασφημοῦντες` → *speaking evil of you* heads on *speaking*, the corpus heading `βλασφημέω` on the verb word in all twelve of its units with TIT.3.2's identical *to speak evil of* on *speak* unhedged, as `καταλαλιάς` → *evil speakings* heads on *speakings* at 1PE.2.1 and `δυσφημίας` → *evil report* on *report* at 2CO.6.8. A **genuinely empty** support verb is the contrast, and the same test tells the two apart, because there no part of the English verb answers to the lexeme and it is a rule 7 helper: `μαρτυρέω` → *bear witness* heads on *witness* in all twenty-eight of its units, as `εὐχαριστέω` → *give thanks* heads on *thanks*, `γνωρίζω` → *make known* on *known*, `εὐθύνω` → *make straight* on *straight*, and `ξενίζω` → *think it strange* on *strange*, the corpus glossing `ξεν-` *strange* and spending *think* on `δοκέω`, `ἡγέομαι`, `νοέω`, `λογίζομαι` and `οἴομαι` alone. `ἡγοῦμαι` → *I think it* is the control and heads on *think* at 2PE.1.13, because there *think* is what glosses the lexeme and rule 2 leaves the complement its own Greek word. So the question is never whether the English reads as a light-verb phrase but which of its words the corpus spends on that Greek word, and a query on the lemma is what answers it. Rule 73 is untouched, governing the inverse case where the **Greek** expresses both elements and the ASV collapses them. Rule 84's line holds here too: where the corpus is unbroken the head is not in doubt, and hedging against it is the recurring failure.

92. Rule 31's carve-out gives an article the supplied relativizer where its modifier has no verb to carry one, and that carve-out does not reach an **expressed appositional predicate noun**. Where the ASV recasts such a phrase as a finite relative clause, the article takes only the determiner the ASV writes for it and the **noun** carries the supplied relativizer and copula. `ὁ μάρτυς ὁ πιστός` → *who is the faithful witness* gives *the* to the article and *who is … witness* to the noun at REV.1.5, as `ὁ κύριός` → *he who is … Master* leaves *who is Master* to `κύριος` at EPH.6.9, and as the anarthrous `κατηγορίᾳ` → *who are accused* at TIT.1.6 and `παρεπιδήμοις` → *who are sojourners* at 1PE.1.1 do. Rule 68's `κοινωνός` → *he is partner* at 2CO.8.23 is this same lexeme with both supplied words on the noun. The test is rule 53's own — what is left in the phrase to carry the supplied words — and an expressed predicate noun is such a token, whereas a prepositional phrase or a bare adverb is not, which is why rules 31 and 32 give the relativizer to the article there, and why JHN.12.43 still splits the copula off onto the noun even where the article keeps the relativizer. Rule 90 is untouched, saying in terms that it blesses no unit in which a head noun is expressed, and rule 63 is not the contrast, its exception being scoped to a subject **pronoun** where an article that supplies no pronoun is none. Where the ASV does write a separate antecedent pronoun, the article owns that pronoun and the noun still keeps the relativizer: `τοὺς … ἀποστόλους` → *them that were* at GAL.1.17, `οἱ … σπιλάδες` → *they who are* at JUD.1.12, and EPH.6.9's own *he*. The determiner the article keeps is whichever the ASV writes, definite or indefinite: the corpus aligns an article to a supplied *a* in all eight such units — GAL.3.20, JHN.6.60, JHN.7.51, JHN.8.44, JHN.12.24, JHN.15.6, JHN.16.21, REV.4.7 — headed on the determiner, and JHN.6.60 is this very recast. Rule 51's supplied-determiner-as-helper clause is scoped to **anarthrous** Greek, as rule 57 says in terms, so do not hand a supplied determiner to a noun standing behind its own article.

93. A `noncompositional` unit under rules 39 and 45 never absorbs an intervening **article**: the article contributes no part of the fused English word, so it stays its own `unrepresented` unit under rule 23. `ἀπὸ τοῦ νῦν` → *henceforth* joins the preposition and the adverb and leaves `τοῦ` empty, as `εἰς τὸ πάλιν` leaves its own article empty at 2CO.13.2. Not one of the corpus's 102 `noncompositional` units contains an article token, so this is not a judgment call. What **is** a judgment call is which of the two the phrase is, and rule 34 against rule 45 decides it on the English: where the adverb's own ordinary gloss already spells the whole English word, the preposition is pleonastic and is `unrepresented` with the adverb taking the word alone, which is why 2CO.13.2 gives *again* to `πάλιν` and nothing to `εἰς`; where the English word carries a sense the adverb alone does not, the preposition is in it and rule 45 joins the pair, which is why `νῦν` — *now* in 54 of its 62 corpus units and never *henceforth* — cannot take *henceforth* by itself. Decide that from what the corpus spends on the adverb, as rule 91 decides a head, and say in the note where the phrase is unattested. The article's status is unaffected either way. That judgment call is scoped to a governed **adverb**, which is a word able to spell an English adverb by itself, and the pleonastic arm is not available at all where the governed word is a **noun** and the single English word the ASV writes is an **adverb**: a noun does not spell an adverb, the adverbial force being what the preposition and the case contribute, so rule 94's count test makes the pair one unit and rule 39's test keeps it `aligned` wherever part of the English answers to the noun's own lexeme, headed on the noun. `ἐν τάχει` → *quickly*, *shortly* and *speedily* is one `aligned` unit on the dative noun in every corpus unit that writes the preposition — 1TI.3.14, REV.1.1, ROM.16.20 and LUK.18.8 — and not one leaves `ἐν` `unrepresented`, the bare `τάχει` taking the word alone only where the Greek writes no preposition, as REV.22.6 does. Do not read a cognate Greek **adverb** the ASV renders by that same English word — `ταχέως` → *shortly*, *quickly*, *hastily* — as evidence that the noun spells it by itself: that is evidence of the opposite, the adverb being where the corpus spends the bare English adverb and the noun being what needs the preposition to reach it.

94. A Greek preposition and the word it governs form **one unit** where the ASV renders the pair jointly and no split meets rule 2's ownership test. Rules 35, 39 and 45 decide which status such a unit takes — `aligned` where some part of that word answers to a source token, `noncompositional` where none does — but English word count alone does not decide whether to split. The corpus's ordinary shape is a single English word: of its compound prepositional units, every one renders to a single English word apart from a supplied copula riding along under rules 7 and 53, as `ἐν αὐτῇ` → *are therein* does, or an English adverbial that is itself one lexeme spelt apart, as `ἐπὶ τόπον` → *any whither* is. Where the ASV instead spreads the phrase over separate words with independently defensible ownership under rule 2, split them: `καθ᾽ ὑπερβολὴν εἰς ὑπερβολήν` → *more and more exceedingly* gives *more* to the first `ὑπερβολήν` and *and more exceedingly* to the second, each preposition standing as its own `unrepresented` unit under rule 34 because no English word answers to it, exactly as `GAL.1.13` splits the same `καθ᾽ ὑπερβολήν`. Rule 45's own closing clause already says this for the case where the separate words each answer to a token — `διὰ τοῦτο` → *For this cause* — and this rule is that clause holding where they do not. Do not keep an idiom together where those correspondences remain independently recoverable, or split it merely because English writes several words. Single-word fusion is not peculiar to a preposition and its complement. Where a Greek **attributive adjective and its head noun** are compressed into a **single English word**, the two tokens are likewise one unit, `aligned` under rule 39's test wherever part of that word answers to each of them, and headed under rule 10 on the **noun**: `λίθος μυλικός` → *millstone* spells *mill-* for the adjective and *-stone* for the noun, as `ἡμέρας μέσης` → *at midday* is one unit at ACT.26.13 and `πάσας ἡμέρας` → *always* at MAT.28.20, and as rule 29 already joins `δέκα καὶ ὀκτώ` → *eighteen*. Do not leave the modifier `unrepresented` and hand the whole word to the noun. What this arm does **not** reach is a phrase one of whose tokens the corpus already glosses by that whole English word **by itself**: there the other token carries no part of it and stands `unrepresented`, which is what rules 34, 48, 80 and 93 decide, `ἀπὸ μακρόθεν` → *afar off*, `οὐ μὴ … εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα` → *never*, `πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων` → *whosoever* and `εἰς τὸ πάλιν` → *again* each leaving a token empty for that reason. So ask first whether one token's own gloss already spells the word, as rule 93 has it, and join the pair only where it does not.

95. Rule 71 confines rule 67's standing division to **proper** prepositions, but gives no test for telling the two classes apart, and the morph tag cannot supply one: the packet tags `ἐνώπιον` — rule 71's own leading example of an improper preposition — `PREP` in every one of its units, exactly as it tags `διά` and `ἐκ`, while `πέραν` and its fellows are tagged `ADV`. Decide from the lexeme instead, as rule 64 decides a particle from its work and never from its tag. A preposition that governs one case only and never forms a verbal compound is **improper**, and rule 71 settles its complex frame on the frame's lexical noun. So `εἵνεκεν τῆς ὑπερβαλλούσης δόξης` → *by reason of the glory that surpasseth* heads on *reason*, and the corpus units heading the identical *by reason of* on *by* — JHN.12.11, REV.9.2, REV.18.19, 2CO.9.14, 2PE.2.2 and 2PE.3.12 — are not counter-precedent, every one of them carrying a proper `διά` or `ἐκ`. Rule 42's reasoning is what forces this: the adverb keeps the English phrase that renders the adverb itself, the noun is what renders it, and the preposition standing in front belongs to the English idiom rather than answering to the governed case. REV.14.10 is the shape to hold in mind, giving `ἐνώπιον` the whole frame including its trailing *of* and leaving the following article a bare *the*, which is why an article after such a frame is not owed an *of the* under rule 21. This rule fixes the classification only. Rule 67's division stays exactly as open as it was for a proper preposition, which is why REV.13.14 and REV.8.13 head `διά` and `ἐκ` on *reason* without contradicting anything here, and a chapter carrying such a frame on a proper preposition should still follow its neighbours and say so in the note.

96. Rules 53, 62, 63 and 68 decide a verbless clause's supplied copula and subject by what is left in the clause to carry them, and none of them says what happens when a **modifier** stands inside the predicate phrase. It is not the carrier. A **determiner-carrying article** keeps only its own English determiner under rule 21, and the **predicate noun** takes the supplied words, discontinuously under rule 15. `οὕτως καὶ τῆς παρακλήσεως` → *so are ye also of the comfort* gives *of the* to the article and the discontinuous *are ye … comfort* to `παρακλήσεως`, as the same verse's `τῶν παθημάτων` → *of the sufferings* gives *of the* to its article and *sufferings* to its noun — one construction, one analysis, which is rule 24. The corpus is what settles this: no unit anywhere aligns an article to an English supplied **subject pronoun** it does not itself realize, while nine put a supplied subject and copula together on a predicate noun — `κοινωνός` → *he is partner*, `ἀπόστολοι` → *they are the messengers* and `δόξα` → *they are the glory* at 2CO.8.23, `θεός` → *it is God* at 2CO.4.6, `κτίσις` → *he is a creature*, `ὥρᾳ` → *it was at hour*, `λόγια` → *it were oracles*. 2CO.5.13's `θεῷ` → *it is unto God* is the shape to hold in mind: there the noun takes the case preposition as well, and its note records that it does so only because no article stood to anchor it — so where one does stand, rule 21 gives the article that preposition and nothing more. An **attributive quantifier or numeral** standing in that phrase is no more the carrier than an article is, and the expletive *there* of an English existential is a supplied subject like any other, decided the same way. `εἷς θεός` → *there is one God* gives a bare *one* to the numeral and the discontinuous *there is … God* to `θεός`, as `οὐδεὶς θεός` → *there is no God* gives a bare *no* to the quantifier and *there is … God* to `θεός`, and as 1TI.2.5 reads the identical frame. A **negative** quantifier is not the exception: rules 41, 59 and 87 decide where the English negation goes and say nothing about a supplied verb, so carrying the clause's negation does not make a quantifier the carrier of one. A bare **negator** is no more the carrier, and an English frame that spells the supplied words and the negation adjacently is not evidence that it is: `οὐ τοῦ θέλοντος` → *it is not of him that willeth* gives a bare *not* to `οὐ` and the discontinuous *it is of him* to the substantival article, as `Οὐχ οἷον` → *it is not as* gives *it is … as* to `οἷον`, and as `οὐ τὰ τέκνα τῆς σαρκός` → *it is not the children of the flesh* gives *it is … children* to the noun and only *the* to its article. A clause carrying an **expressed** finite Greek verb is untouched by that, rule 68 speaking of a verbless clause alone: there the English copula is the artifact of a cleft rather than the clause's own, and the corpus keeps such a frame whole on the negator, as `οὐ Μωϋσῆς δέδωκεν` → *It was not Moses that gave* and `οὐ διὰ τοῦτο πλανᾶσθε` → *Is it not for this cause that ye err* do. Where the negative word is itself the **predicate** rather than a modifier of one, rule 63 applies in the ordinary way and the copula is its helper, `οὐδὲν εἴδωλον ἐν κόσμῳ` → *no idol is anything in the world* keeping *is* on `οὐδέν`; so the test is the one rule 63 already states, which nominal the clause predicates of the other, and never which of them is negative or which stands first. Rules 22, 31, 32, 89 and 90 are untouched and are the contrast: an article that itself realizes a pronoun, a relativizer or an address word may of course carry a copula predicated of it, which is why `τοὺς ἐν πάσῃ θλίψει` → *them that are in any affliction* is unaffected, and why the substantival article of `οὐ τοῦ θέλοντος` takes the supplied subject with it — this rule's observation about articles is scoped to a determiner-carrying one. Rule 62's preposition carrier is not a model for an article, rule 67 having already warned that such an analogy jumps carrier classes.

97. Where a textual unit's decision leaves the projection carrying a reading the ASV **excludes**, English that answers to the missing source word is `added` under rule 5, and the note must carry enough for a later reader to check it: the unit id, the reading the projection carries, the readings that remained, and why the word is absent. Rule 12 is untouched — this is the citation, not a second vote on the unit. Two kinds of decision do it and the packet signals only the first. It marks an **underdetermined** unit whose fallback the evidence ruled out `FALLBACK EXCLUDED BY EVIDENCE` beneath its variant line, and an underdetermined unit whose fallback is still among the readings the English permits carries no such mark and is not this case; but a **selected** unit carries no mark either, and where the reading it selects is shorter than the one the ASV translated the English is `added` on exactly the same terms, the note naming the selected reading in place of the excluded fallback. So the absence of the mark settles nothing on its own: read the variant line. `ὑπὸ Χριστοῦ` → *by Christ Jesus* at PHP.3.12 leaves *Jesus* `added`, `SBLGNT.PHP.3.12.v007` having kept r01 `Χριστοῦ Ἰησοῦ` and r02 `τοῦ Χριστοῦ Ἰησοῦ` while the projection carries the bare base the English rules out; the verse-opening *And* of JHN.9.28 is `added` for the same reason, `SBLGNT.JHN.9.28.v029` having kept the connectives of r01 `καὶ` and r02 `οἱ δὲ` against an asyndetic base; and *kneeling down* at MRK.1.40 is `added` on the selected arm, `SBLGNT.MRK.1.40.v057` having selected r02, which carries no participle of kneeling though the ASV translates one. JHN.10.13 is the same shape for a whole clause and is the standard for the note. A textual note the ASV prints of its own — *Some ancient authorities omit the Son of God* at MRK.1.1, *Some ancient authorities omit and kneeling down to him* at MRK.1.40 — corroborates the reading and belongs in the note, but it does not license the status by itself: what licenses it is that no token of the projection answers to the English. Rule 37 is the contrast: a licensing token displaced by verse numbering is mapped into the ASV packet before alignment; here it stands in no verse of the selected projection at all. Do not reach for rule 7 to fold such a word onto a neighbouring token — a helper belongs to the word that licenses it, and the word that would have licensed this one is absent — and do not reach for `noncompositional`. Rule 11 does not decide this either way, the ASV italicizing such a word only sometimes. The mirror case is where the projection instead carries source material the ASV did not translate, the reading it carries being **longer** than the one the ASV rendered: each surplus token is then its own `unrepresented` unit and owes the same note. It does not turn on the unit having been decided. ACT.9.12 is the first instance and its unit `SBLGNT.ACT.9.12.v010` is underdetermined, r00-base against r01, the projection carrying `ἐν ὁράματι` where the ASV writes no *in a vision* in that verse, so the preposition and the noun stand as two `unrepresented` units whose notes name the unit id and the readings that remained. Rule 2 is why they are two units and not one: an empty target gives no ground for joining them, and rules 30, 48 and 93 already keep `unrepresented` per token. Rule 12 is untouched here too, this being a statement about what the English realizes and not a vote on the unit. A reference comparison will report every such token as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction.

98. Rule 88's protection of a supplied juncture is scoped in terms to where the ASV **converts a Greek circumstantial participle into a coordinate finite clause**, and it does not reach an **asyndetic seam between two like constituents both rendered alike**. There the juncture is `added` under rule 5, no source token carrying the coordination. `ἐπισκέπτεσθαι … τηρεῖν` → *to visit … and to keep* at JAS.1.27 marks the supplied *and* `added`, as 2CO.3.7 does across two attributive participles and REV.15.6 across two adjectives. 2CO.3.7 is the governing precedent and is the corpus's one test of this limit decided after rule 88 entered the contract: it concedes nothing and cites rule 5 with REV.15.6 by name. Rule 86's test gives the same answer and is the reason for it — a participle carries subordination, which is why rule 86 grants it a supplied *when* or *if*, but a second infinitive, participle or adjective carries no coordination, so nothing is present for the word to answer to. Rule 29's compound numeral is narrower still and is not a general licence, naming an obligatory juncture inside one numeral idiom. Do not read rule 88's closing aside — that the corpus's `added` bare *and* units are all something else — as an exhaustive list; it was written when 2CO.3.7 and JAS.1.27 did not yet stand. TIT.2.9 `εἶναι` → *and to be* is the one contrary unit and is weak evidence: its note cites no rule and the chapter was finished before rule 88 existed. Rule 11 does not decide this either way, the ASV italicizing such a juncture only sometimes. An expressed Greek conjunction is untouched, and so is a conjunction expressed elsewhere in the sentence whose English the ASV repeats in a coordinated clause, which rule 86 already names as a licensing token.

99. Rule 91's operational test decides an **interrogative or correlative adjective** the ASV expands with the frame *what manner of*, and the head is ***what***. `ὁποῖος` → *what manner of man* heads on *what* at JAS.1.24, as `ποταποὺς` → *what manner of persons* does at 2PE.3.11, `ποταπήν` → *what manner of* at 1JN.3.1, `ποῖον` at 1PE.1.11, and `ποίῳ` at JHN.12.33, JHN.18.32 and JHN.21.19 — six units on *what* and none on *manner*; `ὁποῖος` heads on the *what-* word at GAL.2.6 as well, `ὁποῖοί` → *whatsoever*. The *manner of* is the supplied frame and any noun following it the generic completion, so rule 17's generic-noun clause keeps the head on the interrogative word exactly as it does for `ταῦτα` → *these things*. The recurring failure is decomposing the Greek instead — arguing that `ὁποῖος` or `ποταπός` adds a sortal element over a bare `τίς` and that *manner* is therefore what glosses it — which is the compositional argument rule 91 replaces with a query on the lemma. A **noun** whose own English is *manner of X* is the contrast and is untouched by this: there the frame is not supplied, and rule 66 decides it on its own terms, `ἀναστροφή` → *manner of life* heading on *life* at 1PE.1.18, 1PE.3.16 and EPH.4.22 and `καθαρισμόν` → *manner of purifying* on *purifying* at JHN.2.6. Where the frame expands a **quantifier** the same test holds and the quantifier is the head, `παντί` → *all manner of* on *all* at REV.21.19.

100. Rule 82 governs a `καί` answering a comparative where no `οὕτως` stands, and says nothing about the pair standing together. Where `οὕτως` and a **separate** `καί` both stand, the adverb takes the whole English correlative — *so* or *even so*, headed on *so* — and the `καί` is decided on its own by rule 65's English-side test: `aligned` on the word the ASV writes for it, `unrepresented` where it writes none. `οὕτως καὶ ἡ πίστις … νεκρά ἐστιν` → *Even so faith … is dead* gives *Even so* to the adverb and leaves the particle empty, the ASV writing no *also*; where it does write one, `οὕτως … καί` → *even so … also* gives *also* to the particle and still leaves *even so* whole, as JHN.5.21, JHN.5.26, 2CO.1.5 and EPH.5.28 do, and as a bare *so … also* does at 2CO.1.7, 2CO.8.6, EPH.5.24, GAL.4.29 and JAS.1.11. The corpus is unanimous across all thirteen such units and heads `οὕτως` → *even so* on *so* in all six of its instances. Do not split *even* off onto the `καί` as its direct gloss: rule 82 forbids in terms heading such a unit on *even*, and confines that bare-*even* gloss to where the ASV writes no correlative at all (2CO.11.12, EPH.2.3). Reading the *so* out of the adverb's unit in order to claim that no *so* competes is circular, and it produces a crossed alignment, the English *Even* preceding *so* where the Greek order is the reverse. A preceding comparative is not required and its absence licenses nothing: JAS.1.11 has none and splits the same way. A reference comparison will report an `unrepresented` `καί` as a disagreement, and that report is not a correction.

101. Rule 42's closing clause reads as though it were scoped to a standing article, and it is not. An improper preposition keeps its trailing English preposition — *apart from*, *separate from*, *near to*, *nigh unto*, *in the sight of*, *in the presence of* — whenever the ASV gives the governed noun no determiner that an **article** could anchor, and whether or not a Greek article stands there at all. `χωρὶς ἐμοῦ` → *apart from* at JHN.15.5 and `χωρὶς Χριστοῦ` → *separate from* at EPH.2.12 are anarthrous, with no article in the phrase, and both keep the preposition on the adverb, as `χωρὶς τῶν ἔργων` → *apart from works* does with an `unrepresented` article standing. What moves the preposition is rule 21's determiner and nothing else: where the ASV writes one for the governed noun the article takes it and carries the case preposition with it, leaving the adverb bare — *apart* and *from the* at 2CO.12.3, *near* and *to the* at JHN.11.54, *in the presence* and *of the* at JHN.20.30. So the presence of a Greek article is not the test, because an article the ASV gives no determiner has nothing to anchor. Rule 21's determinerless clause hands a case preposition to the noun only where no improper preposition is expressed to hold it, and no corpus unit anywhere puts such a trailing preposition on a bare governed noun. Where the ASV renders the adverb by a single word the question does not arise, as `χωρίς` → *without* and *besides* are.

102. An article that governs an **expressed** noun is not thereby barred from taking a nominal the ASV supplies inside its own phrase, and rule 22's *never the determiner* is not scoped to a substantival article. Where the ASV writes a determiner plus a supplied noun in front of the article's own noun, both words are the article's and the unit heads on the **supplied noun**: `τοῦ κυρίου` → *the kingdom* of gives the anaphoric nominal and the case preposition to the article and leaves `κυρίου` a bare *Lord* at REV.11.15, as `τῆς δόξης` → *the Lord* does, and as `τὴν … αἰώνιον` → *the life* does at 1JN.1.2, whose note records in terms that rule 49 does not reach an article governing an expressed head noun. Rule 60's closing clause already says the head follows the nominal wherever the article supplies one, `τῆς μελλούσης` → *that hour which* heading on *hour*; this rule is that clause holding for a supplied noun as well as a supplied determiner. The same holds where the article's complement is a **substantival adjective** and no noun is expressed at all: rule 49 gives the article both the determiner and the supplied noun, so `τὸν πτωχόν` → *the … man* and `τὰ ἐπιτήδεια` → *the things* and *needful* leave the adjective bare, headed on *man* and *things*. **Adjective** there names the ordinary case and is not a scope. The complement may equally be a substantival **quantifier** or the identity pronoun **`αὐτός`**, and the supplied nominal is the article's in either: `τὰ πάντα` → *all things* gives *things* to the article and a bare *all* to the quantifier, which is what rule 17 already says of that phrase in terms, and `τὰ αὐτά` → *the same things* gives the discontinuous *the … things* to the article under rule 15 and a bare *same* to the pronoun, as `τῷ αὐτῷ` → *by that same rule* gives *by that … rule* to the article, headed on *rule*, and a bare *same* to the pronoun. Rule 49 does not empty an article before `αὐτός`: what rule 49 empties an article for is a **demonstrative** that has taken its determiner, and rule 60 has already refused to read the identity pronoun as one, giving `αὐτῇ τῇ ὥρᾳ` → *that very hour* the article's own *that* and the intensive pronoun only *very*. Rule 103's article arm is what keeps such a supplied nominal from being `added`, however plainly the phrase is elliptical and whether or not the ASV italicizes the word. Rule 17's generic-noun clause is the contrast and is scoped to **anarthrous** Greek, where no article competes and the supplied noun is a rule 7 helper on the adjective — `πτωχός` → *a poor man* heads on *poor*, as rule 58's `νεκρῶν` → *the dead* and rule 51's anarthrous clause have it, and as an anarthrous `πάντα` → *all things* heads on *all*. So the test is whether a Greek article stands to take the determiner, exactly the test rules 51 and 58 already use, and a verse writing both shapes decides each on its own article. The corpus is unanimous: all fifty-three units of an article taking a determiner plus a nominal head on the nominal and none on *the*, and every unit finalized since rule 49 entered the contract reads the substantival-adjective shape the article's way, twelve to none. JAS.1.11 corrected this very head from *the* to *man* and JAS.1.25 repeats it, so do not read rule 17's unqualified wording as reaching a phrase an article governs. What the article takes is a **supplied** nominal, and rule 66's test is what identifies one: a word that completes the phrase and glosses no element of the adjective's own lexeme, as *man*, *part* and *things* do. Where the ASV instead renders the substantival adjective by a **multiword expression of its own**, every content word of which answers to that lexeme, no word of it is supplied and this rule has nothing to hand over: the article takes only the determiner, with any case preposition riding along under rule 21, and the whole expression stays with the adjective, headed under rule 66 on the word that glosses the lexeme rather than on the phrase's formal English head. `τῷ παραλυτικῷ` → *to the sick of the palsy* gives *to the* to the article and *sick of the palsy* to the adjective headed on *palsy*, as the same phrase does where the ASV writes *unto the sick of the palsy*. `ὁ κωφός` → *the … man* with a bare *dumb* is the contrast, *man* glossing no element of `κωφός`; and the anarthrous `παραλυτικόν` → *a man sick of the palsy* is rule 17's case, where no article competes and the completion stays with the adjective too. So ask of each English word whether it completes the phrase or renders the lexeme before handing any of it to the article, and never split one lexeme's own English between the article and the adjective in order to give the article a noun.

103. Supplied English **lexical content whose source token is already realized elsewhere in the verse** is `added` under rule 5, and rule 7 does not fold it. **The test is licensing, not recurrence.** Ask whether some source token's own English expression is still incomplete, not merely whether a same-lexeme English word stands elsewhere in the verse: a supplied word that completes what a token was going to say is a rule 7 helper on it however often that lexeme recurs, and only a word whose licensing token has already spent its English falls here. ACT.10.22 folds the supplied *of God* into `ἐχρηματίσθη`, the lexeme meaning to be divinely instructed, no other token licensing it; LUK.8.23, LUK.8.42 and LUK.8.49 fold *with water*, *of age* and *house* onto the single word whose English they complete; and HEB.6 puts it as present **and unspent** rather than italicized. Where the token that licenses the supplied word is an **article** rather than a spent noun, rule 102 takes it into the article's own phrase and nothing is `added`: `τὰ σά` → *thy goods* gives *goods* to the article at LUK.6.30, and 1CO.15 reads a repetition a substantival article governs the same way. What decides that is **where the supplied nominal stands**, and never which slot the Greek left elliptical: a supplied noun standing inside a phrase a Greek article governs completes that article's own English under rule 102 and is folded there, headed on the supplied noun, however plainly it echoes a noun the same verse expresses elsewhere. `ὁ γὰρ αὐτὸς κύριος πάντων` → *for the same Lord is Lord of all* gives the discontinuous *the … Lord* to the article and a bare *same* to the intensive pronoun, `κύριος` keeping the predicate *is Lord*, exactly as `τοῦ κυρίου` → *the kingdom* does at REV.11.15 with `βασιλεία` standing expressed in the same verse. Where the ASV repeats a predicate nominal the Greek left elliptical, `οὐκ ἔστιν αὕτη ἡ σοφία ἄνωθεν κατερχομένη` → *This wisdom is not a wisdom that cometh down* marks the second *a wisdom* `added` headed on the nominal, `σοφία` being spent on the first *wisdom*, exactly as `ὁ δὲ μεσίτης ἑνὸς οὐκ ἔστιν` → *a mediator is not a mediator of one* does at GAL.3.20 in the identical frame. GAL.3.20 is that frame and not the one above because its article governs the expressed `μεσίτης`, so the article's phrase is already complete and the repeat stands in a slot no token governs; do not read it as licensing an `added` nominal inside a phrase whose article has no expressed noun of its own. The supplied determiner stays inside the `added` unit, as GAL.3.20, REV.4.1 and REV.20.12 keep it. The corpus reads it this way throughout — a repeated antecedent inside a finite relative rendering at 2CO.3.7, a repeated predicate at REV.17.14 and REV.21.12, a resumed verb at REV.14.14 and 2JN.1.12, and a repeated quantifier at 2PE.3.11 — and 2CO.3.7 says in terms that no unit folds a repeated antecedent after a relativizer. Rule 7's list is *articles, auxiliaries, pronouns, of, to, and similar words*, and a full lexical noun or verb is not among them, which is why the absence of a competitor does not license the fold: a helper belongs to the word that licenses it, and the word that would have licensed this one has already spent its English. A resumptive **pronoun** is the contrast and it goes the other way, being exactly what rule 7 names: `Πολλὰ … γράφειν` → *many things … to write \*them\** folds the *them* onto `Πολλά` at 3JN.1.13. A repeated **function word** is the same contrast and is decided the same way: where one Greek preposition or conjunction governs two coordinate members and the ASV writes its English twice, the second occurrence is a rule 7 helper on the member it introduces and is never `added`, exactly as rule 40 has it for the repeated infinitival marker of `ἵνα ποιήσω … καὶ τελειώσω` → *to do … and to accomplish*, which gives *to accomplish* whole to `τελειώσω`, and as rule 86 already refuses `added` for the English of a conjunction expressed elsewhere in the sentence and repeated in a coordinated clause. `εἰς Μωϋσῆν καὶ τὸν θεόν` → *against Moses and against God* gives the second *against* to `θεόν` under rule 21's determinerless clause and leaves the article `unrepresented`; where the ASV writes a determiner for that member the article carries the repeated preposition instead under rule 21, as it carries any other. Do not stretch the spent preposition over the repetition under rule 15 either: the corpus's `added` units under this rule are nouns, predicates, verbs, quantifiers and antecedents, and not one is a word from rule 7's own list. Rule 11 does not decide this either way, the ASV italicizing such a repetition only sometimes. Rule 37 is the contrast: a licensing token displaced by verse numbering is mapped into the ASV packet before alignment, whereas here it stands in this one and is already spent. Rule 97 is the other, where the licensing token stands in no verse of the projection at all. Say in the note that reading the repetition as a discontinuous second realization under rule 15 remains defensible, as GAL.3.20, 2CO.3.7 and REV.21.12 each concede.

104. Rule 91's operational test asks what the corpus spends on a lexeme, and where the lemma returns **nothing** the answer is a query on the neighbouring class, never a decomposition of the Greek. Query the English phrase, the prefix family, the root family, or the frame — whichever the phrase supplies — and follow it. `ἑρπετῶν` → *of creeping things* heads on *creeping*, `ἑρπετόν` occurring nowhere outside its own verse and `ἕρπω` nowhere at all, because the corpus renders a single creeping-thing substantive by this very phrase five times and heads the modifier every time: `רֶמֶשׂ` → *creeping things* at 1KI.4.33, GEN.1.24 and GEN.6.7 and → *creeping thing* at GEN.1.26 and GEN.6.20, 1KI.4.33 saying in terms that *creeping* carries the lexeme and *things* is the generic noun supplied. So `ζῷον` → *living creature* and `κτίσμα` → *created thing* are no analogy for it, and rule 66's own test shows why: the corpus spends bare *creature(s)* and *thing* on those lexemes, so their English noun is a real gloss, whereas it never spends a bare *things* on this one. The hapax `εὐπειθής` → *easy to be entreated* heads on *entreated* the same way, the `εὐ-` compounds heading the stem word in all four units of `εὐάρεστος` → *well pleasing* and the `πειθ-` family heading the root word throughout its fifteen; and `φλογίζω`, unattested outside its verse, heads *setteth on fire* on *fire*, the `set` + complement frame heading its complement at GAL.5.1, JHN.3.33, JUD.1.8, 1PE.1.13, JHN.5.45 and 2PE.3.12 without exception. A lexeme whose own sense **is** the verb word is the contrast and the same query finds it: `ἐξέρχεται` → *cometh forth* heads on *cometh*, because `ἔρχομαι` means *come*. Rule 99's *what manner of* frame is scoped in terms to an interrogative or correlative of **quality** whose completion is a supplied sortal noun, and it does not reach a **degree** correlative whose completion is a dimension adjective: there the dimension word carries the magnitude sense and takes the head, so `ἡλίκην` → *how much* heads on *much* and `ἡλίκον` → *how small* on *small*, as `πηλίκος` → *how large* heads on *large* at GAL.6.11, `ὅσα` → *how much soever* on *much* at REV.18.7, `ὅσαι` → *how many soever be* on *many* at 2CO.1.20, and as the demonstrative member of the same paradigm does in all three units of `τηλικοῦτος` → *so great* on *great*. Analyse the interrogative and demonstrative members of one paradigm alike, which is rule 24's habit. Where the neighbouring class returns nothing either, say so plainly in the note and name the analogy the reading rests on, as `ἐνάλιος` → *things in the sea* does.
105. The ASV's supplied appositional ***even*** — the italicized word that introduces an appositive English marks by apposition rather than by a conjunction — is decided by the **class of the Greek token that carries the appositive it introduces**, and not by the ASV's italics, which rule 11 makes evidence rather than a verdict. Where an **expressed preposition** carries it, *even* folds onto that preposition as a rule 7 helper headed on the prepositional word: `ἐκ` → *even from* at GAL.1.15 and *even of* at JAS.4.1, `εἰς` → *even unto* at 2CO.10.16, `διά` → *even by* at 2CO.1.19, and `ἐν` → *even in* at GAL.5.14 and 1JN.5.20, as the improper prepositions do at PHP.2.8, REV.12.11, REV.14.20, REV.18.5 and 2CO.12.2 — ten units, none `added`, and no `added` bare *even* anywhere in the corpus stands where a preposition introduces the appositive. Where a **substantival article** carries it, *even* is its own `added` unit under rule 5 headed on itself, and the article keeps only its bare pronoun: `ὁ` → *he* at 1JN.2.22, JHN.1.27 and JAS.4.12, as `τοῖς` → *to them* at JHN.1.12, `τοὺς` → *them that* at REV.13.6 and `τὰ` → *the things that were* at REV.16.3 do, and as an articular appositive does at 1JN.2.25, JHN.3.13, REV.20.14, EPH.2.15 and JHN.15.26. Rule 32's *and nothing else* is what decides that side, rule 90 relaxing the phrase only for a **relativizer**, which is pronoun and relativizer at once, where *even* is neither; 1JN.2.25 states the positive reason and it is the whole distinction between the two carriers — a case preposition rides with an article because it realizes the article's **own case**, and *even* realizes nothing. So the article carrier is settled in rule 71's shape rather than rule 67's: nine unhedged non-conceding units against one, REV.8.9, with the six remaining folds — JHN.11.27, JUD.1.4, 1JN.5.13, JHN.14.17, JHN.14.26 and REV.19.21 — every one of them hedged and three conceding `added`. Where an **anarthrous noun or name** carries it, *even* folds onto that noun as a rule 7 helper headed on the noun: `Χριστοῦ` → *even Christ* at COL.2.2, as `Χριστός` → *even Christ* at EPH.4.15. What empties the article carrier is rule 32's *and nothing else*, which is scoped to an article and reaches no other word, so a bare noun has nothing to keep an ordinary helper out and rule 7 folds it as it folds any other supplied word onto the token that licenses it. Say in the note that marking such an *even* `added` under rule 5 remains defensible: the precedent is two units and EPH.4.15 is hedged, though the corpus carries no `added` bare *even* on a noun carrier anywhere. An **articular** noun is not this case and is the article's, decided above. Rule 24 does not require a chapter writing more than one of these to match them, the carrier classes being different, which is why JAS.4 folds at 4:1 and adds at 4:12. Rule 98 reaches none of these carriers, being scoped in terms to an asyndetic seam between two **like** constituents both rendered alike, and rule 88's juncture principle is not a route to the fold, rule 98 having already scoped it to a circumstantial participle converted into a coordinate finite clause. A supplied *even* answering an expressed `καί` or standing in a correlative is untouched by this rule and is decided by rules 82 and 100, and one the ASV supplies where no appositive follows at all — REV.2.13 `ὅπου` → *even where* — is aligned in the ordinary way.
106. An English particle or adverb that realizes a Greek **preverb** rides inside the compound verb's own unit and is never `added`, as a discontinuous realization under rule 15 where the ASV separates them: `ἐξῆλθεν` → *went forth*, `ἐξέβαλον` → *cast out*, `ἀνέβη` → *went up*, `ἐπεβίβασαν` → *set thereon* at LUK.19.35, `προσηργάσατο` → *hath made more* at LUK.19.16, `ἀπολάβωσιν` → *receive again* at LUK.6.34. The head is the English word glossing the **verbal root**, never the particle. The corpus is unanimous: 963 units across 141 chapters carry such a particle and every one of them is `aligned`, none `added`, and the head stands on the verb word in all but one class. This is scoped to a particle **no other source token stands to answer to**. Where an expressed preposition does answer to it — because the particle is part of that preposition's own English gloss, as *out* is of `ἐκ` → *out of* — the whole English prepositional expression is the preposition's, headed on the prepositional word, and the compound verb keeps only its verb word: `ἔξελθε ἐξ αὐτοῦ` → *come* and *out of*, `ἐξῆλθεν ἐξ αὐτοῦ` → *came* and *out of*, as `εἰσελθὼν εἰς` → *entered* and *into* and `ἀπῆλθεν ἀπ᾽ αὐτοῦ` → *departed* and *from* do. Rule 36 is what decides that and says it in terms: an English preposition governing a prepositional phrase realizes the Greek preposition and not the verb it depends on, even where English idiom makes the verb phrasal, and a preposition is left `unrepresented` only where English realizes it nowhere at all — so do not leave such a preposition a bare *of* while the verb takes the *out*. That arm is scoped to where the ASV writes **one** English particle for the two, and the count of English words the ASV wrote is what decides it, as rule 94 decides a compound unit by that same test. Where the ASV writes a particle for the preverb **in addition to** a complete prepositional expression, each token takes its own and the verb does not go bare: `Ἔξελθε … ἐκ τοῦ ἀνθρώπου` → *Come forth* and *out of the man* gives *forth* to the imperative and *out of* to `ἐκ`, as `τὴν ἐξ αὐτοῦ δύναμιν ἐξελθοῦσαν` → *the power proceeding from him had gone forth* gives *forth* to the participle and *proceeding from* to `ἐξ`. That the preverb and the preposition are the same morpheme is not the test and does not strand the extra word: this rule's own count forbids marking such a particle `added`, and rule 36 forbids handing it to a preposition whose English is already complete, so the verb is the only token left to take it. A **noncompositional prepositional phrase** standing in the same clause answers to the particle in the same way, and the single English word is that phrase's rather than the preverb's: `συνήχθησαν ἐπὶ τὸ αὐτό` → *were gathered together* gives *were gathered* to the verb and *together* to `ἐπί` + `αὐτό` as one `noncompositional` unit under rule 45, the intervening article standing `unrepresented` under rule 93, as MAT.22.34 and ACT.4.26 read it and as ACT.2.44 reads the same phrase where no compound verb competes. Where no such phrase stands, the preverb keeps the word in the ordinary way, which is why `συνηγμένοι` → *gathered together* and `συνήχθησαν` → *were gathered together* keep *together* inside the verb's own unit. A preverb whose sense the following preposition's gloss does **not** carry keeps this rule's ordinary treatment, which is why `ἀναβαίνων ἐκ τοῦ ὕδατος` → *coming up out of the water* gives *up* to the participle and *out of* to `ἐκ` in one clause, and why `ἐκβάλλει … εἰς` → *driveth … forth* and *into* does. Rule 94 decides only whether the preposition and the word it governs form one unit, and does not decide where the particle goes. Where the compound verb governs a bare dative or genitive that the ASV writes with an English preposition, that preposition realizes the case government and rides with the governed word under rules 21, 36 and 69, as MRK.14.65 has it and as ACT.4.1 `ἐπέστησαν αὐτοῖς` → *came* and *upon them* and ACT.4.3 `ἐπέβαλον … αὐτοῖς` → *laid* and *on them* do. That arm too is scoped to where the ASV writes an English **verb word of its own** for the compound, as *came* and *laid* are. Where it writes none, and the English preposition is the only word carrying the compound's lexical sense, the preposition stays inside the verb's unit and the governed word takes its bare pronoun or noun: `προϊσταμένους ὑμῶν` → *are over* and *you* gives *are over* to the participle headed on *over* and a bare *you* to the genitive, `προΐστημι` meaning to stand before while *are* is only the periphrastic copula. Rule 17 is what forces this, forbidding a head on a subject or an auxiliary: a unit stripped to *are* would have no lexical word left to head on, whereas the governed word loses nothing, its case being what the verb's own sense already expresses. 1TH.5 writes both arms, giving *upon them* to the dative of `ἐφίσταται` → *cometh upon them* and keeping *over* with the participle of `προϊσταμένους`, which is what shows that an English verb word standing for the compound, and not which oblique case the verb governs, is what decides between those two. An **accusative** object is the contrast, and there the case is exactly what decides: English writes no preposition to realize an accusative, so where the ASV gives such an object an English preposition that preposition is the verb's own phrasal government and stays inside the verb's unit, the governed word keeping a bare noun or pronoun. `ἐκδεχομένου αὐτούς` → *waited for them* gives *waited for* to the participle and a bare *them* to the pronoun, as `ψηλαφήσειαν αὐτόν` → *feel after him* gives *feel after* to the optative and `ἀκούσαντες ἀνάστασιν` → *heard of the resurrection* gives *heard of* to the participle. Rules 21 and 36 are why: rule 21 hands a governed word the English preposition that realizes its **case**, and an accusative has none to realize, while rule 36 leaves a preposition with the verb only where no Greek preposition is expressed for it to answer to, which is exactly this configuration. ACT.17 writes both arms, giving *with them*, *with Paul*, *of him* and *unto him* to the oblique objects of `διαλέγομαι`, `προσκληρόομαι`, `ἐπιλαμβάνομαι` and `κολλάομαι` and keeping *for*, *after* and *of* inside the verbs of `ἐκδέχομαι`, `ψηλαφάω` and `ἀκούω`. Where a Greek preposition **is** expressed the question does not arise and rule 36 gives it the English word, as it does for `ἀκούω` with `περί` → *hear of your state*. That expressed preposition need not govern the object itself, and the object's own case does not shield it: where the accusative object is a **substantival article** whose phrase an expressed preposition governs, the English preposition standing between the verb and that phrase answers to the Greek preposition and never to the verb, the article keeping only the nominal the ASV writes for it and the verb going bare. `διαγινώσκειν … τὰ περὶ αὐτοῦ` → *judge of his case* gives a bare *judge* to the verb, *of* to `περί`, *his* to `αὐτοῦ` and *case* to the article, exactly as rule 36's `τὰ περὶ ὑμῶν … μεριμνήσει` → *will care truly for your state* gives *for* to `περί` and *will care* to the verb. Rule 36's compression clause is the contrast and is narrow: it empties `περί` only where the ASV writes no preposition at all, as `τὰ περὶ ἡμῶν` → *our state* does. So ask what stands **inside** the object phrase before reading an English preposition as the verb's, and do not read a phrasal English verb as evidence that its particle belongs to the object: the test is whether the object's own case is a case English realizes with a preposition **and** whether its phrase carries a preposition of its own, which is why `ἀκούσαντες ἀνάστασιν` → *heard of the resurrection* is this arm's case and the same verb with a `περί` phrase for its object is not. The one exception is an idiom that leaves no English verb word to head on at all: `Ἆρον` → *away with him* at JHN.19.15 and `Αἶρε` → *away with* at LUK.23.18 head on *away*, because nothing else was written.
107. An intensive `αὐτός` that the ASV renders by a **two-word English emphatic** is one `aligned` unit headed on the ***-self*** word, never on the plain pronoun standing in front of it. `αὐτοὶ` → *they themselves* heads on *themselves*, as `αὐτὸς` → *he himself* heads on *himself* in ten corpus units, `αὐτὸς` → *I myself* on *myself* at PHP.2.24, `αὐτὸς` → *his own self* on *self* at 1PE.2.24, and `αὐτοῦ` → *own self* on *self* at ROM.16.2; discontinuity changes nothing, HEB.2.14's *he … himself* being one unit under rule 15. Rule 17 is what decides it: the *-self* word is what realizes the intensive force the pronoun carries, while the *he*, *she*, *they* or *I* beside it is the subject word English syntax requires and rides inside the unit as a rule 7 or rule 9 helper. So this is not the genuine tie rule 17 lets English word order break, and a head on the plain pronoun is not a materially different analysis under rule 16. The corpus is thirteen units to four, and of the four JHN.6.6 and MRK.10.12 concede this reading in their own notes, ROM.16.2 heads its own second `αὐτός` on *self* in the same verse, and only REV.19.12 argues the point, on a tie this rule denies. Where the ASV writes a **single** English word the question does not arise and that word is the head, as the units of *himself*, *itself*, *myself*, *thyself* and *herself* are, and as `αὐτὸς` → *selfsame* is. A **personal pronoun** — `ἐγώ`, `σύ`, `ἡμεῖς`, `ὑμεῖς` — that the ASV expands with a supplied *-self* word is the contrast and goes the other way, headed on the **pronoun**: `Ὑμεῖς` → *Ye yourselves* heads on *Ye* in all five of its corpus units, as `ἡμεῖς` → *we ourselves* heads on *we* at 2PE.1.18 and TIT.3.5 and `ἐγὼ` → *I myself* on *I* at PHP.3.4 and 2CO.12.16 — nine units against none. The test is the one this rule already applies, which of the two words answers to the source lexeme: `αὐτός` **is** the intensive, so the *-self* word realizes it and the plain pronoun beside it is what English syntax requires, whereas a personal pronoun is what *ye*, *we* or *I* spells and the *-self* word beside it is the supplied intensifier, a rule 7 helper riding inside the unit. So the lexeme and not the English phrase decides, which is why the identical *Ye yourselves* heads on *Ye* for `Ὑμεῖς` at ACT.20.18 and on *yourselves* for `αὐτοὶ` at ACT.20.34, one chapter writing both. Where the ASV instead writes a **separate** word for each token the pair splits like any other and each keeps its own head, `ὑμῶν αὐτῶν` → *your own selves* giving *your* to `ὑμῶν` and *own selves* to `αὐτῶν` headed on *selves*; and where **one** English word stands for both tokens the question does not arise, `ὑμῖν αὐτοῖς` → *yourselves* being one `aligned` unit on that word. An `αὐτός` carrying no intensive force is untouched by this rule: an oblique pronoun is aligned in the ordinary way, and a genitive one carries the English possessive and displaces its article under rule 20.
