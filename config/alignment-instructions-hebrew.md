# Atomic verse alignment contract (Hebrew)

This contract is `ot-hebrew-verse-v220`. Apply it independently to each prepared
verse.

## The ledger, unchanged

1. Partition every supplied `s###` source ID and `t###` target ID exactly once. Never invent an ID.
2. Prefer the smallest defensible semantic unit. Split only when every resulting correspondence is independently defensible and that source–target allocation is clearly preferable to materially plausible alternatives; an imaginable partition is not enough. Judge lexical and grammatical contributions in context: a correspondence need not be a dictionary-literal gloss or exclude ordinary English helpers. Where English expresses a construction jointly and its pieces cannot be assigned non-arbitrarily, retain the fused rendering as `noncompositional`. Source or target token count is diagnostic, never sufficient grounds to split. This tests ownership of the rendering, not alternative heads or helper placements already settled by a specific rule; explicit rulings below, including fused `aligned` renderings, retain precedence. **And because it tests ownership of a rendering, it does not reach English that no source morpheme owns.** A run of supplied English answering to no morpheme at all is one `added` unit where its words stand together and do one office in the clause, however many words it runs to and however many separate rules account for their absence from the source: rule 43 already places a supplied *have* together with any numeral, subject or object the ASV supplies alongside it, rule 20 a supplied lexical verb together with a supplied object, and rule 33 a supplied preposition together with the supplied noun it stands before. Head such a unit on the word carrying its force under rules 24 and 31, or leave the head null where no word carries it, which rule 5 permits. Do not divide it word by word on this rule's preference for the smallest unit: there is no source–target allocation to test between its parts, so nothing distinguishes them, and what the ledger asks is only that the whole run be partitioned once. Contiguity and office are the conditions, exactly as contiguity is in rules 30 and 55. Where the ASV's own rendered material stands between two runs of supplied English they are two units, each placed by the rule that governs it, as rule 20 makes the dummy and copula of a cleft one unit and the cleft relative standing beyond the predicate another; and where two adjacent supplied words do different offices — a noun supplied for a possessive to stand on beside a connective supplied for the next colon — they are two units on the same terms. `נָצְרוּ דָעַת` → *preserve* ***him that hath*** *knowledge* is one `added` unit headed on *hath*, and `טוֹב־עַיִן הוּא` → *He* ***that hath*** *a bountiful eye* is one likewise, a supplied relative and a supplied verb of having composing between them the single office of making the Hebrew predicate into a relative clause.
3. `aligned` requires nonempty source and target arrays. Both heads must be members of their arrays.
4. `unrepresented` is source-only, with a valid source head, an empty target, and a null target head.
5. `added` is target-only, with an empty source and null source head. Its target head may be null or a member of the target array.
6. Reserve `noncompositional` for a genuinely indivisible phrase. Both heads are required and must belong to their respective arrays. Use the lexical English anchor as target head; where the source phrase has no uniquely defensible internal head, use its first source token as the deterministic display anchor rather than leaving the head null. **But read rules 23 and 30 before reaching for that fallback.** Where the fused expression's single English word writes one morpheme's own lexeme, that morpheme is the uniquely defensible head and the fallback does not reach it: `בְּעֵינֵי פַרְעֹה` → *Pharaoh* heads on the name and `בְעֵינֵי עֲבָדָיו` → *servants* on the noun, exactly as rule 30 heads a fused expression on the morpheme carrying the lexeme and rule 23 heads a preposition fused with its construct noun on the construct. What the fallback governs is a phrase whose one English rendering writes no morpheme's lexeme in particular — an idiom rendered by a word of its own, or a compound of function morphemes — and not one where a name or noun standing in the phrase is the very word the ASV wrote. Where the rendering runs to several English words and more than one morpheme's lexeme is written among them, no single morpheme is uniquely defensible on that ground and the fallback stands, as it does for `יִּתֵּן קֹלוֹ בִּבְכִי` → *he wept aloud*.
7. ASV addition markup is evidence, not an infallible `added` label.
8. Where materially different alignments remain defensible, say which one you took and why in the unit's note. There is no confidence field: it was dropped after `ot-hebrew-verse-v14`, because a note naming the competing reading is what a later reader can act on and a grade was not. Units aligned under an earlier contract still carry one. Where a rule below cites a corpus unit as `medium`, that is a historical reading of how hedged the precedent is — evidence about that unit, never an instruction to record anything.
9. The prepared source projection already applies the chapter's ketiv/qere decisions and their qere fallbacks. Do not make new textual decisions inside the alignment file.
10. This verse is the whole unit of work. Nothing carries to another verse except what a chapter has recorded in its conventions log.

## A source token is a morpheme, not a word

MACULA Hebrew segments below the orthographic word, and that segmentation is
the alignment's granularity. One written word routinely arrives as several
`s###` tokens.

11. Align each morpheme on its own merits. A prefixed conjunction, preposition, article, or relative and a suffixed pronoun are separate source tokens, and each takes the English it licenses. **A present source function morpheme has priority over a supplied-word analysis.** First assign an English function word to any source function morpheme whose sense it carries; only when no source morpheme licenses that word may a noun, verb, construction, or clause take it as a supplied English helper under the rules below.
12. A morpheme may have no letters of its own. When the article assimilates into a pointed preposition, MACULA still supplies it as a token, shown in the briefing as a bracketed form such as `[הַ]`. It is a real token: align it to English *the* when the ASV has one, and mark it `unrepresented` when it does not.
13. Do not merge a morpheme into its host word for convenience. `וְ` + `הָ` + `אָרֶץ` → *And the earth* is three units, not one.

## What each Hebrew form normally does

14. The conjunction `וְ` is normally English *and*, including the waw of a consecutive verb form: the conjunction takes *And* and the verb takes the verb. Where the ASV renders it by some other connective, align it to that word instead. **The list is open and is read off the ASV rather than checked against a set**: the corpus already aligns a waw to *but*, *then*, *so*, *or*, *now*, *also*, *for*, *therefore*, *neither*, *nor*, *yea*, *yet*, *even*, *with*, *moreover* and *howbeit*, among others. A connective the ASV has written for a waw is never `added`, and it is never folded into a neighbouring word to avoid one: a town list writes *with* for the waw of `וְחַצְרֵיהֶן` → *with their villages* and *even* for the waw of `וָיָמָּה` → *even unto the sea*, and each waw takes the word written for it. **Where the ASV renders one connective morpheme by a correlative pair such as *both … and*, *either … or* or *whether … or*, both English words belong to that morpheme's one discontinuous unit, headed on the closing connective (*and* or *or*); the opening correlative is not `added`.** **This governs the disjunctive `אוֹ` exactly as it governs a waw**, since what licenses the pair is the one connective the ASV has rendered by it and not the lemma: `לַזָּכָר אוֹ לַנְּקֵבָה` → ***whether*** *a male* ***or*** *a female* gives *whether … or* to the single `אוֹ`, headed on *or*. Where the Hebrew instead writes a connective of its own for each member — `אוֹ … אוֹ` → *either … or* — each takes only the English word written for it and there is no pair to place. **But that governs only a pair no other morpheme has a claim on.** Where another morpheme standing in the clause is itself what the opening correlative says — a present negative for the *neither* of *neither … nor*, the `מִן` of a `מִן … עַד` range for the *both* of *both … and* — that morpheme takes the opening word and the waw takes only the closing connective, exactly as rule 47 gives a written *the* to a present article before a supplied one and rule 46 gives a *that* to a present relative before a bare participle. `אֲשֶׁר לֹא־יָדְעוּ הֵמָּה וַאֲבוֹתָם` → *whom* ***neither*** *they* ***nor*** *their fathers have known* gives *neither* to `לֹא` and *nor* to the waw, and `מֵעוֹף הַשָּׁמַיִם וְעַד־בְּהֵמָה` → ***both*** *the birds of the heavens* ***and*** *the beasts* gives *both* to `מִן` and *and* to the waw, leaving the `עַד` `unrepresented` under rule 40. **A morpheme that merely stands where the opening correlative stands has no claim on it.** What gives another morpheme that word is that the morpheme is itself what the correlative says, so read sense and not position: the two `לְ`s of `לַזָּכָר אוֹ לַנְּקֵבָה` stand exactly where an English *whether* and a second *for* would stand, and a benefactive preposition is not what *whether* says, so both are `unrepresented` under rule 40 and neither takes the word. Count the correlative's members against the morphemes that could carry them, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s. This does not touch rule 35's fusion, which is a single English word carrying every morpheme's sense: a lone *Nor* or *neither* with no answering *nor* written is that rule's, and only a written pair is this one's. **And a pair is written only where the opening correlative stands before the first member.** Where the ASV instead postposes a floating *both* after the coordinated members, no correlative pair is written and the word is not the waw's: the waw takes the *and* written for it, and the *both* is its own `added` unit under rule 30, for the reason rule 51 gives about a second English word offered to a waw that already holds one. `אֹתוֹ וְאֶת־בְּנוֹ לֹא תִשְׁחֲטוּ בְּיוֹם אֶחָד` → *ye shall not kill it and its young* ***both*** *in one day* leaves its *both* `added`, while the *both* of `מֵעוֹף הַשָּׁמַיִם וְעַד־בְּהֵמָה` above is written where the pair opens and is placed as that sentence directs. Read the English word order, since what tells the two apart is where the ASV has put the opening word and not that it has written one. Read what stands in the clause for that waw in particular, as rule 51 requires for *also*, and where the ASV leaves it unrendered, mark it `unrepresented`. **But read whether a waw is present at all before placing the connective.** Where the ASV writes a coordinating *and*, *or* or *but*, or a resumptive apodosis connective such as *then*, *so* or *therefore*, for a clause or phrase in which no conjunction morpheme stands, no morpheme licenses that word and it is its own `added` unit under rule 30. Hebrew juxtaposes where English coordinates, and the ASV supplies the connective freely: `עַל־כָּתֵף תִשָּׂא בָּעֲלָטָה תוֹצִיא` → *shalt thou bear it upon thy shoulder,* ***and*** *carry it forth in the dark* writes one for a clause the Hebrew leaves asyndetic, and `לְיוֹשְׁבֵי יְרוּשָׁלִַם אֶל־אַדְמַת יִשְׂרָאֵל` → *concerning the inhabitants of Jerusalem* ***and*** *the land of Israel* writes one for a second prepositional phrase the Hebrew merely sets beside the first. Do not fold such a word into the preposition, the verb or the noun standing next to it to avoid the `added`, for the reason rule 42 gives about a surplus *and* and rule 51 about a supplied *also*: the word is written beside those renderings rather than inside one of them, and a preposition whose own relation the ASV has left unstated is `unrepresented` under rule 40 rather than a home for a coordinator. Count the clause's conjunctions against the ASV's connectives, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s, and mark whichever side is in surplus. **The apodosis connective is counted on exactly those terms, and the list of words that can carry it is as open as the connective list above.** Casuistic and temporal law writes its apodosis with a waw as often as without one, and the ASV writes a *then* either way, so read the Hebrew: `וְשָׁמְרוּ בְנֵי־יִשְׂרָאֵל אֶת־מִשְׁמֶרֶת יְהוָה` → ***then*** *the children of Israel kept the charge of Jehovah* gives *then* to the waw written for it, while `עַל־פִּי יְהוָה יַחֲנוּ` → ***then*** *according to the commandment of Jehovah they remained encamped* leaves the same word `added`, because that apodosis is juxtaposed with no conjunction in it at all. Do not give such a *then* to the preposition, the verb or the temporal phrase it stands beside, and do not read a waw the ASV has left unrendered as its home either: where the apodosis writes a waw and the ASV opens the clause bare, that waw is `unrepresented` under this rule's earlier sentence. Read the clause before counting, since a morpheme that has an English word of its own is not made a surplus by this sentence: a present negative carries a *nor* or a *neither* under rule 35, a `כִּי` or an `אִם` carries a *but* or an *or* the ASV has written for it, and only a connective no morpheme answers to is `added`. **And a coordinator standing inside one morpheme's own English rendering is not a connective supplied for the clause at all.** Where the ASV renders a single source morpheme by a fixed English phrase that contains an *and* or an *or*, the word coordinates the parts of that phrase and not two source items, so it belongs to that morpheme's unit, is neither `added` nor a separate connective, and does not move the unit's head, which rule 44 leaves on the lexical verb because a phrasal particle names nothing on its own. Decide it by asking what stands on either side of the English word in the Hebrew, which is the subtraction test rules 16, 31 and 43 use and the one rule 30 applies to its one-English-word condition. Where a source clause or phrase stands on each side, the preceding sentences govern and a connective no waw answers to is `added`; where the same one morpheme's rendering runs on both sides and no source item is coordinated at all, the word is written inside that rendering rather than beside it. `וַיִּתְהַלֵּךְ בְּתוֹךְ אֲרָיוֹ` → *And he went up and down among the lions* keeps *up and down* whole with the one verb it renders, while `אָבְדָה תִקְוָתָהּ` → ***and*** *her hope was lost* leaves its supplied *and* `added`, because there a written clause stands on either side of it. **And where the ASV writes two connective-force words together at a clause head, read which morpheme's own sense each carries before placing either.** Hebrew opens a clause with a waw and a discourse adverb standing side by side — `וְעַתָּה` is the commonest — and the ASV answers the pair with two English words, as *And now*, *Now therefore* or *Wherefore now*. Two English words answer to two morphemes and neither is a surplus, so the adverb takes the word that writes its own lexeme and the waw takes the connective left over, exactly as this rule's correlative sentences give the opening word to a morpheme that is itself what that word says and rule 11 gives a function word first to the morpheme whose sense it carries. `וְעַתָּה הִנֵּה` → ***And now*** *behold* gives *And* to the waw and *now* to `עַתָּה`, and `וְעַתָּה תְּנָה־לִּי` → ***Now therefore*** *give me* gives *Now* to `עַתָּה` and *therefore* to the waw, though the waw's word there stands second. Position is not what decides it, for the reason rule 51 gives about a postposed *also*: the unit is reordered under rule 25 rather than broken, and one chapter may write both shapes, as JOS.14.10 and JOS.14.12 do. Where the ASV writes only one word for such a pair, count as this rule already directs and mark `unrepresented` whichever morpheme it leaves unrendered.
15. The article `הַ` aligns to English *the*. Hebrew has no indefinite article, so an English *a* or *an* belongs to the noun that licenses it and is not `added`. **The same ownership applies to a definite article the ASV supplies where no Hebrew article morpheme is present:** it belongs to the nominal expression it determines and is not `added`. **Read that as the noun of the expression, not as its leftmost word.** Where the expression writes both a numeral and the noun it counts, the supplied determiner joins that noun's unit and the numeral takes only its own word: `בַּחֲמִשָּׁה עָשָׂר יוֹם` → *the fifteenth day* gives *the day* to `יוֹם` and *fifteenth* to the numeral, and `לִשְׁלֹשָׁה עָשָׂר פָּרִים` → *of the thirteen bullocks* and `לְאַרְבָּעָה עָשָׂר כְּבָשִׂים` → *of the fourteen lambs* give *the bullocks* and *the lambs* to their nouns. A numeral takes such a determiner only where the Hebrew writes no noun for it to determine, which is rule 31's substantival numeral — `בְּאֶחָד לַחֹדֶשׁ` → *on* ***the first day*** *of the month*. And this reaches a supplied determiner only: where an article morpheme stands in the phrase it takes the written *the* first, under rules 29 and 47, and where the ASV writes a distributive *each* or *every* the numeral takes that word under rule 41. **And it applies to a supplied quantifying determiner such as *any*, which is the word English writes in that same slot where *a* will not stand — under a negation, in a question, or after a comparative.** Where a bare noun carries no determiner morpheme of its own and the ASV writes an *any* before it, that word determines the nominal expression exactly as a supplied *a* or *the* does: it joins the noun's unit, is not `added`, and is never the head, which stays on the word carrying the lexeme under rule 31 — `לִמְלָאכָה` → *for any work* gives *for* to the preposition and *any work* to the noun, headed on *work*. **But only where no morpheme in the clause licenses that determiner.** A `כֹּל` standing on the noun is itself what the *any* renders and takes it, the noun keeping only its own word — `כָּל־עֵץ` → *any tree*, `כָּל־כְּלִי` → *any vessel* — and where the ASV recasts a clause's negation onto a nominal standing in it, the negative takes the determiner that negation has become. **Read which nominal the ASV has put the determiner on rather than what its grammatical role is**: an object takes it in `לֹא יֵעָשֶׂה לִמְלָאכָה` → *it was meet for* ***no*** *work*, which gives *no* to `לֹא`, discontinuous from the verb it negates under rule 25, and leaves `מְלָאכָה` the bare *work*; a subject takes it in `אַל־תָּבֹא רְנָנָה בוֹ` → *Let* ***no*** *joyful voice come therein*, which gives *no* to `אַל` and leaves the noun its own *joyful voice*. A jussive or prohibitive `אַל` is read on the same terms as `לֹא`. Count the determiners and read which morpheme each answers to, as rule 41 counts a phrase's determiners and rule 29 counts its *the*s. This does not reach a supplied possessive, which rule 18 leaves `added` where no suffix or other possessive morpheme is present. **A supplied determiner that distinguishes one member of a class from another is read on exactly those terms.** Where the ASV writes a correlative *one … another*, or another contrastive determiner standing where an *a* or a *the* would stand, before a noun that carries no determiner morpheme of its own, that word determines the nominal expression as a supplied *a* or *any* does: it joins the noun's unit, is not `added`, and is never the head. `מִמַּטֶּה לְמַטֶּה אַחֵר` → *from* ***one*** *tribe to another tribe* gives *one tribe* to the first `מַטֶּה`, headed on *tribe*, while `אַחֵר` keeps the *another* written for it. Do not read such a word as evidence that a numeral stands in the phrase, and do not mark it `added` under rule 30 because it is not one of the determiners named above: what decides it is that no morpheme in the phrase licenses the word, which is the count the preceding sentences run. But run that count first, since a numeral standing in the phrase is itself what such a word renders and takes it under rule 31 — `לְאֶחָד מִמִּשְׁפַּחַת` → *unto* ***one*** *of the family* — and one chapter may write both shapes, as NUM.36.8 and NUM.36.9 do. **But a quantifier the ASV writes before a determiner of its own is standing in no determiner's slot at all.** Every supplied determiner above stands where an *a* or a *the* would stand before the noun's own word. Where the ASV instead writes a partitive expression — a quantifier and an *of* before a nominal that already carries a written or a supplied *the* of its own, as *any of the fugitives* or *one of the cities* — the quantifier heads a phrase of its own and determines nothing, so the sentences above do not reach it: where no morpheme in the clause licenses the expression, the quantifier and its *of* stand together and do one office, and they are one `added` unit under rule 2, headed on the quantifier. Read which determiner stands immediately before the noun's own word, as rule 29 counts the ASV's *the*s; a noun holding a *the* of its own is not a noun the quantifier determines, and an *of* the ASV writes between the two nouns of a construct chain is the rectum's under rule 19 whatever partitive stands before the chain. But run that count first as well, since a morpheme standing in the clause takes the expression before this sentence reaches it: a partitive `מִן` takes *any of* whole under rule 19, a `כֹּל` standing on the noun takes the *any* under this rule's earlier sentences, and a numeral standing in the phrase takes the *one* under rule 31, as `לְאֶחָד מִמִּשְׁפַּחַת` → *unto* ***one*** *of the family* does above. Where nothing does, `פְּלִיטֵי אֶפְרַיִם` → ***any of*** *the fugitives of Ephraim* and `בְּעָרֵי גִלְעָד` → *in* ***one of*** *the cities of Gilead* leave both supplied words `added`, the construct noun keeping the supplied *the* rule 29 gives it and the rectum its own *of*. An ASV italic on such a pair corroborates it and decides nothing, by rule 7. **For Aramaic, a separately segmented determined-state ending (`Td`) is treated likewise:** align it to *the* when the ASV writes a corresponding article, and mark it `unrepresented` under rule 29 when the ASV writes no separate article, including where another determiner supplies the determination. **A determiner that directly realizes another morpheme is not a corresponding article.** In the temporal relative `עִדָּנָא דִּי` → *what time*, *what* realizes the written relative `דִּי`, while the determined-state ending is `unrepresented`; the relative morpheme takes the word before the nominal determination does, exactly as a present `כֹּל` or negative takes its determiner above. More specific construct and substantival rules still decide which source unit inside that expression takes it.
16. The definite direct-object marker `אֵת` (H853) has no English realization of its own and is normally its own `unrepresented` unit; that is the ruling on the large majority of its occurrences. It takes English only where the ASV spends a word on the object relation itself and no other morpheme licenses that word. Five families qualify. An English verb may reach its object through a preposition — *hearken unto*, *spake unto*, *inquire of*, *fight against*, *send for* — where that preposition exists only to attach the object to the verb; it is the marker's rendering, not the object noun's, and the marker heads on it. An appositional or resumptive marker may be rendered *even* or *to wit*. **But only a marker standing on the very phrase that English word introduces.** Where the ASV writes an appositional *even* or *to wit* before a phrase no marker marks, no morpheme licenses it and it is its own `added` unit under rule 30, whatever markers stand elsewhere in the clause: `וְחַקּוֹתָ עָלֶיהָ עִיר אֵת־יְרוּשָׁלִָם` → *portray upon it a city,* ***even*** *Jerusalem* gives *even* to the marker, which stands on the appositive itself, while `נָתַתִּי לְךָ אֵת־שְׁנֵי עֲוֺנָם לְמִסְפַר יָמִים שְׁלֹשׁ־מֵאוֹת וְתִשְׁעִים יוֹם` → *a number of days,* ***even*** *three hundred and ninety days* leaves the *even* `added`, because the clause's one marker stands on `שְׁנֵי עֲוֺנָם`, which the ASV renders bare. Do not reach across the clause for a marker to carry such a word, for the reason rule 42 gives about a surplus *and*: the word is written beside those renderings rather than inside one of them. **But an *even* the ASV writes inside another morpheme's own multi-word rendering is not an appositional *even* at all, and the preceding sentence does not reach it.** Where the ASV expands an extent or limit preposition as *even unto*, *even to*, *even till* or *even for*, the whole expression is that preposition's rendering and belongs to its unit, headed by rule 24 on the word carrying the relation — *unto*, *to*, *till*, *for* — and the *even* is neither `added` nor any marker's, whatever markers stand elsewhere in the clause: `וַיִּירַשׁ אֶת־אַרְצוֹ מֵאַרְנֹן עַד־יַבֹּק עַד־בְנֵי עַמּוֹן` → *from the Arnon unto the Jabbok* ***even unto*** *the children of Ammon* gives *even unto* to the second `עַד`, which is the morpheme the two words are written for. Read what the English word stands before, which is this rule's subtraction test again: an appositional *even* stands before a nominal that renames what precedes it, and this one stands before the object of the very preposition written beside it, so dropping it diminishes that preposition's rendering and nothing else. Two limits. Where a connective morpheme stands in the clause and the ASV writes a bare *even* for it, rule 14 governs and the word is the connective's; the corpus aligns a waw to *even* in 179 units and to *even unto* in none, so read which of the two the ASV has written. And where the ASV leaves the preposition's own relation unstated, rule 40 governs as before and there is no expression to place. The corpus aligns a preposition to *even unto* in 33 units and `עַד` to *even to* in 28 more, always headed on the second word. **Read that first limit as a count of the ASV's words against the morphemes standing in the phrase, and not as a test of whether the word *even* stands alone on the page.** Where a conjunction morpheme stands immediately before the limit preposition and the ASV writes *even unto*, *even to*, *even till* or *even for* at the head of that phrase, two English words answer to two morphemes and neither is a surplus: the waw takes the *even*, exactly as rule 14 gives the *even* of `וָיָמָּה` → *even unto the sea* to the waw written there, and the preposition takes the relational word alone, exactly as it does when the same phrase is written *and unto*. `וְעַד־בַּעַל גָּד` → ***even unto*** *Baal gad* divides that way, and it divides identically to the `וְעַד` of `וְעַד מִשְׂרְפוֹת מַיִם` → ***and unto*** *Misrephoth maim* standing in the same chapter, the waw taking whichever connective the ASV wrote for it and the `עַד` taking *unto* in either case. Count the phrase's connective morphemes against the ASV's connectives and read which pair of members each stands between, as rule 26 counts a `מִן … עַד` range's connectives and rule 42 counts waws against *and*s; a waw beside such an expansion takes the bare *even* and never the pair, which is what the corpus's count of no waw aligned to *even unto* records. The whole-expression reading of the preceding sentences governs where no connective morpheme stands beside the preposition, which is what `עַד־יַבֹּק עַד־בְנֵי עַמּוֹן` above is, and it governs a comparative or other preposition expanded the same way with no conjunction in the phrase: `כַּחוֹל` → ***even as*** *the sand* keeps both words with the `כְ`. So read whether a conjunction is present before placing the *even*, exactly as rule 14's own closing sentences direct for a coordinator and rule 51 for an *also*. And a marker on an object the ASV fronts before its clause and resumes inside it with a pronoun may be rendered *as for*: `וְאֶת־הַיְבוּסִי יוֹשְׁבֵי יְרוּשָׁלִַם לֹא־יָכְלוּ בְנֵי־יְהוּדָה לְהוֹרִישָׁם` → *And* ***as for*** *the Jebusites the inhabitants of Jerusalem, the children of Judah could not drive them out* gives *as for* to the marker, headed on *as* by rule 24, since the resuming *them* is the suffix's under rule 18 and nothing else answers to the two words. **The same limit governs that third family.** Where the ASV writes *as for* before a fronted phrase no marker stands on, no morpheme licenses the expression and it is its own `added` unit under rule 30, headed on *as*: `וּמַרְאֵיהֶם דְּמוּת אֶחָד לְאַרְבַּעְתָם` → *And* ***as for*** *their appearance, they four had one likeness* leaves the *as for* `added`, because `מַרְאֵיהֶם` is a bare noun and suffix with no marker and no preposition on it. Do not fold the expression into the fronted noun to avoid the `added`, for the reason just given about a surplus *even*, and do not reach for rule 33: that rule gives a supplied function word to a noun whose own case-function licenses it, and a topicalized nominal's fronting is not such a case-function. Where a morpheme does stand on the fronted phrase it takes the expression, whether that morpheme is a marker as above or a fronting `לְ` — `לָאוֹפַנִּים לָהֶם קוֹרָא` → ***As for*** *the wheels, they were called* gives *As for* to the `לְ` under rule 11. **And a marker on a direct object the ASV demotes to a role phrase takes the preposition written for it.** Where the ASV recasts the marked object as the role the thing serves and writes a supplied *for* or *as* before it, promoting the object's appositive to the English verb's object — `וְהֵבִיא אֵת־קָרְבָּנוֹ שְׂעִיר עִזִּים` → *he shall bring* ***for*** *his oblation a goat* — that word is the marker's and it heads on it, since the marked noun keeps its own English and nothing else in the clause answers for the preposition. Rule 33 is what governs the same clause written without a marker, and it says so in its own terms: a nominal with no preposition and no object marker on it takes the supplied word itself, as `וְהֵבִיא קָרְבָּנוֹ` → ***for*** *his oblation* and `יָבִיא קָרְבָּנוֹ` → ***as*** *his oblation* do. So read whether a marker stands on the noun before placing the word, and do not leave it `added`: one of the two morphemes always licenses it. **And a marker standing on a nominal the ASV construes as an adverbial adjunct takes the preposition written for that adjunct on those same terms.** Where the marked phrase is rendered not as the English verb's object but as an adjunct of place, time or manner, and the ASV writes a supplied preposition before it to state what the phrase contributes to its clause, that word answers to the marked phrase's relation and no other morpheme in the phrase carries it: the marker takes it and heads on it, while the noun keeps its own English. `וְשָׁפַט אֶת־יִשְׂרָאֵל אֵת כָּל־הַמְּקוֹמוֹת הָאֵלֶּה` → *and he judged Israel* ***in*** *all those places* gives *in* to the second marker, the first marker standing on the verb's own object and going `unrepresented` as usual. Read the condition and not the lemma of the preposition, exactly as rule 40 directs: *in*, *on*, *at*, *after* and *according to* are read alike, and the corpus aligns an object marker to *according to* at HAG.2.5 and JDG.11.39 and to *at*, *about*, *after*, *on* and *upon* elsewhere. Two limits. A preposition the English verb's valency needs to reach its own object is the first family's and not this one, so ask whether the marked phrase is what the English verb reaches before placing the word. And where the ASV renders the marked adjunct bare, nothing is spent on the relation and the marker is `unrepresented` as usual. Test by subtraction: drop the English word and ask which morpheme has lost its rendering. If nothing but the object relation is diminished, the marker takes the word; if the ASV writes the object bare — *he built the house* — the marker is `unrepresented`. Do not confuse H853 with its homograph the preposition `אֵת` *with* (H854), which is an ordinary preposition aligning by rule 11.
17. An inflected verb may align to an English subject pronoun, auxiliary, and main verb together. Choose the lexical English verb as the target head. **This permission is specific to a pronoun realizing the verb's person and number.** A lexical English subject noun the ASV supplies where no source nominal is present answers to no morpheme and is its own `added` unit: `יָשְׁבוּ בָהּ` → *men shall dwell therein* gives *shall dwell* to the verb and leaves *men* `added`. **A volitive English frame belongs to the verb whose mood it renders.** Where the ASV expands an imperative or jussive with *Oh* or *Oh that ... would*, the framing words join the verb's unit and the unit heads on its lexical predicate: `הוֹדוּ` → *Oh give thanks* heads on *thanks* under rule 44, and `יוֹדוּ` → *Oh that ... would praise* heads on *praise*. **And the frame need not open with *Oh*.** Where the ASV writes the fixed English acclamation *Long live* for a jussive of `חָיָה`, the *Long* is what English spends on that jussive's optative force and joins the verb's unit on the same terms, headed on *live*: `יְחִי הַמֶּלֶךְ` → ***Long live*** *the king*. An ASV italic on the *Long* corroborates the supply and decides nothing, by rule 7; the corpus reads `יְחִי` → *Long live* in four units across four chapters, always headed on *live*, and marks the *Long* `added` in none. A lexical subject noun supplied inside such a frame is still `added` under the preceding sentence — *Oh that men would praise* leaves *men* outside the verb's discontinuous unit. This applies only where no optative particle or multi-source optative idiom stands in the Hebrew; a present `לוּ` or `מִי יִתֵּן` takes its own fixed English formula under rule 30. **A presentative dummy *there* is not such a lexical subject.** Where the ASV recasts a finite verb or participle with a presentative frame — `יֵּצֵא` → *there came forth*, `נִשֵּׂאת` → *there was lifted up* — the dummy belongs to the verb's unit, by the same constructional reasoning that puts it on `הָיָה` under rule 21, and the unit still heads on the lexical verb. When the verse also has an explicit subject noun, that noun's tokens are its own unit and only a genuinely pleonastic pronoun stays with the verb. **Where the ASV recasts a waw plus an explicit subject pronoun as a relative clause, the English *who* or *which* realizes the pronoun; the waw is `unrepresented` if the ASV writes no separate connective.** Do not give the relative pronoun to the waw merely because it opens the recast clause: `וְהוּא אַחֲרָיו` → *which was behind him* gives *which* to `הוּא` and leaves the waw unrepresented, and `וַאֲנִי זָקַנְתִּי` → *who am old* gives *who* to `אֲנִי` on the same terms. **But read whether the relative is what realizes that pronoun before applying that sentence.** What it governs is a recasting in which the pronoun itself becomes the relative, which is why the ASV writes no other word for it and the relative's referent is the pronoun's own. Where instead the ASV writes the pronoun's own English word beside the relative, and the relative points at something else the clause speaks of, the pronoun is holding a rendering of its own and is not a home for a second one, as rule 51 says of a waw, while a relative pointing at another antecedent realizes nothing of it and would head on a word that does not write its lexeme, against rule 24: the pronoun takes the word written for it, the finite verb keeps its auxiliaries and lexical verb, and the waw is the morpheme licensing the relative connective, exactly as it is where no pronoun stands at all. `וְאַתְּ אָלִית` → *about* ***which*** ***thou*** *didst utter a curse* gives *thou* to `אַתְּ`, *which* to the waw and *didst utter a curse* to the verb, while the supplied *about*, whose relation no morpheme carries, is its own `added` unit under rule 30, since no bare nominal stands for rule 33 to place it on. Read whom the relative refers to and count the clause's English words against the morphemes that could carry them, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s; one verse may write both shapes, and JDG.17.2 does, aligning `אֲנִי לָקַחְתִּי` → *I took* with the pronoun holding its own *I*. **A question auxiliary the ASV fronts belongs to the interrogative particle and not to the verb.** English marks a yes-or-no question by fronting an auxiliary, and where `הֲ` stands in the clause that word is what the ASV has written for it: `הֲלוֹא לֶאֱמוּנָה` → *do not thine eyes look upon truth* gives *do* to `הֲ` in a clause that has no verb at all to take it, and `הַעַל אֵלֶּה לֹא אֶפְקֹד` → *Shall I not visit* gives *Shall* to `הֲ` and *I visit* to the verb, even though that auxiliary also carries the imperfect's future. The `אִם` opening a following disjunctive question is read the same way, since it is what marks that clause as the second question: `אִם לֹא תָחִילוּ` → *will ye not tremble* gives *will* to `אִם` and *ye tremble* to the verb. **But a particle for which the ASV has written a connective of its own is not a home for the auxiliary as well.** Where the ASV renders that `אִם`, or another particle standing at the head of the second question, by a written *Or*, *Or if* or another connective, that word is the particle's under rule 14, the particle takes no second English word, and the fronted auxiliary is the ordinary one this rule's first sentence gives the verb: `אִם יְדַבֵּר אֵלֶיךָ רַכּוֹת` → ***Or*** *will he speak soft words unto thee* gives *Or* to `אִם` and *will he speak* to the verb. This is the reasoning of this rule's second limit below, where an interrogative holding an English word of its own leaves the auxiliary to the verb, and of rule 51's ruling that a waw holding a connective of its own is not a home for a second word. Count the clause's English function words against the morphemes that could carry them, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s; where the ASV writes no connective for the particle, the preceding sentence governs as before. Where the ASV marks the question by inversion alone and writes no auxiliary, the particle is `unrepresented`: `הַאוֹתִי לֹא תִירָאוּ` → *Fear ye not me*. Two limits. Where no interrogative morpheme is present at all, a fronted *do* is the verb's own under this rule's first sentence: `לֹא שָׁפָטוּ` → *do they not judge*. And this governs the yes-or-no particle only: an interrogative word that has an English word of its own — `מָה` → *what*, `אֵי` → *How* — leaves the fronted auxiliary to the verb, as `אֵי לָזֹאת אֶסְלַח לָךְ` → *How can I pardon thee* leaves *can I pardon* whole. **And a particle heading an indirect question takes the subordinating word the ASV writes for it, which puts it in that same position.** Where `הֲ` introduces a question embedded under a verb of testing, seeing, knowing or asking and the ASV writes *whether* or *if* at the head of the embedded clause, that word is what the particle is rendered by: the particle takes it and heads on it, and a modal or auxiliary the ASV fronts inside that clause belongs to the predicate under this rule's first sentence. `לְמַעַן נַסּוֹת בָּם אֶת־יִשְׂרָאֵל הֲשֹׁמְרִים הֵם אֶת־דֶּרֶךְ יְהוָה` → *that I may prove Israel,* ***whether*** *they* ***will keep*** *the way of Jehovah* gives *whether* to `הֲ` and *will keep* to the participial predicate, while the explicit pronoun keeps only its own *they* under rule 20. This is the count the paragraph above runs, read for the first question rather than the second: a particle already holding an English word of its own is not a home for a second one, as rule 51 says of a waw, and giving it both would leave the predicate's auxiliary unaccounted against rule 2. The corpus aligns an interrogative `הַ` to *whether* in six units. Read what the ASV has written before reaching for this sentence: where it marks the embedded question by the fronted auxiliary alone and writes no subordinator, the particle takes that auxiliary as in a direct question, and where it writes neither, the particle is `unrepresented` as above. **An English matrix duration frame is not an auxiliary of the finite verb inside the temporal clause it introduces.** Where `עַד` plus a temporal interrogative and `לֹא` plus a finite verb is rendered *how long will it be ere ...*, the temporal-interrogative expression takes *how long will it be* as one `noncompositional` unit, headed on *long*; the negative takes *ere*; and the finite verb takes only its subordinate subject and predicate. `עַד מָתַי לֹא יוּכְלוּ` → *how long will it be ere they attain* and `עַד אָנָה לֹא תִשְׁקֹטִי` → *how long will it be ere thou be quiet* follow that division. The dummy *it* is not the finite verb's person and number, and assigning the matrix frame to that verb would give it two English clauses. This does not make a simple *how long ...* indivisible where the ASV writes no separate matrix frame; there each morpheme is aligned on its own merits under rule 2. **Where the ASV recasts a waw plus a finite verb as a relative clause and no explicit subject pronoun in that clause is realized by that relative, the relative *who* or *which* is the connective the ASV has written for the waw and belongs to the waw; the verb keeps its subject, auxiliaries and lexical verb.** `וַתֹּאכַל` → *which hath devoured* gives *which* to the waw and *hath devoured* to the verb. This is the other side of the explicit-pronoun case above: a pronoun the relative realizes takes it before the waw does, but a pronoun standing in the clause under an English word of its own is not such a pronoun, and without one the waw is the morpheme licensing the relative connective. Where no waw or other conjunction stands either, rule 55 governs the supplied relative instead. The finished corpus aligns a waw directly to *which* in ten units, so this is settled rather than a tie to hedge.

**An English matrix duration frame is not an auxiliary of the finite verb inside the temporal clause it introduces.** Where `עַד` plus a temporal interrogative and `לֹא` plus a finite verb is rendered *how long will it be ere ...*, the temporal-interrogative expression takes *how long will it be* as one `noncompositional` unit, headed on *long*; the negative takes *ere*; and the finite verb takes only its subordinate subject and predicate. `עַד מָתַי לֹא יוּכְלוּ` → *how long will it be ere they attain* and `עַד אָנָה לֹא תִשְׁקֹטִי` → *how long will it be ere thou be quiet* follow that division. The dummy *it* is not the finite verb's person and number, and assigning the matrix frame to that verb would give it two English clauses. This does not make a simple *how long ...* indivisible where the ASV writes no separate matrix frame; there each morpheme is aligned on its own merits under rule 2.

**Where the ASV recasts a waw plus a finite verb as a relative clause and no explicit subject pronoun stands in that clause, the relative *who* or *which* is the connective the ASV has written for the waw and belongs to the waw; the verb keeps its subject, auxiliaries and lexical verb.** `וַתֹּאכַל` → *which hath devoured* gives *which* to the waw and *hath devoured* to the verb. This is the other side of the explicit-pronoun case above: a present pronoun takes the relative before the waw does, but without one the waw is the morpheme licensing the relative connective. Where no waw or other conjunction stands either, rule 55 governs the supplied relative instead. The finished corpus aligns a waw directly to *which* in ten units, so this is settled rather than a tie to hedge.
18. A pronominal suffix aligns to the English pronoun that realizes it, which is usually a possessive or object pronoun: `מִינֵהוּ` → *his kind* splits into the noun and *his*. **Where the ASV's paraphrase realizes one suffix twice, both pronouns belong to that suffix's one possibly discontinuous unit; an earlier realization does not make the later one `added`.** `מִכֶּם מָזוֹר` → *cure you of your wound* gives *you ... your* to the suffix and heads on *you*, the direct pronoun rather than the possessive recasting. **Where the ASV intensifies a possessive with *own*, the whole emphatic possessive belongs to the suffix:** `דְּרָכֶיךָ` → *thine own ways* gives *thine own* to the suffix and *ways* to the noun, headed on *thine*; do not attach *own* to the host noun merely because it stands beside it. **Where the ASV replaces the suffix's ordinary pronoun with a lexical nominal expression naming its referent, that whole expression realizes the suffix and the supplied lexical antecedent is not `added`; head on the noun or name that carries the referent.** `נְחָלֶיהָ` → *the streams of Edom* gives *of Edom* to the suffix and heads on *Edom*, and the suffix in `בִּסְעִיפֶיהָ הַפֹּרִיָּה` → *in the outmost branches of a fruitful tree* takes *of a tree*, headed on *tree*, while the adjective keeps *fruitful*. **An independent personal pronoun the ASV renders the same way goes with it.** Where a written subject or object pronoun is realized not by an English pronoun but by a demonstrative with a supplied lexical noun naming its referent, that whole expression realizes the pronoun and the supplied noun is not `added`; head on the noun carrying the referent: `הִיא הַסִּיר` → ***this city*** *is the caldron* gives *this city* to `הִיא`, exactly as rule 20's identifying formula gives *These ... that* to a `הוּא`, and an ASV italic on the supplied noun corroborates it without deciding it, by rule 7. Where the referent's own noun stands written in the clause, the pronoun takes only the determiner the ASV writes for it and the article is `unrepresented` under rule 41's limit: `הִיא נִתְּנָה הָאָרֶץ` → ***this*** *land* *is given*. **Where the ASV expands an object suffix into a full nominal expression containing a pronoun, the whole expression may realize the suffix, but the target head remains the pronoun that directly realizes the suffix's lexeme:** `אֲשִׁיבֶנּוּ` → *I will turn away the punishment thereof* gives *the punishment thereof* to the suffix and heads on *thereof*, not on the generic noun *punishment*. All eight occurrences of this prophetic formula across Amos 1–2 follow that analysis. **An English relative pronoun may be what realizes a suffix.** Where the ASV recasts the suffix's own clause as a relative clause modifying the noun its referent names, the relative — *that*, *which*, *who*, *whom* — is the pronoun the suffix is rendered by and joins its unit, exactly as rule 17 gives the *which* of such a recasting to an explicit subject pronoun, and whatever preposition the suffix stands on keeps only what the recasting leaves it under rules 40, 43 and 49: `שָׂרִים זָהָב לָהֶם` → *princes* ***that*** *had gold* gives *had* to the possessive `לְ` under rule 43 and *that* to the suffix, and `וַיָּסֶךְ אֱלוֹהַּ בַּעֲדוֹ` → *And* ***whom*** *God hath hedged in* gives *whom* to the suffix and leaves `בַּעַד` `unrepresented` under rule 40. Such a relative is not a subordinator supplied for the clause: rules 46 and 55 govern a clause with no morpheme in it to answer for the word, and here the suffix answers for it, so read whether a pronominal morpheme stands in the clause before marking a relative `added`. **And where the ASV supplies the antecedent noun as well as the relative, the two sentences above are one ruling and not two.** Where the suffix's clause is recast as a relative clause and the ASV writes a lexical noun naming the suffix's referent for that relative to modify, the noun and the relative together realize the suffix in one possibly discontinuous unit and neither word is `added`: `עָלֵימוֹ אָבַד כָּלַח` → ***Men*** *in* ***whom*** *ripe age is perished* gives *Men ... whom* to the suffix, discontinuous under rule 25 around the `עַל` that keeps its own *in*, and `לֹא עֹזֵר לָמוֹ` → ***men that*** *have no helper* gives *men that* to it while the possessive `לְ` keeps the *have* rule 43 gives it. Rule 17's ruling that a supplied lexical subject noun is `added` does not reach such a noun: that ruling governs a noun supplied as the subject of a finite verb, and this one is the antecedent of a relative and answers to a suffix standing in the clause, so read what the supplied noun does before marking it. Head on the noun that carries the referent, as the sentences above head `הִיא הַסִּיר` → *this city* on *city* and `נְחָלֶיהָ` → *the streams of Edom* on *Edom*; the sentence heading an expanded object suffix on its pronoun governs a generic noun standing beside a pronoun that realizes the suffix directly — *the punishment thereof* — and a relative linking a recast clause to the noun that names the referent is not that. The competing head is the relative, so name it in the note under rule 8. Where no suffix or other possessive morpheme is present, a supplied possessive expression such as *your own* or *thine own* answers to no source morpheme and is `added` under rule 30. **The suffix on an infinitive construct is that infinitive's subject and takes the English subject pronoun the ASV writes for it**, while the infinitive keeps the verb: `עַד כַּלֹּתוֹ אֹתְךָ` → *until he have consumed thee* gives *he* to the suffix and *have consumed* to the infinitive, and the object marker and its own suffix are read separately by rules 16 and 11. The ASV's pronoun need not agree in number with the suffix — `עַד הַאֲבִידוֹ אֹתָךְ` → *until they have caused thee to perish* — because the English subject answers to the referent the translation has in view; align the pronoun that is written. This governs only a suffix that is actually present: where none is, a subject pronoun the ASV supplies for an infinitive belongs to the infinitive itself under rules 48 and 52, and rules 40 and 43 dispose of the other places where the English pronoun realizing a morpheme is neither possessive nor object.
19. In a construct chain, the English relational expression belongs to the nomen rectum, headed on that noun. This is normally *of*, handled exactly as a Greek genitive `of + noun`, but where the ASV recasts the construct relation with *from*, *for*, *in*, *to*, *over*, or another preposition, that expression belongs there too. The construct state of the first noun is what signals the relation, but the first noun's unit takes only its own English word. **The rectum may be a pronominal suffix rather than a noun, and nothing changes.** Where the ASV renders such a suffix not with a possessive but with *of* and a pronoun — `כֻּלָּם` → *all of them*, `מַרְבִּיתָם` → *the greatest part of them* — the whole *of + pronoun* is the suffix's one unit, headed on the pronoun under rule 24, and the construct noun keeps only its own word: `כֹּל` takes *all*, never *all of*. Do not read that *of* as part of an English quantifier or as licensed by the construct noun's own state, for the reason this rule's third sentence gives. The corpus aligns a suffix to *of them* headed on *them* in 116 units and records no `כֹּל` → *all of* treatment, and where the construct noun's own English is something else the division shows in a single unit: `כֻּלָּם` → *five of them in all* gives *in all* to `כֹּל` and *of them* to the suffix. Where the ASV writes a possessive or a bare pronoun instead, rule 18 governs as before and there is no *of* to place. **But the word the construct holds must be one the ASV has written as a noun, and a participle or infinitive it has written as an English verb is not one.** Where the ASV renders the governing word by an English verb phrase — a light verb with its complement under rule 44, or any verb reaching its own object — and writes the *of*, *unto* or other preposition that English verb needs to reach the suffix, the word answers to the verb's valency and not to a relation between two nominals: it joins the verb's unit under rule 44's supplied-preposition clause, headed where that rule heads it, and the suffix keeps only the pronoun rule 18 writes for it. `לְהַכִּירֵנִי` → *that thou shouldest take knowledge* ***of*** *me* and `מַכִּירֵךְ` → *he that did take knowledge* ***of*** *thee* divide alike, though the first is an infinitive construct and the second a participle in construct state, because the ASV wrote one idiom for both and the Hebrew wrote a bare object suffix in both. Decide it by the subtraction test this rule already runs: drop the English preposition and ask whether the relation between two nominals has gone, as it has in *all of them*, or whether an English verb has been left unable to reach its object, as it is in *take knowledge thee*. Rule 44 states the same boundary from its own side, and rule 33 states it again in deferring only to a governing verb the ASV has itself rendered as an English verb; what the morpheme is tagged is no part of the condition, and what the ASV made of it is the whole of it. **A quantifying construct noun with a nominal rectum is read on exactly those terms.** Where `כֹּל`, `אֶחָד` or another quantifier stands in construct on a noun and the ASV writes an *of* between the two, that *of* is the chain's own relation and belongs to the rectum, the quantifier keeping only the word written for it: `אַחַד שְׁעָרֶיךָ` → *any* … *of thy gates* and `כָּל־עֵץ` → *any kind of tree* give *of gates* and *of tree* to their recta. **A generic noun the ASV supplies to expand the quantifier does not move that *of*.** Such a noun joins the quantifier's own unit under rule 31, which heads it on the word carrying the lexeme, so `כֹּל` takes *any kind* headed on *any*, exactly as it takes a bare *any* under rule 15 where the ASV supplies nothing. Do not read *all of*, *any of* or *any kind of* as one indivisible English quantifier: the *of* stands between the two nouns the Hebrew writes, and the subtraction test rules 16 and 31 use finds the relation between them gone when it is dropped. A partitive `מִן` before the noun is not this case, since no construct relation stands there and no morpheme but the preposition licenses either word: `מִן־הַבָּשָׂר` → *any of the flesh* is one unit on the `מִן`, headed on *any* under rule 24. **But a preposition that renders the construct noun's own lexeme is that noun's rendering and not the relation's.** Where the first noun is itself a directional or relational word and the ASV spends its one English word on that lexeme instead of writing a noun for it — `דֶּרֶךְ הַצָּפוֹן` → *toward the north*, `דֶּרֶךְ הַקָּדִים` → *toward the east* — the preposition belongs to the construct noun and heads its unit, while the rectum takes only its own word and the rectum's article takes the written *the* under rule 29. Decide it by the subtraction test rules 16, 31 and 43 use: drop the English preposition and ask whether the construct noun has lost its whole rendering or only the relation between the two nouns has gone. Where the construct noun would be left with nothing at all, the word is its own and this rule's first sentence does not reach it; where the construct noun keeps an English word of its own — `דֶּרֶךְ אַמָּה אֶחָת` → *a way of one cubit* — the first sentence stands and the *of* goes to the rectum. Do not mark such a construct `unrepresented` in order to give its preposition to the rectum: that leaves a lexical noun holding a plain English rendering unaligned, against rule 2. The corpus renders a construct `דֶּרֶךְ` by *toward*, *is toward* or *was toward* in eighteen units, always headed on the preposition, and records no unit where the rectum of such a phrase holds the preposition while the construct goes unrepresented. Two riders. Where a Hebrew preposition stands before such a construct noun and the ASV writes a single English rendering for the pair, rule 30 fuses them instead, whether that rendering is one word, as `לִפְנֵי` → *before*, or a compound preposition written in two or three, as `לְעֻמַּת` → *answerable to*, which the corpus fuses at EZK.40.18 and EZK.48.13, 18 (twice) and 21 and nowhere splits. Read rule 30's condition here as one rendering rather than one token, exactly as that rule states it: a second English word defeats the condition only where the ASV spends it on one morpheme in particular, and subtracting the *to* of *answerable to* leaves no morpheme short of a rendering of its own, since the construct noun is not a word the ASV ever writes alone; and where the phrase is the predicate of a verbless clause the construction-supplied copula joins the construct noun's unit under rule 20, as `דֶּרֶךְ הַצָּפוֹן` → *were toward the north*. **And a second `דֶּרֶךְ` standing in the same phrase is not a repetition the ASV renders once.** `הַדֶּרֶךְ דֶּרֶךְ הַצָּפוֹן` → *the way toward the north* gives *the way* to the absolute, with its article taking the *the*, and *toward* to the construct, and rule 22 reaches neither, because each has an English word of its own.
20. A verbless clause supplies its English copula from the construction, not from nothing. In *that it was good*, the supplied *it was* belongs to the unit headed on `טוֹב`; do not mark it `added`. **Where the ASV renders a verbless clause with a presentative *there was* or *there were*, the dummy *there* is part of the same construction-supplied frame as the copula and joins the unit that bears that copula under this rule.** It is never `added`, and the unit still heads on its lexical predicate or anchor. **The anchor may be of any kind, since what licenses the dummy is the presentative frame the construction supplies and not the part of speech the frame is built on.** A nominal anchor, a prepositional predicate and an adverbial anchor are read alike: `וְעוֹד חָמֵשְׁ שָׁנִים` → *and* ***there are yet*** *five years* gives *there are yet* to `עוֹד`, headed on *yet*, and the numeral phrase keeps its own English. Do not read the list as closed and mark the dummy `added` because the anchor is neither a noun nor a preposition; the sentence above says it is never `added`, and rule 17 puts the same dummy on a finite verb and rule 50 on an existential `אַיִן`. **But the dummy this sentence governs is the presentative *there* and no other.** A cleft or impersonal *it* the ASV writes in such a clause — *it was not you that sent me hither*, *It is enough* — is not a presentative frame and no morpheme licenses it, so it is its own `added` unit under rule 30, exactly as the supplied lexical verb of the next sentence is. **And where the ASV clefts a clause, the whole English frame goes the same way.** A cleft writes a dummy *it*, a copula, and a relative before the clause that carries the predication, and where the Hebrew writes a finite verb for that predication no morpheme answers to any of the three: the dummy and its copula are one `added` unit headed on the copula, and the cleft relative is its own `added` unit under rule 55, which marks a subordinator the ASV has written over a clause no written subordinator reaches. The copula is not this rule's construction-supplied one, because the test below asks which morpheme is there to carry the English and a clause whose predication a written verb already carries licenses no copula; and the relative is not rule 46's, because that rule supplies a relative to make a participle or a headless clause into a noun phrase, whereas a clefted nominal the ASV has already rendered is a written antecedent with a finite clause juxtaposed to it, which rule 46 excludes in its own terms. `לֹא־רַבִּים יֶחְכָּמוּ` → ***It is*** *not the great* ***that*** *are wise* gives *not* to the negative, *the great* to the substantival adjective under rule 46 and *are wise* to the finite verb, leaving *It is* and *that* `added`. Read what stands in the Hebrew before marking any of the three: a written `אֲשֶׁר` takes the relative under rule 46's first rider, an explicit pronoun takes a *that* the ASV writes for it under rule 18, and where the clefted clause is itself verbless the copula is the predicate's under the sentences below and only the dummy and the relative are supplied. **But an impersonal *it* the ASV writes inside one morpheme's own fixed English rendering is not such a dummy.** Where a Hebrew predicate word or interjection is rendered by an English expression the ASV never writes that word without — `רַע בְּעֵינֵי` → *it displease* and *it seem evil*, `חָלִילָה` → *Far be it* — the *it* is part of that morpheme's own rendering, joins its unit and is never `added`, exactly as rule 30 gives the pronouns of `נָא` → *I beseech thee* to the particle and rule 55 gives the *As* of `חַי־אָנִי` → *As I live* to the adjective; rule 40 already reads `אִם־רַע בְּעֵינֶיךָ` → *if it displease thee* that way. Tell the two apart by the ASV's own use of the English, which is rule 31's test read for a frame rather than for a noun: where the ASV elsewhere writes that predicate's own word with nothing around it — *enough* for `רַב` — the frame is supplied and the preceding sentences govern, and where it never writes the word without the frame, the frame is the morpheme's. The unit still heads on the word carrying the lexeme, so `חָלִילָה` → *Far be it* heads on *Far*. This reaches a frame standing inside one morpheme's own rendering only: where every morpheme of the clause already holds its own English and the word is the opening of a cleft the ASV has built around it, the preceding sentences govern as before, and a referential subject pronoun answering to no morpheme at all is `added` on the terms rule 17 gives a supplied lexical subject noun. **Apart from that presentative dummy, this covers the copula and nothing more.** Where the ASV instead supplies a whole *lexical* verb for a clause whose Hebrew has none — the allotment formula `וְלִבְנֵי קְהָת הַנּוֹתָרִים ... עָרִים עָשֶׂר` → *And unto the rest of the sons of Kohath* ***were given*** *by lot ... ten cities* — no morpheme's rendering that verb completes, and it is `added` under rule 30, together with any object the ASV supplies alongside it, as in *were given them*. Test by asking which morpheme is there to carry the English: `הָיָה` carries the *have* of rule 43 and the frame of rule 21, a Hebrew verb carries the light verb of rule 44, and a preposition that is itself the predicate carries the copula of rule 49 — but a preposition the ASV has already spent a word on, as in *unto the rest*, carries nothing further, and a clause with no verb in it at all licenses no verb. **But the copula is whatever verb the ASV writes where a copula would stand, and not the word *be* alone.** English links a subject to its predicate with *seem*, *become*, *prove*, *remain* and *come* as readily as with *be*, and such a verb is the construction-supplied copula of this rule rather than a supplied lexical verb: it joins the unit of the predicate morpheme it links, which keeps its own English beside it, and that unit still heads on the word carrying the predicate's lexeme under rule 24 rather than on the linking verb. `וּגְדוֹלָה הִיא אֵלָי` → *and it* ***seemed*** *great unto me* gives *seemed great* to `גָּדוֹל`, headed on *great*, while `הִיא` keeps only its own *it*; `הַכֹּל כַּאֲשֶׁר לַכֹּל` → *All things* ***come*** *alike to all* gives *come alike* to the `כַּאֲשֶׁר` that is the clause's predicate, headed on *alike*. This is rule 49's reading of `אֲשֶׁר לַבָּיִת` → *which* ***belonged to*** *the house* and of a resultative `לְ` → *shalt become*, holding for a predicate of any kind and not only a prepositional one, and rule 30 already states that a copula the construction hands to the whole of a fused expression does not defeat its one-rendering condition. What tells such a verb from the supplied lexical verb of the preceding sentences is whether a predicate morpheme stands in the clause for it to link: a linking verb stands beside that predicate's own English and adds no participant the Hebrew does not write, whereas *were given* introduces an event and an agent the Hebrew never wrote, and rule 34's *cheweth* stands in a line whose predicate the Hebrew leaves unwritten altogether. Test by subtraction as rules 16, 31 and 43 do — drop the English verb and ask whether what has gone is the link English writes for a predication the Hebrew already made, or the predication itself. **But an event of the subject's own is enough to make such a verb a supplied lexical one, and a participant the Hebrew does not write is not the condition.** Where the English verb names something the subject does, rather than asserting that the subject is, becomes or continues what the predicate says, it predicates an event the Hebrew never wrote and is `added` under the preceding sentences, however plainly the clause is verbless and whatever its predicate is made of — a prepositional predicate included, which rule 49 hands the construction's copula and nothing further. `נַפְשִׁי לַאדֹנָי` → *My soul* ***waiteth*** *for the Lord* leaves the *waiteth* `added` and gives the *for* to the `לְ`, while `לְעוֹלָם חַסְדּוֹ` → *his lovingkindness* ***endureth*** *for ever* keeps its verb with the `לְ` under rule 49, because enduring through the time the predicate names is the continuance a copula asserts and waiting for a person is not. Run the subtraction above by asking what a bare *is* written in that verb's place would leave the clause saying: where the bare copula says what the Hebrew says, the written verb is the construction's, and where an action goes missing with it, the verb is supplied. An ASV italic on such a verb corroborates it and decides nothing, by rule 7. **The same lexical-verb boundary holds inside a nominal recasting.** A construct relation can license the English preposition used for it, but not a supplied lexical participle: `חֲמַס אֶרֶץ` → *violence done to the land* gives *violence* to the construct noun and *to the land* to its dependent under rule 19, while *done* is `added` because no source verb licenses it. This is not rule 31's generic completion of a substantive's lexeme: *done* predicates an event of the violence rather than naming the violence. **The copula goes to the predicate, and where a predicate morpheme is present an explicit subject takes only its own English.** Where the clause writes its subject — a pronoun such as `אָנֹכִי`, `אֲנִי`, `אַתְּ` or `הוּא`, or a subject noun — that morpheme takes the English pronoun or noun realizing it and nothing more, while the supplied *am*, *art*, *is*, *are* or *was* joins the predicate, exactly as rule 49 puts it on the preposition heading a prepositional predicate and rule 34 puts it on the predicate a gapped line licenses. `עֶבֶד אַבְרָהָם אָנֹכִי` → *I* ***am*** *Abraham's servant* gives *am* to `עֶבֶד` and *I* to `אָנֹכִי`; `בַת מִי אַתְּ` → *Whose daughter* ***art*** *thou* gives *art* to `בַת`; `הוּא אֲדֹנִי` → *It* ***is*** *my master* gives *is* to `אֲדֹנִי`; and `מִי הָאִישׁ הַלָּזֶה` → *What man* ***is*** *this* gives *is* to `מִי`, which is the clause's predicate, leaving *this* to the demonstrative. **A tripartite clause whose resuming pronoun the ASV renders by nothing but that copula is read on those same terms.** Where the clause writes a subject noun and resumes it with `הוּא`, `הִיא` or `הֵמָּה` before the predicate, and the ASV realizes the subject once by the noun, the resuming pronoun is the unrendered occurrence and is its own `unrepresented` unit under rule 22, while the copula joins the predicate as the preceding sentences direct, discontinuous around a possessive or an article under rule 25: `יְהוָה הוּא נַחֲלָתוֹ` → *Jehovah* ***is*** *their* ***inheritance*** gives *is inheritance* to `נַחֲלָה` and leaves `הוּא` nothing. Do not give the copula to that pronoun on the ground that a present morpheme takes its English before a construction-supplied reading: what that reasoning governs, in rules 46, 47 and 48, is an English word some morpheme's own sense answers to, and a copula answers to the predication rather than to the pronoun, which is why this rule sends it to the predicate wherever a predicate morpheme stands. Where instead the ASV writes a word of its own for the pronoun — `הוּא הַדָּבָר` → ***that*** *is the thing* — that pronoun is holding an English word already and is not a home for a second one, as rule 51 says of a waw, and the copula still joins the predicate. One chapter may write both shapes, and DEU.18.2 and DEU.18.22 do. **But the predicate morpheme that sentence looks for is one free to bear the copula, and a predicate that is itself a clause is not.** Where the predicate of such a tripartite clause is a relative or headless relative clause whose written finite verb already carries its own predication, no morpheme in it is available to take the matrix copula: assigning the word to that verb would leave one Hebrew verb holding two English clauses, which rule 17 refuses for the same reason in its matrix duration frame, and the resuming pronoun is then the clause's only overt anchor and takes the copula, exactly as an explicit subject does where the predicate is wholly elided. `מַה־שֶּׁהָיָה הוּא שֶׁיִּהְיֶה` → *That which hath been* ***is*** *that which shall be* gives *is* to `הוּא`, while `שֶׁ` keeps the *that which* rule 46's first rider gives it and `יִּהְיֶה` keeps its own *shall be*. Read what the predicate is before applying the preceding paragraph: a nominal, adjectival or prepositional predicate is free to take the copula and the pronoun is `unrepresented` as before, and one chapter may write both shapes, as ECC.1.9 and ECC.1.17 do. **Where the predicate is wholly elided and no predicate morpheme is available to bear the construction-supplied copula, the copula instead joins the explicit subject as the clause's sole overt anchor:** `אֲנִי` → *I am*. This exception does not apply where a predicate is written. **And a copula the ASV fronts as a question auxiliary belongs to the interrogative particle, not to the predicate.** Where `הֲ`, or the `אִם` opening a following disjunctive question, stands in a verbless clause and the ASV writes a single *Is*, *Am*, *Art*, *Are* or *Was* at the clause head, that one word is both this rule's construction-supplied copula and the question auxiliary rule 17 gives the particle: the particle takes it and heads on it, and the predicate keeps only its own English. `הֲלֹא יִרְאָתְךָ כִּסְלָתֶךָ` → ***Is*** *not thy fear of God thy confidence* gives *Is* to `הֲ` and leaves `כִּסְלָה` the bare *confidence*. This is rule 50's reading of an interrogative existential clause holding for a verbless clause with any predicate, and rule 17's *belongs to the interrogative particle and not to the verb* holding where there is no verb to compete for the word. Where the ASV writes a second copula for the predicate itself, that one is the predicate's under this rule's earlier sentences; where no interrogative morpheme stands in the clause, there is no particle to take it and those sentences govern alone. Read the Hebrew clause for which side is the predicate rather than the English word order, since the copula stands beside the subject in every one of these; where the predicate's own words then stand apart from it, the unit is discontinuous under rule 25. **Rule 20 also governs an identifying formula where the ASV repeats one subject pronoun as both an English subject demonstrative and a predicate determiner.** Both realizations belong to that pronoun in one discontinuous unit, while the copula still goes to the written predicate: `הוּא אַהֲרֹן וּמֹשֶׁה` → *These are that Aaron and Moses* gives *These ... that* to `הוּא`, headed on *These*, and *are Aaron* to `אַהֲרֹן`. The repeated demonstrative is not `added`; it is the ASV's emphatic identifying expansion of the single pronoun.
**Rule 20 also governs an identifying formula where the ASV repeats one subject pronoun as both an English subject demonstrative and a predicate determiner.** Both realizations belong to that pronoun in one discontinuous unit, while the copula still goes to the written predicate: `הוּא אַהֲרֹן וּמֹשֶׁה` → *These are that Aaron and Moses* gives *These ... that* to `הוּא`, headed on *These*, and *are Aaron* to `אַהֲרֹן`. The repeated demonstrative is not `added`; it is the ASV's emphatic identifying expansion of the single pronoun.

21. `הָיָה` in an existential or presentative clause may align to English *there was*, *there be*, or *it was*. The dummy subject belongs to that verb's unit. In the resultative `הָיָה לְ`, where the ASV recasts the clause as a passive — `הָיָה לִשְׂרֵפַת אֵשׁ` → *is burned with fire* — the copula belongs to `הָיָה` and the `לְ` is its own `unrepresented` unit, since the recasting leaves no English word for it.
22. A repeated or resumptive element that the ASV renders once is still its own unit. Mark the unrepresented occurrence `unrepresented` rather than folding it into the rendered one. Where the repetition is a counted noun distributed across a compound numeral — Hebrew writes one `שָׁנָה` per numeral group where the ASV writes a single *years* — the rendered occurrence is the last, closing the whole Hebrew figure as the English noun closes the whole English one. Do not settle it by number agreement: Hebrew writes `שָׁנִים` after 2–10 and `שָׁנָה` from 11 up whatever the total, while the English plural follows the sum, so a match between them is an accident of two unrelated agreement systems and is not evidence. Do not settle it by which numeral the English noun stands beside either, since English drives the counted noun to the end of the phrase whatever order the translation chose. Tail-to-tail is what survives, and it holds across both Hebrew group orders and the ASV's frequent reordering of them. **Tail-to-tail governs a run of absolute occurrences only.** Where the phrase is instead headed by a **construct** — a regnal date such as `בִּשְׁנַת עֶשְׂרִים וָשֶׁבַע שָׁנָה` → *In the twenty and seventh year* — the construct is what the English realizes, and the trailing absolute is the resumptive occurrence that goes `unrepresented`. So the rendered occurrence is the last of several absolutes but the first where a construct heads them, and MACULA's morphology is what decides which case this is. Position is the consequence, not the rule. **But a morpheme whose sense the ASV has written inside a fused English word is not an unrendered occurrence.** Where a relative clause resumes its antecedent with a pronominal suffix on a preposition, or with a resumptive adverb, and the ASV writes one pronominal adverb for the whole — *wherein*, *whereon*, *whereby*, *whither*, and the bare *therein* of a preposition and its own suffix — the relative, the preposition and the resumptive morpheme share one unit under rule 30, headed on the relative, or on the preposition where no relative stands. Do not mark the suffix `unrepresented` under this rule's first sentence: rule 30's one-English-word condition is met, because that word carries the resumption as well as the relation, exactly as *Nor* carries both the coordination and the negation in rule 35. The corpus reads `אֲשֶׁר בּוֹ` → *wherein* as one three-morpheme unit in twenty-one places and `בָּהּ` → *therein* as one two-morpheme unit in sixty-one, and records no unit where such a suffix stands outside the word. So ask first whether the English word carries the morpheme's sense: what this rule's first sentence governs is an element the ASV has left unwritten, as a second `בֵּין` or a surplus `שָׁנָה` is, and not one it has written inside a fusion. **But read whether the ASV has written one English word or two before reading a fusion there.** Where it renders the relative and the preposition by two separate English words — *upon which*, *in which*, *with whom* — rule 30's one-English-word condition is unmet, rule 2 splits them, and each of the two morphemes takes the word written for it, the relative taking the relative pronoun and the preposition taking its own. The resumptive suffix is then the occurrence the ASV has left unwritten and is its own `unrepresented` unit under this rule's first sentence, since the relative pronoun already carries the resumption; this is the division rule 43 makes in `אֲשֶׁר בּוֹ מוּם` → *that hath a blemish*, where two morphemes point at the antecedent and the ASV writes one word for it. Do not instead give the relative pronoun to the suffix under rule 18 and mark the written relative `unrepresented`: what that rule places is a relative in a clause with no relative morpheme in it, and a present morpheme takes its English before another reading does, as rules 46 and 47 direct. One chapter may write both shapes for one and the same Hebrew clause, and JDG.16.26 → *the pillars* ***whereupon*** *the house resteth* and JDG.16.29 → *the two middle pillars* ***upon which*** *the house rested* do. **The interrogative `אֵי` with a following `זֶה` is one interrogative expression, and its demonstrative is not an unrendered occurrence either.** Where the ASV renders `אֵי־זֶה` by a single interrogative word — *where*, *what*, *which* — the two morphemes share one unit under rule 30, headed on `אֵי`, which carries the wh-lexeme; the demonstrative is enclitic to the compound rather than a repeated or resumptive element, so this rule's first sentence does not reach it and it is never marked `unrepresented` beside a rendered `אֵי`. A construction-supplied copula joining that unit does not defeat rule 30's one-rendering condition, exactly as it does not for `לִפְנֵי` → *was before*, so `אֵי־זֶה הַדֶּרֶךְ` → ***Where is*** *the way* fuses as `אֵי־זֶה` → *what* does. The corpus fuses this compound in every unit it records — JER.6.16, 1KI.13.12, ISA.66.1 twice, ECC.11.6 and 1KI.22.24 — and splits it in none. Two limits. Where the ASV spends a word on each morpheme, the condition is unmet and rule 2 splits them as usual. And this governs `אֵי־זֶה` and no other pair: a deictic `זֶה` standing after an interrogative that keeps its own English word — the `זֶה` of `לָמָּה זֶּה` or of `בֶּן־מִי־זֶה`, and the `זֶה` of `מִי זֶה` where the ASV writes *this* for it — is this rule's ordinary case, rendered where the ASV writes a word for it and `unrepresented` where it does not. **A resumptive possessive suffix is read on exactly those terms, and it need not stand on a preposition.** Where the clause resumes its antecedent with a suffix on a noun inside it and the ASV writes one *whose* for the pair, the relative and that suffix share one unit under rule 30, headed on the relative, while the noun keeps its own English: `אֶרֶץ אֲשֶׁר אֲבָנֶיהָ בַרְזֶל` → *a land* ***whose*** *stones are iron* is one unit on `אֲשֶׁר` and the suffix, with *stones* left to `אֶבֶן`. Contiguity is not the condition, for the reason rule 35 gives: however much of the clause stands between them, the one English word still carries both the relation and the resumption, so nothing is left over to mark. The corpus reads `אֲשֶׁר` with its resumptive suffix as one *whose* unit at DEU.28.49 and 1KI.8.39, and as a `noncompositional` one at AMO.2.9 and DAN.10.1 among eighteen such places. **But read which morphemes stand in each clause before counting a second occurrence.** Where a further clause is coordinated under the one written relative and the ASV rewrites its *whose* at the head of that clause, a resumptive suffix standing in that clause is what the second word realizes, under rule 18's sentence giving a suffix the English relative pronoun that renders it: `וּמֵהֲרָרֶיהָ תַּחְצֹב נְחֹשֶׁת` → *and out of* ***whose*** *hills thou mayest dig copper* gives its *whose* to the suffix on `הֲרָרֶיהָ`, not to the relative as a repetition under rule 25. Where no such suffix stands in the coordinated clause, rule 55's distributed subordinator governs as before and every occurrence is the relative's. **And read whether the suffix points at the antecedent at all before treating it as resumptive.** Every fusion above rests on a morpheme that resumes the antecedent, and a pronominal suffix standing inside the relative clause does that only where its referent is the antecedent; agreement is the first check and reference decides, so read MACULA's person, gender and number against the noun the clause modifies. Where the suffix instead denotes something else the clause speaks of, the one English pronominal adverb carries the relative's own relation and nothing of that suffix, rule 30's condition is unmet for it, and the fusion does not reach it: the relative takes the adverb alone, the suffix takes whatever English pronoun the ASV writes for it under rule 18, and the preposition it stands on is disposed of by rules 11 and 40 like any other. `וְאֶת־תַּחֲלֻאֶיהָ אֲשֶׁר־חִלָּה יְהוָה בָּהּ` → *and the sicknesses* ***wherewith*** *Jehovah hath made* ***it*** *sick* gives *wherewith* to `אֲשֶׁר` alone, *it* to the suffix of `בָּהּ`, which is feminine singular and denotes the land rather than the masculine plural `תַּחֲלֻאִים` the relative modifies, and leaves the `בְּ` `unrepresented` under rule 40, since the ASV promotes its object to the direct object of *made ... sick*. A relative left holding such a word by itself is not a unit short of its parts: the corpus aligns a bare `אֲשֶׁר` to *wherewith* in fifty-five units, and its *wherein* units include bare relatives beside the three-morpheme fusions. Count the English pronouns against the morphemes that could carry them, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s: where the ASV writes a pronoun besides the adverb, some morpheme other than the relative answers for it, and where it writes none, the resumption is inside the adverb and the paragraphs above govern. **A distributive repetition is read on those same terms, and there the rendered occurrence is the first.** Where Hebrew joins a noun to a repetition of itself with a waw and the ASV writes one English noun with a distributive determiner standing before it — `לְשַׁעַר וָשָׁעַר` → *at* ***every gate***, and likewise *each* or *every one* — the first occurrence takes the noun's own English word together with that supplied determiner under rule 15, the waw is `unrepresented` under rule 14 for want of a written connective, and the repeated occurrence is `unrepresented` under this rule's first sentence. Do not fuse the three morphemes into one unit under rule 30: that rule's condition is one rendering of which no morpheme owns a part, and the English noun is a word the ASV spends on this noun in particular, which the subtraction test rules 16, 31 and 43 use finds gone with it while the repetition and the coordination are left saying nothing either way. Tail-to-tail above governs a counted noun distributed across a compound numeral and does not reach this: what the ASV writes here is a determiner and a noun opening one phrase, and the first occurrence is where they stand, exactly as rule 47 reads a phrase's single *the* onto the first of the articles it opens. **But that reading rests on the single preposition opening the phrase, and a repetition whose occurrences each carry their own rendered preposition is not it.** Where Hebrew writes a preposition on each occurrence — `כְּפַעַם בְּפַעַם` → *as at other times* — and the ASV renders both prepositions and writes one English noun after the second, that noun stands inside the second phrase and the second occurrence is the rendered one, the first going `unrepresented` under this rule's first sentence; read which preposition the English noun stands after, as rule 47 reads which word the phrase's single *the* stands before. Three limits. Where a single English word carries the whole repetition, spending nothing on either occurrence in particular — a lone adverb such as *daily* — rule 30's condition is met and the morphemes share one unit as before. Where the ASV writes a connective for the waw, or an English word for each occurrence, nothing is left unrendered, rule 2 splits them, and each morpheme takes the word written for it. And where an article or another determiner morpheme stands on the noun, that morpheme takes the written determiner under rules 41 and 15 as it always does, since what this paragraph places is the repetition and not the determiner.

## Heads

23. A one-token source unit takes that token as its source head. In a multi-source unit choose the lexical or syntactic head. **Where one morpheme in the unit carries content the others do not, that morpheme is the head, whatever its position.** A construct noun fused with its preposition heads the unit — `לִפְנֵי` → *before*, `בְּתוֹךְ` → *among* — exactly as rule 30 directs; a negation heads the unit it is fused into, as rule 35 heads a `וְ` + `לֹא` *Nor* on the negative and rule 48 heads `מֵאֵין` → *without* on the `אַיִן`; and a relative heads a resumptive fusion, as rule 22 heads `אֲשֶׁר בּוֹ` → *wherein* on the relative. **Where no morpheme carries content the others lack — a compound preposition written from two prepositions, or a pair of particles — the syntactic head decides, and that is the morpheme governing the rest, which in Hebrew is the one standing first**: `מֵאֵת` → *of*, `מֵעַל` → *from* or *against*, `כַּאֲשֶׁר` → *as* and `הַאִם` → *shall* each head on their opening morpheme. **Two lexical morphemes may tie on exactly those terms, and what the morphemes are tagged is no part of the condition.** Where rule 30 fuses a construct chain, or a noun with its attributive, into a single English word that writes both lexemes — `עִקְּבֵי סוּס` → *horsehoofs*, `הֹלְכֵי נְתִיבוֹת` → *travellers*, `אֳרָחוֹת עֲקַלְקַלּוֹת` → *byways* — neither noun carries content the other lacks, since each one's sense stands inside the one English word, so the preceding sentence governs and the head is the morpheme governing the rest: the construct, or the noun the attributive modifies, which in Hebrew is the one standing first. This is rule 6's ruling that a rendering writing more than one morpheme's lexeme leaves none of them uniquely defensible, read for the source head of an `aligned` fusion rather than for the display anchor of a `noncompositional` one. But read whether both lexemes are written before reaching for it: where the single English word writes one noun's lexeme alone, this rule's first sentence governs and that morpheme heads, as `אִשָּׁה זוֹנָה` → *harlot's* heads on `זוֹנָה` under rule 30. **The English word written is not what chooses a source head.** Rule 24's cross-language anchoring governs the target head alone, so do not move the source head onto the morpheme whose own sense the single English word happens to carry: one and the same `מֵעַל` rendered *from* in one verse and *against* in the next is one construction with one source head, and reading it off the translation would make the source analysis a function of the ASV's choice of preposition. Where two morphemes genuinely tie, choose and name the competing reading in the note under rule 8.
24. A target head is the cross-language lexical display anchor: the English token that most directly realizes the source head's lexeme, with English syntax used only to break a genuine tie. Choose the lexical predicate over subjects and auxiliaries (`was gathered` heads on `gathered`), the noun in `of + noun`, and the substantive over a generic English noun supplied to expand it (`living creature` for a substantival adjective heads on `living`; rule 31 gives the test and governs the whole family). Where one morpheme is rendered by a multi-word English preposition, the head is the token carrying the lexeme rather than the connective: `מִן` → *out of* heads on *out*. **Where the ASV recasts one morpheme's relation as a fixed English phrase in which no word realizes that morpheme's own lexeme, head on the content word the phrase turns on, found by rule 31's subtraction test, and never on one of its connectives**: `מִן` → *of one piece with* heads on *piece*, because dropping *piece* leaves the phrase saying nothing while *of*, *one* and *with* are what English spends on the relation, exactly as `מִן` → *by reason of* heads on *reason*. This reaches only a rendering that drops the lexeme; where a word does realize it, the preceding sentence governs and the head stays there. **But a degree word the ASV writes beside a comparative preposition is not the token carrying the lexeme, and the *out of* sentence does not reach it.** Where a comparative `מִן` is rendered by a degree adverb together with the relational word — *more than*, *rather than* — the comparison's own relation is what the morpheme says, and the degree adverb is what English spends on a comparison it writes without one elsewhere, so the unit heads on *than*, exactly as a bare `מִן` → *than* does. Test by the subtraction rules 16, 31 and 43 use: drop *than* and the comparison has no relation left to state, while dropping *more* or *rather* leaves the comparative the ASV writes in that shape throughout. Read which of the two English words the ASV has added rather than which of them stands first, since in *out of* it is the opening word that carries the ablative lexeme and the closing one that is the bare connective, and in *more than* it is the other way about. The corpus aligns `מִן` to *more than* headed on *than* in twenty-five units and to a bare *than* in a hundred and nineteen. **And the relational word is whichever one the ASV has written, not the word *than* in particular.** English states a comparison of excess with *too … for* as readily as one of degree with *more … than*, and where the ASV renders a comparative `מִן` that way the sentences above govern unchanged: both words belong to that one morpheme's possibly discontinuous unit and the head goes on the relational word, so `פְּלִיאָה דַעַת מִמֶּנִּי` → *too wonderful for me* gives *too … for* to the `מִן`, headed on *for*, while the predicate adjective keeps its own *wonderful* with the copula rule 20 supplies. Do not leave the degree adverb with the adjective, the verb or the noun it stands beside, since rule 20 states in its own terms that the construction supplies the copula and nothing more; and do not mark it `added` under rule 30, since rule 11 gives a function word first to the morpheme whose sense it carries and the excess is part of what the comparative says. The unit is discontinuous under rule 25 wherever the predicate stands between the two English words, as it does in `יִרְבּוּ … מֵחוֹל` → *more in number than the sand*. Run the same subtraction before applying this: drop the relational word and the comparison has no relation left to state, while dropping *too* leaves the comparison the ASV writes elsewhere without it. **But read that sentence as a ruling about a degree adverb, and not as a direction to head on whichever English word states the relation.** Where a preposition is rendered by a fixed English phrase built on a relational content word and closed by a colourless *to* or *of* — *according to*, *by reason of*, *out of* — that content word is the token carrying the lexeme and the head goes there, never on the connective, whatever relation the source preposition would state if it stood alone. `לְ` → *according to* and `כְּ` → *according to* both head on *according*, because the same subtraction finds that dropping *according* leaves a bare dative or comparative stating some other relation altogether, while dropping *to* leaves the conformity the ASV wrote the phrase for. What the *more than* sentence excludes is a degree adverb English adds to a comparison it writes elsewhere without one; a word the ASV never omits from the phrase is not such an addition, so ask whether the ASV writes the relation without that word before treating it as added. The corpus heads `לְ` → *according to* on *according* in a hundred and seventeen units and on *to* in none. Review this choice independently of the unit's edges.
25. Reordered or discontinuous target tokens may occupy one unit when they realize one source item. Do not absorb unrelated intervening words into that unit. **Where English repeats one source function word because its scope is distributed across coordinated or emphatically repeated phrases or clauses, every English occurrence belongs to that source morpheme's one possibly discontinuous unit.** `מִן` in `מִבּוֹא בְדָמִים וְהוֹשֵׁוַע` → *from bloodguiltiness, and from avenging* takes both occurrences of *from*, while the waw takes only *and*; `בִּי אֲנִי` → *upon me ... upon me* gives both occurrences of *upon* to the single preposition while the suffix and explicit pronoun take their respective *me*. The repeated English word is neither `added` nor reassigned to a neighboring source item merely to make the units contiguous. **And the repeated word need not be one the source writes.** Where another rule assigns a supplied English function word to a morpheme's unit — a preposition the English verb's valency needs to reach its own object under rule 44, or a word the ASV supplies for a bare nominal under rule 33 — and the ASV writes that same word again over an elliptical or gapped parallel member, the second occurrence belongs to that same morpheme's one possibly discontinuous unit on exactly the terms above, however much stands between. `תְּבַקְשֶׁנָּה כַכָּסֶף וְכַמַּטְמוֹנִים תַּחְפְּשֶׂנָּה` → *search* ***for*** *her as* ***for*** *hid treasures* gives both *for*s to the verb, since the second stands for the searching the comparative clause elides, while each comparative `כְּ` takes only the *as* written for it. Do not read such a repetition as a second English word for the preposition, the conjunction or the comparative particle it stands beside, which would give one morpheme a two-word rendering the ASV writes for it nowhere else; and do not mark it `added` under rule 30, since the morpheme that licensed the first occurrence licenses this one. Test by subtraction as elsewhere — drop the repeated word and ask whether what has gone is the reach of a rendering already placed in the clause, or a relation no morpheme there carries. **An explicit personal pronoun is read the same way where the ASV resumes it in a later clause of the same verse.** Where a following verbless clause writes no subject of its own and the ASV supplies the subject pronoun that an explicit `הוּא`, `הֵמָּה`, `אֲנִי` or the like standing earlier in the verse denotes, both occurrences belong to that pronoun's one discontinuous unit, exactly as rule 18 gives both pronouns of a twice-rendered suffix to the suffix and rule 20 gives both halves of an identifying formula to its one `הוּא`: `הֵמָּה סֹחֲרֵי יָדֵךְ ... בָּם סֹחֲרָיִךְ` → *they were the merchants of thy hand ... in these were* ***they*** *thy merchants* gives both *they*s to the one `הֵמָּה`, headed on the first. Such a pronoun is not `added`, and it does not join the predicate's unit: rule 20 hands the predicate the construction's copula and, in a presentative clause, its dummy *there*, and a referential pronoun is neither, so the clause's supplied *were* goes to the predicate while the pronoun goes to the morpheme that denotes it. This governs a clause with no subject of its own: where a finite verb stands in the resumed clause it takes the English subject pronoun under rule 17, which is a pronoun realizing that verb's own person and number. **And where the Hebrew stands the pronoun is not part of the condition.** Hebrew postposes an explicit subject pronoun after its predicate and that predicate's adjuncts, often before a coordinated second subject, while English writes a subject before its verb; where the ASV writes the pronoun in both places it has realized one written morpheme twice, and both occurrences are that pronoun's one discontinuous unit, headed on the first English occurrence: `וְהִנֵּה נִצָּב עַל־עֹלָתוֹ הוּא וְכָל־שָׂרֵי מוֹאָב` → *and lo* ***he*** *was standing by his burnt offering,* ***he*** *and all the princes of Moab* gives both *he*s to the one `הוּא`, while `נִצָּב` keeps the *was standing* rule 20 leaves it and the coordinated subject keeps its own English. So read whether one written pronoun is realized twice rather than which side of the resumption it stands on, and do not hand the first occurrence to the predicate to make the units contiguous: rule 17's permission reaches a pronoun realizing a finite verb's own person and number, and a participle or other predicate morpheme in a verbless clause takes only the copula. **And the morpheme that denotes the resumed subject need not be a pronoun.** Where a common noun or a proper name written in the verse is the referent the ASV resumes, every English realization of that referent belongs to the noun's one possibly discontinuous unit on exactly the terms above, headed on the first English occurrence: `עַל־אֱלוֹהַּ נוֹרָא הוֹד` → ***God*** *hath upon* ***him*** *terrible majesty* gives both *God* and *him* to the one `אֱלוֹהַּ`, and `שַׁדַּי לֹא־מְצָאנֻהוּ שַׂגִּיא־כֹּחַ` → *Touching* ***the Almighty****, we cannot find him out;* ***He*** *is excellent in power* gives the resuming *He* to `שַׁדַּי`, while `שַׂגִּיא` keeps the *is excellent* rule 20 leaves it. This is the preceding paragraphs' own reasoning read for the morpheme that is actually written — the pronoun goes to the morpheme that denotes it — since what licenses the English word is that the verse writes the referent, and not the part of speech the ASV first realized it by. Rule 17's ruling that a supplied lexical subject noun is `added` does not reach such a pronoun: that ruling governs an English noun answering to no morpheme at all, as `יָשְׁבוּ בָהֶּ` → *men shall dwell therein* is, and here a written nominal answers to it, so read whether the verse writes the referent before marking a supplied subject `added` or folding it into the predicate under rule 20. **But that nominal must stand earlier in the Hebrew verse than the clause whose subject the ASV supplies, and what it does where it stands is no part of the condition.** Resumption is the continuing of a referent the verse has already named, which is why the paragraphs above ask for a nominal standing earlier in the verse; so read the source order rather than the English, and read whether the nominal is there to be continued rather than what case or office it holds. A vocative, a fronted topic or any other detached noun standing before that clause is a nominal the pronoun resumes and takes the pronoun exactly as a written subject does: `אַל בָּנָי ... מַעֲבִרִים עַם־יְהוָה` → *Nay,* ***my sons*** *... **ye** make Jehovah's people to transgress* gives *ye* to `בֵּן`, headed on *sons* under this rule's closing sentence, which is also what rule 36 means in refusing to mark a vocative's supplied *ye* `added`. Where instead the verse's only mention of that referent stands after that clause and inside a clause of its own, nothing there is being continued: the supplied pronoun answers to no morpheme this clause reaches and is its own `added` unit under rule 20's closing sentence, whatever a neighbouring verse names, since rule 10 makes the verse the whole unit of work. `מֵקִים מֵעָפָר דָּל ... כִּי לַיהוָה מְצֻקֵי אֶרֶץ` → ***He*** *raiseth up the poor out of the dust ... For the pillars of the earth are Jehovah's* leaves that *He* `added`, the name being written only as the predicate possessor of a later causal clause, while `יְהוָה מֵמִית וּמְחַיֶּה מוֹרִיד שְׁאוֹל וַיָּעַל` → *Jehovah killeth, and maketh alive:* ***He*** *bringeth down to Sheol, and bringeth up* gives its *He* to the `יְהוָה` standing at the head of the verse, and 1SA.2.6 and 1SA.2.8 are the two shapes in one chapter. The cataphoric case below is the limit of this and not a counter-example: `בְּפֶה חָנֵף` writes its possessive before the lexeme-bearing word inside a clause the noun itself stands in, which is a reordering this rule allows, and not a reach forward into a clause the noun has nothing to do with. **But the morpheme those sentences look for is one the verse writes as a nominal of its own, and a finite verb's inflection is not one.** Where the only morpheme in the verse denoting that referent is the person and number of a finite verb, rule 17 has already realized it as that verb's own subject pronoun, and a further subject pronoun the ASV supplies for a coordinated verbless clause answers to nothing left in the verse: it is its own `added` unit under rule 20's closing sentence, while the copula joins the predicate as that rule directs. `יִצְפֹּן לַיְשָׁרִים תּוּשִׁיָּה מָגֵן לְהֹלְכֵי תֹם` → ***He*** *layeth up sound wisdom for the upright;* ***He*** ***is*** *a shield to them that walk in integrity* gives the first *He* to `יִצְפֹּן` under rule 17, leaves the second `added`, and puts the *is* on `מָגֵן`. Read what the verse writes rather than what the passage is about, since a referent named in a neighbouring verse is not written in this one and rule 10 makes the verse the whole unit of work; where the verse does write the nominal, the preceding paragraph governs as before. **But a morpheme already holding a pronoun of its own is not a home for a second one.** Where more than one morpheme in the verse denotes that referent, each takes only the English written for it, as rule 51 says of a waw and rule 43 of a spent preposition: the object suffix of `מְצָאנֻהוּ` above takes the *him* of *find him out* under rule 18, and the further *He* goes to `שַׁדַּי`. Count the ASV's realizations against the morphemes that could carry them, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s. **And where the two realizations are not of the same kind, the head goes to the one writing the noun's own lexeme, whichever of them stands first.** The direction to head on the first English occurrence settles a tie between realizations of one kind — two *he*s, two *from*s, or a noun and the pronoun resuming it in that order — and it is not a preference for the phrase's left edge. Where the ASV realizes one written nominal both by a pronoun and by the word that writes its lexeme, rule 24 governs and the head is the lexeme-bearing word, exactly as rule 18 heads a twice-written suffix on the pronoun that realizes it directly rather than on the generic noun beside it, and as rule 31 keeps a head off a word the ASV supplied. This reaches a construct chain whose *nomen rectum* the ASV promotes to the clause's English subject while writing the chain's own relation as a possessive on the head noun: `בְּפֶה חָנֵף יַשְׁחִת רֵעֵהוּ` → *With* ***his*** *mouth* ***the godless man*** *destroyeth his neighbor* gives both *his* and *the godless man* to the one `חָנֵף`, headed on *godless*, while `פֶּה` keeps its own *mouth*, and `וּבְהַוַּת בֹּגְדִים יִלָּכֵדוּ` → *But* ***the treacherous*** *shall be taken in* ***their own*** *iniquity* divides the same way, headed on *treacherous*. Such a possessive is not `added` under rule 18's closing sentence and the promoted subject is not `added` under rule 17: a morpheme in the phrase answers to each, which is the count the paragraph above runs. Read which English word writes the lexeme before reaching for the first occurrence, since Hebrew writes the possessor after its head noun while English writes the possessive before it, so the pronoun stands first as often as not. **But every sentence above places a further realization of the nominal itself, and a pronoun the ASV supplies to complete an English verb's valency is not one.** Where the Hebrew writes no object morpheme on the verb and no object marker in the clause, and the English verb reaches its object only through a pronoun the ASV has written — `וַיִּשְׂאוּ וַיָּבִאוּ אֶל־דָּוִד` → *and* ***took it*** *and* ***brought it*** *to David* — rule 44's supplied-object clause governs and that pronoun is its own `added` unit, however plainly a noun standing earlier in the verse names what it denotes. Reading such a pronoun as a resumption would leave that clause nothing to govern, since a supplied object is supplied precisely because the verse has already named the thing, and rule 44's own `וַיַּפֵּל` → *he let* ***it*** *fall* and `וַיַּשְׁקְ` → *gave* ***them*** *drink* would be resumptions too. What the sentences above place is a subject the clause writes no word for, or a second realization of the nominal inside a phrase the Hebrew writes, as the *him* of `עַל־אֱלוֹהַּ` is the object of the very preposition that noun stands under. So read what the English pronoun completes and not only what it denotes: a supplied subject in a verbless clause is this rule's — `וְעֵץ חַיִּים תַּאֲוָה בָאָה` → *when the desire cometh,* ***it*** *is a tree of life* gives its *it* to `תַּאֲוָה` — and a supplied object of a written verb is rule 44's. Where an object morpheme does stand it takes the pronoun under rule 18 as before, and one verse may write both, as 2SA.23.5 does in giving the *it* of *it is all my salvation* to `בְּרִית` and leaving the *it* of *he maketh it not to grow* `added`. **But an expletive the ASV writes to fill a subject slot English cannot leave empty realizes no referent at all, whatever the verse writes.** Where the ASV extraposes a clausal subject the Hebrew writes — an infinitive phrase, or another clause the ASV renders in full further on — and puts an anticipatory *it* where that subject would stand, the pronoun denotes nothing the verse names and rule 20 governs it: it is its own `added` unit, while the predicate keeps the copula that rule gives it and the extraposed clause keeps its own English. `תוֹעֲבַת כְּסִילִים סוּר מֵרָע` → ***it*** *is an abomination to fools to depart from evil* leaves that *it* `added`, with *is an abomination* on the construct noun and *to depart* on the infinitive. What the sentences above place is a pronoun denoting a referent the verse names and the ASV has rendered by a word of its own, so read what the pronoun stands for before folding it into a written morpheme's unit: `וְעֵץ חַיִּים תַּאֲוָה בָאָה` → *when the desire cometh,* ***it*** *is a tree of life* gives its *it* to `תַּאֲוָה`, which the ASV has already written as *the desire*, and one chapter may write both shapes, as PRO.13.12 and PRO.13.19 do. **An interrogative particle or a negative whose scope covers coordinated clauses is read the same way.** Where the Hebrew writes `הֲ` or `לֹא` once in the first of two coordinated clauses and the ASV rewrites it at the head of the second, every occurrence belongs to that one morpheme's discontinuous unit, headed on the first: `הֲלוֹא מַחֲזֵה־שָׁוְא חֲזִיתֶם וּמִקְסַם כָזָב אֲמַרְתֶּם` → ***Have*** *ye* ***not*** *seen a false vision, and* ***have*** *ye* ***not*** *spoken a lying divination* gives both *have*s to the `הֲ` and both *not*s to the `לֹא`, while each verb keeps only its own subject pronoun and lexical verb. This is rule 55's reading of a subordinator the ASV rewrites over a coordinated clause, applied to the particle that governs one. The second occurrence is neither `added` under rule 30 nor folded into the second clause's verb under rule 17. **Where in that first clause the morpheme stands is no part of the condition, and neither is a repetition of the same English word.** What licenses the second occurrence is that the morpheme's scope reaches the second clause, so a negative predicating of a verbless clause governs a coordinated colon from the middle of the first as readily as from its head, and the ASV answers it with whatever word carries the negation where that colon opens — a *Nor* or a *neither* as readily as a second *not*. `שְׂאֵת פְּנֵי־רָשָׁע לֹא־טוֹב לְהַטּוֹת צַדִּיק בַּמִּשְׁפָּט` → *To respect the person of the wicked is* ***not*** *good,* ***Nor*** *to turn aside the righteous in judgment* gives both *not* and *Nor* to the one `לֹא`, headed on the first. Read the clause's scope rather than the morpheme's position, exactly as rule 51 reads a postposed *also*, and do not mark such a word `added` under rule 14's closing sentences because no conjunction stands in the second colon: what that count marks is a coordinator no morpheme answers to, and what distinguishes a *Nor* from a bare *and* is the negation the written negative is there to license, as rule 35 says in heading such a word on the negative. An ASV italic on such a word corroborates it and decides nothing, by rule 7. But read what is actually written before applying it: where the Hebrew gaps the morpheme in the second line and a waw stands there, and the ASV writes a single word carrying both the coordination and the negation, rule 35 governs and that word goes to the waw, as `לֹא עֲלִיתֶם ... וַתִּגְדְרוּ` → *Ye have not gone up ..., neither built up* leaves the *neither* to the waw alone; and where no conjunction morpheme stands in that colon at all, rule 35's fusion has no coordination to rest on and the distributed reading above governs.

## Constructions that recur across the Old Testament

26. Read `בֵּין X וּבֵין Y` by the connective the ASV actually writes. Where it is recast as *X from Y*, the second `בֵּין` carries *from*, while the first `בֵּין` and its waw are `unrepresented`. Where the ASV preserves *between X and Y*, the first `בֵּין` takes *between*, the waw takes *and*, and the repeated second `בֵּין` is `unrepresented`. Do not transfer the *and* to the repeated preposition: the waw is the morpheme that licenses it. **A verse may write more than one such pair, and the count runs pair by pair.** Where the ASV writes a *between* for each pair — `בֵּין הַקֹּדֶשׁ וּבֵין הַחֹל וּבֵין הַטָּמֵא וּבֵין הַטָּהוֹר` → *between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean* — each written *between* belongs to the `בֵּין` that opens the pair it stands before, only the `בֵּין` repeated inside a pair is `unrepresented`, and every waw keeps the *and* written for it. Do not read *the repeated second `בֵּין`* above as every `בֵּין` after the first in the phrase: count the ASV's *between*s against the pairs and read which pair each opens, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s. **Hebrew also writes the second member with `לְ` in place of a repeated `בֵּין`, and the same reading follows the morpheme that is actually there.** In `בֵּין X לְ Y` → *between X and Y* the `בֵּין` takes *between* and the `לְ` takes the *and*, exactly as the waw does above, because the `לְ` is what stands where the connective stands and no waw is present to claim it: `בֵּין שֶׂה לָשֶׂה` → *between sheep and sheep* is the shape. Do not mark such a `לְ` `unrepresented` in order to leave the *and* `added`, and do not reach for rule 14's last sentence: that sentence governs a connective no morpheme answers to, and here one does. **Where the phrase runs on into further `לְ` members the ASV writes as bare coordinated nouns, only the `לְ` standing where the ASV writes its connective takes one.** `בֵּין שֶׂה לָשֶׂה לָאֵילִים וְלָעַתּוּדִים` → *between sheep and sheep, the rams and the he goats* gives *and* to the first `לְ`, leaves the two later ones `unrepresented` under rule 40, and gives the second written *and* to the waw. Count the phrase's connective morphemes against the ASV's connectives and read which pair of members each stands between, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s. **A `מִן … עַד` range is read on the same terms, by the morpheme that stands where the ASV's connective stands.** Where the Hebrew writes the second endpoint with a waw, that waw takes the *and* and the `עַד` is `unrepresented` under rule 40, which is the reading rule 14 gives `מֵעוֹף הַשָּׁמַיִם וְעַד־בְּהֵמָה` → ***both*** *the birds of the heavens* ***and*** *the beasts*. Where no waw is written, the bare `עַד` is what stands where the connective stands and it takes the *and* the ASV has written, exactly as the `לְ` member does above: `מִזָּכָר עַד נְקֵבָה` → ***both*** *male* ***and*** *female* gives *both* to the `מִן` under rule 14 and *and* to the `עַד`. Do not read rule 14's example as leaving every `עַד` `unrepresented`: what goes unrepresented there is a `עַד` whose connective a waw has already answered for, and a `עַד` no waw stands beside is not that. The corpus aligns a `עַד` to *and* in eight units across four chapters, including the waw-less `מֵאָדָם עַד־בְּהֵמָה` of GEN.6.7 and GEN.7.23, and the opening `מִן` still takes the *both* in either case, as its own ten corpus units do. **But that sentence disposes of the range's connective and no more, and a `עַד` for which the ASV has written a preposition of its own is not `unrepresented`.** What the waw answers for is the coordination, so where the ASV writes the *and* for the waw and an English preposition besides before the second endpoint's own word, two English words answer to two morphemes and neither is a surplus: the waw takes the connective and the `עַד` takes the word written for the extent relation and heads on it, while the endpoint noun keeps its own English. `מֵעַתָּה וְעַד־עוֹלָם` → *From this time forth* ***and for*** *evermore* divides that way, giving *and* to the waw, *for* to the `עַד` and *evermore* to `עוֹלָם`. This is the division rule 16 already makes in `וְעַד־בַּעַל גָּד` → ***even unto*** *Baal gad*, where the waw takes the bare *even* and the `עַד` takes *unto*, and it is the same division that rule writes for `וְעַד מִשְׂרְפוֹת מַיִם` → ***and unto*** *Misrephoth maim*; read whichever connective the ASV wrote for the waw and leave the relational word to the preposition in either case. Rule 40 is what decides the preposition, as it decides every preposition: ask whether the ASV spends an English word on what that `עַד` contributes, and mark it `unrepresented` only where nothing is written for its own relation, which is what the bare-noun second endpoint of `וְעַד־בְּהֵמָה` above is. Do not fold such a preposition's word into the endpoint noun, into the opening `מִן`, or into the waw that already holds a connective of its own, for the reason rule 51 gives about a waw holding an English word of its own and rule 42 about a surplus *and*: count the phrase's English words against the morphemes that could carry them before marking any of them.
27. The `לְ` of `לֵאמֹר` has no English realization and is its own `unrepresented` unit.
28. Where the ASV renders a construct chain attributively rather than with *of* — `עֵץ פְּרִי` → *fruit trees* — follow the ASV rather than imposing rule 19.
29. A construct noun cannot take the article, so a pointed `בַּ` before one is preposition-only and the English *the* is supplied. Follow MACULA's morphology rather than the pointing. Where the chain's *nomen rectum* does carry an article, that morpheme takes the *the* the ASV writes for the chain even though English fronts it to the phrase's left edge and renders the rectum anarthrously — `לֻחֹת הָאֲבָנִים` → *the two tables of stone*, `כּוּר הַבַּרְזֶל` → *the furnace of iron*. Supply a *the* on a construct noun only where no article morpheme is present, or for a second English *the* in a multi-level chain, which the article's own leaves for the outer construct: `לְבַב כָּל בְּנֵי הָאָדָם` → *the hearts of all the children of men*. **Fronting is what the anarthrous rendering forces, not a preference for the phrase's left edge.** Where the ASV instead renders the rectum arthrously, writing a *the* before the construct and another before the rectum — `מִשְׁפַּחַת הַקְּהָתִי` → *the families of the Kohathites*, `בְּנֵי הַקְּהָתִי` → *the sons of the Kohathites*, `שְׂדֵה הָעִיר` → *the fields of the city*, `מִשְׁפְּחוֹת הַלֵּוִי` → *the families of the Levites* — nothing is fronted: the article takes the *the* standing before its own word and the construct takes a supplied one, exactly as an outer construct does in a multi-level chain. Count the ASV's *the*s and read which word each stands before, as rule 47 reads which word its single *the* stands before. **But that count governs a rectum the ASV renders arthrously, and a multi-level chain whose innermost rectum is left with no determiner at all is the fronting case, however many constructs stand over it.** Where two or more construct nouns stand over a rectum carrying an article, the ASV writes that rectum with no determiner of its own, and it writes a *the* before more than one of the chain's English nouns, the article's *the* is the one standing before the word of the noun the article's own word immediately depends on, and the outer construct takes the supplied second *the* this rule allows it, the noun between them keeping only its own word and the *of* rule 19 gives it. `אֹצְרוֹת בֵּית הָאֱלֹהִים` → *the treasuries of the house of God* gives *the treasuries* to `אוֹצָר`, *the* to the article, *of house* to `בַּיִת` and *of God* to `אֱלֹהִים`, exactly as `לְבַב כָּל בְּנֵי הָאָדָם` → *the hearts of all the children of men* gives *the hearts* to the outer construct and the *the* before *children* to the article. Do not read the counting direction above as handing that inner *the* to the construct noun it stands before, which would leave a present article morpheme holding nothing while a written *the* went to a supplied reading, against rule 2 and against the priority rules 46 and 47 give a morpheme that is there. Read the rectum's own determiner first, as the sentences above direct: where the ASV writes a *the* or another determiner before the rectum's own word nothing is fronted and the count governs as before, and where it writes none the article's *the* is fronted one level and this paragraph governs. **And a rectum the ASV renders indefinitely is not an anarthrous one.** Where the article morpheme stands on the rectum, the ASV writes an indefinite article before the rectum's own word, and the phrase's single *the* stands before the construct — `נֶפֶשׁ הָאָדָם` → *the body of a man* — nothing is fronted: the *a* is that noun's own under rule 15, one word is not determined twice, and the article is `unrepresented` under this rule's last sentence while the construct takes a supplied *the*. What forces fronting is a rectum the ASV leaves with no determiner at all, as `לֻחֹת הָאֲבָנִים` → *the two tables of stone* is, so read which determiner stands before the rectum's word before counting the phrase's *the*s. **A construct relation the ASV recasts as an English possessive is not a fronting either.** Where the ASV writes the rectum as a possessive standing before its head noun — `מִלַּת מַלְכָּא` → *the king's matter*, `שַׁלִּיטָא דִּי מַלְכָּא` → *the king's captain*, `שַׁעַר הַמֶּלֶךְ` → *the king's gate* — the *the* at the phrase's left edge stands before the possessor's own word, because an English possessive expression takes the determiner of the possessor and not of the head noun. So the article on the rectum takes it and the construct noun keeps only its own word, a present article morpheme taking its English before a supplied one does; supply a *the* on the construct only where the ASV writes a second one, as `רַב טַבָּחַיָּא דִּי מַלְכָּא` → *the captain of the king's guard* does. Do not read the left edge as evidence of fronting: what fronting answers to is an anarthrous rectum, and here the rectum is the very word the *the* determines. **Read that as the determiner of the possessive, not as the word *the*, and read it whether or not the possessor carries an article of its own.** An English possessive expression takes the determiner of the possessor whatever that determiner is, so an indefinite *a* standing at the phrase's left edge stands before the possessor's word exactly as a *the* does, and where the possessor's word carries no article morpheme the determiner the ASV supplies still goes to that word rather than to the construct noun: `נְשִׂיא בֵית־אָב` → *a prince of* ***a fathers*** *house* gives *a fathers* to `אָב` and *of ... house* to `בַּיִת`, discontinuous around the possessive under rule 25. This is rule 15's supplied determiner placed by this rule's own reasoning — the noun that licenses an English *a* in such a phrase is the possessor, since *a father's house* is the house of *a* father — and the construct noun takes a determiner of its own only where the ASV writes a second one, on the terms just given. A `דִּי` or other relational morpheme standing between the two nouns is rendered inside the possessive and shares the rectum's unit under rule 30. **And a chain the ASV renders attributively under rule 28 places its supplied determiner on these same terms.** Where the ASV writes the construct noun as an attributive standing before the rectum's own word — `קוֹמַת אֲרָזָיו` → *the tall cedars thereof*, `מִבְחוֹר בְּרֹשָׁיו` → *the choice fir trees thereof*, `יְרַק דֶּשֶׁא` → *the green herb*, `בְּתוּלַת בַּת־צִיּוֹן` → *The virgin daughter of Zion* — and no article morpheme stands in the phrase, the single written *the* stands before the construct noun's word and that noun takes it as a supplied one, exactly as it does where the same chain is written with *of*. Do not hand it to the rectum on the ground that the rectum is the head noun of the English phrase: rule 15's direction to read the noun of the expression rather than its leftmost word governs a numeral and the noun it counts, where the numeral is not a noun the ASV determines at all, and it does not reach a construct chain, whose determiners this rule counts. Count the ASV's *the*s and read which word each stands before, as above: where the ASV writes one for each, each noun takes the one standing before its own word, and where an article morpheme stands on the rectum the fronting sentences above govern as before. The unit still heads on the word carrying the lexeme under rule 31, so `קוֹמַת` → *the tall* heads on *tall*. **And the same holds where the ASV renders the rectum attributively instead and the construct noun is the English head.** Where the rectum's word stands first and the construct's word after it — `דְּבַר עֶצֶב` → *a grievous word*, `אִישׁ חֵמָה` → *A wrathful man*, `מַחְשְׁבוֹת רָע` → *Evil devices* — and no article morpheme stands in the phrase, the single supplied determiner is still the construct noun's, because what this rule supplies is a determiner on the construct noun and not on whichever of the two words the ASV has put first. Do not hand it to the rectum on the ground that it stands before the rectum's word: the counting sentences above weigh the ASV's written determiners against the article morphemes standing in the phrase, and where no article morpheme stands there at all there is nothing to count, so the preceding paragraph's reasoning governs in either English order. Where the ASV writes a determiner for each word, each noun takes the one standing before its own word, as above. Read MACULA's morphology before applying any of this, since rule 15's direction to take the noun of the expression governs a noun with an absolute modifier standing beside it — `לֵב שָׂמֵחַ` → *A glad heart*, `שׁוֹר אָבוּס` → *a stalled ox* — where no construct relation is written and this rule's count does not arise. **A rectum that is a proper name is not an exception.** An article MACULA segments off a toponym takes the ASV's *the* on the same terms as one on a common noun, and is never set aside as lexicalized in the name: `בְּנֵי הָרָמָה` → *The children of Ramah* and `אַנְשֵׁי בֵית אֵל וְהָעָי` → *The men of Beth el and Ai* give the single written *the* to the article and leave the construct noun bare. **But an article the ASV spells into its name is already rendered.** Where the rectum is transliterated with its article — `בְּנֵי הַסֹּפֶרֶת` → *the children of Hassophereth* — the article shares the name's unit under rule 30, it has taken its *the* inside that English word rather than as a separate one, and the *the* the ASV does write stands before the construct as a supplied one. Read the transliteration before counting: nothing is fronted from an article English has already spelled out. Mark the article `unrepresented` only where the ASV writes no *the* for the phrase at all, as in `הֶעָרִים הָאֵלֶּה` → *these cities*.
30. Where English fuses several morphemes into one word, such as *wherein*, they share a unit **only when that shared English rendering carries the sense of every morpheme placed in it**. Reserve `added` for English that no morpheme licenses at all. This governs a fixed multi-word Hebrew expression the ASV renders with one English word too, but it never absorbs a morpheme whose sense that rendering does not carry: in `דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים` → *chronicles*, the two lexical nouns may share *chronicles*, while the separately segmented article takes a written *the* or is its own `unrepresented` unit; `אִם לֹא` → *surely* may share one unit when *surely* carries both morphemes' senses. Such a fused expression is headed on the morpheme carrying the lexeme, rather than leaving another morpheme whose sense the rendering carries `unrepresented`. The one-English-word condition is load-bearing in both directions: `בְּיַד` → *by* and `מִפְּנֵי` → *because of* fuse, but where the ASV spends a second English word on the expression — `הֵשִׁיב פָּנִים` → *deny* fuses while the same idiom as *say ... nay* does not — the condition is unmet and rule 2 splits it. **Read that condition as one English rendering, not one English token.** A compound English preposition written in two or three words — *because of*, *by reason of*, *out of* — is a single rendering of which no morpheme owns a part, and it fuses; so does an expression whose unit additionally carries a copula or other word the construction hands to the whole of it under another rule, as `לִפְנֵי` → *was before*. What defeats the condition is English the ASV spends on one morpheme in particular, so that each morpheme has a rendering of its own: `לִפְנֵי` → *to meet* gives *to* to the preposition and *meet* to `פָּנֶה`, and `עַד לְ` → *until there was* gives *until* to `עַד` and the predicate's supplied copula to the `לְ` under rule 49, while the same `עַד לְ` → *to* fuses. Test by subtraction as rules 16 and 31 do: drop the second English word and ask whether a particular morpheme has lost its rendering or only the expression as a whole is diminished. What the second morpheme is tagged does not matter; a preposition before a construct noun fuses on the same terms as a compound preposition. **And such a compound's single English word carries the preposition's own relation even where no part of it states that relation separately.** Where `מִן`, `לְ`, `בְּ` or `עַל` stands before a construct noun and the ASV writes one English preposition for the pair, the condition is met and the two share a unit headed on the construct noun: `מִפְּנֵי` → *before*, `לִפְנֵי` → *before*, `עַל־פְּנֵי` → *upon*, `בְּקֶרֶב` → *among*. **Read that list as the commonest prepositions and not as a closed one:** what licenses the fusion is that a preposition stands on a construct noun and one English rendering carries the pair, so a `כְּ`, an `אֶל`, an `אַחַר` or any other preposition in that position is read on exactly the same terms, and `כְּפִי` → *as* fuses as `לְפִי` and `מִפְּנֵי` do. Do not read this rule's first sentence as requiring a separate English trace of the ablative, directional or locative sense before fusing, and do not mark the preposition `unrepresented` beside a construct noun holding the whole word: that is the outcome the fusion sentence above forbids and the one rule 22's closing paragraph forbids for a morpheme whose sense the ASV has written inside a word, and the subtraction test finds no second English word for either morpheme to own. **The same prohibition runs the other way, and the construct noun is the side more often mistaken.** Do not mark the construct noun `unrepresented` beside a preposition holding the whole rendering either: the noun of such a compound is not a word the ASV ever writes alone, its sense is inside the one English word exactly as the preposition's is, and the reasoning that forbids the one direction forbids the other, so the two share a unit headed on the construct noun as before. Read whether any English word in the expression renders that noun's own lexeme, which is this rule's subtraction test again: `כְּפִיךָ` → *even as* ... *art* writes nothing for `פֶּה` in particular, so the pair fuses and the suffix keeps only the *thou* rule 18 writes for it, while `לִפְנֵי` → *to meet* gives `פָּנֶה` a word of its own and rule 2 splits it. A pronominal suffix on the construct noun is no part of the fusion in either case: it takes whatever English pronoun the ASV writes for it under rule 18, or goes `unrepresented` under rule 40 where the ASV writes none, and its presence is not evidence that the preposition and its noun have separate renderings. Where the ASV instead writes a word for each — `מִפְּנֵי` → *from before*, `מִקֶּרֶב` → *from among* — each morpheme has a rendering of its own, the condition is unmet, and rule 2 splits them; what tells the two shapes apart is independently recoverable ownership under rule 2, not the raw number of English words or which senses an English preposition leaves implicit. The corpus fuses `מִפְּנֵי` to *before* in fifteen units and to *because of* in seventy-five, `לִפְנֵי` to *before* in five hundred and ninety-seven, and a preposition with `תּוֹךְ` or `קֶרֶב` to *among* in twenty-seven. Contiguity is part of the condition for such an expression: a verb and the preposition it governs, held apart by the subject or the object, is not one. It is not a condition on fusion generally — rule 35's *Nor* and *never* fuse across intervening material, because there the one English word still carries every morpheme's sense. `גָּדַל ... מִן` → *exceeded* and `הוֹסִיף ... אֶל` → *exceed* are a verb whose preposition the ASV's comparative recasting leaves unrendered, so rule 2 splits them and the preposition is `unrepresented`. Rule 22 governs a repetition of the *same* element, not the parts of an idiom. **The expression need not be an idiom, and a full lexical noun fuses on the same terms as a function morpheme.** Where Hebrew writes a generic head noun before an attributive that names the class — `אִשָּׁה` or `אִישׁ` before a participle or an adjective — and the ASV renders the pair by one English word, the two share one unit headed on the morpheme carrying the lexeme: `אִשָּׁה זוֹנָה` → *harlot's* is one unit headed on `זוֹנָה`, with the indefinite article going to that nominal expression under rule 15, and `אִשָּׁה` is not marked `unrepresented` beside it. The one-English-word condition and its subtraction test decide this exactly as they decide a compound preposition — dropping the English word diminishes only the expression as a whole, and no morpheme owns a part of it — so where the ASV instead spends a word on each, as `אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה` → *man of war*, the condition is unmet and rule 2 splits it. Do not read *fixed multi-word Hebrew expression* above as a requirement that the pair be idiomatic. **And a demonstrative modifying a noun is such an attributive.** Where Hebrew writes a noun with the demonstrative that modifies it — `הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה` and the like — and the ASV renders the pair by one English word, the two share one unit on exactly the terms just given, headed on the morpheme whose lexeme that word most directly writes, which is ordinarily the demonstrative; an article standing in the phrase takes a written *the* under rule 29 or is its own `unrepresented` unit where the ASV writes none, exactly as the article of `דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים` → *chronicles* is. `הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה` → *thus* and `הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה` → *that* both fuse, and JDG.8.1 and JDG.8.3 are the two renderings in one chapter. **And a single English word that is one morpheme's own ordinary rendering does not defeat the condition.** What defeats it is a second English word the ASV spends on one morpheme in particular, so where one word alone stands for the whole expression the pair fuses even though that word is what the ASV writes for one of the morphemes elsewhere: this is rule 40's ruling that a preposition and its noun recast as one English adverb fuse though the word is the noun's ordinary rendering, and it is this rule's own `אִשָּׁה זוֹנָה` → *harlot's*, where *harlot's* is `זוֹנָה`'s own word and `אִשָּׁה` is still not marked `unrepresented`. Do not mark the other morpheme `unrepresented` on that ground, and do not reach for rule 22's first sentence, which governs an element the ASV has left unwritten and not one whose sense stands inside a fused word. Where the ASV instead writes a word for each — `הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה` → *this thing* — the condition is unmet, rule 2 splits them, and each morpheme takes the word written for it. **And an article standing on the noun of such a fused expression shares the unit where the one English rendering carries its determination.** Where an article, the noun it determines, and a prefixed preposition or a directive `he` standing with them are rendered by a single English adverb — `הַיּוֹם` → *to day*, *now* or *ago*, `כַּיּוֹם` → *first*, `הַחוּצָה` → *abroad* — the English names the definite thing the article determines and no word is written for any one of those morphemes apart from the others, so all of them share one unit headed on the morpheme carrying the lexeme, exactly as rule 40 fuses `בֶּאֱמֶת` → *faithfully* and rule 32 fuses a host with its directive `he`. Do not set the article outside such a unit on the strength of this rule's `דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים` sentence: what that sentence governs is an article determining the *nomen rectum* of a chain whose one English word writes the two nouns' lexemes and says nothing of their determination, and there the ASV's own *the* stands ready for the article whenever it is written. Test by the subtraction this rule already runs — drop the English word and ask whether the determination as well as the lexeme has gone unsaid. Where the ASV instead writes a determiner of its own for the article, the condition is unmet, rule 2 splits the expression, and each morpheme takes the word written for it: `כְּהַיּוֹם` → *at this time* gives *at* to the `כְּ`, *this* to the article under rule 41 and *time* to the noun, and one chapter may write both shapes, as 1SA.9.13 and 1SA.9.27 do. The corpus aligns `הַיּוֹם` to *to day* as one article-and-noun unit headed on *day* in ten units, and reads `כָּל־הַיָּמִים` → *for ever* as one unit with the article inside it in seven. **For this rule, a particle's single idiomatic English rendering includes the pronouns the English formula requires.** The entreaty particle `נָא` → *I beseech thee* or *we pray thee* takes the complete formula, headed on *beseech* or *pray*; its English subject and object pronouns are not `added`, because they realize the particle's one entreaty rather than supply arguments of the neighbouring imperative. Where the ASV's formula omits an object, as *I pray*, align only what it writes.

31. Where one Hebrew substantive is rendered by a multi-word English phrase, head it on the word carrying the lexeme rather than on the word the ASV supplies to complete the phrase. Decide which is which by dropping each word in turn: the head is the one whose loss loses the lexeme. `יַבָּשָׁה` → *dry land* heads on *dry*, because *land* alone is any land; `בָּמָה` → *high place*, *high places* heads on *high*, because *place* alone is any place; so too `קֹדֶשׁ` → *holy place*, `מִסְתָּר` → *secret places*, `תִּמֹרָה` → *palm trees*, `עֹלָה` → *burnt offerings*. **Neither part of speech nor position decides this.** The supplied word is usually the English phrase's head noun and the lexeme-bearing word usually an attributive adjective standing first, but do not move a head onto a generic noun merely because it is the noun — that reasoning would head *dry land* on *land* — and do not reach for the first word as a rule. Where the ASV supplies the modifier instead, the same test moves the head the other way: `תַּנִּין` → *sea monsters* heads on *monsters*, because *sea* is a specifier added to a word that already names the creature. **Where the subtraction test ties, read the ASV's own use of the English noun.** A class noun the ASV writes alone for a Hebrew word of that same class — in the verse, in the passage, or as its ordinary rendering of the word before you — is not a supplied generic but the word that realizes the lexeme, and a modifier standing beside it is the ASV's specifier distinguishing one class word from another, so the head stays on the class noun: JOB.4.10-11 writes a bare *lion* for `אַרְיֵה` and *the fierce lion*, *the young lions* and *The old lion* for `שַׁחַל`, `כְּפִיר` and `לַיִשׁ`, and all four head on *lion*; JOB.4.17 writes *a man* for `גֶּבֶר` and *mortal man* for `אֱנוֹשׁ`, and both head on *man*; `יֶתֶר` → *tent cord* heads on *cord*. Where the ASV never writes that noun alone for the word — *place* for `בָּמָה`, *sleep* for `תַּרְדֵּמָה` → *deep sleep* — the noun is the supplied generic and the head is the modifier, as above. **The ASV's *man-servant*, *maid-servant*, *men-servants*, and *maid-servants* always head on *servant* or *servants*.** Treat *man*, *men*, and *maid* alike as the compounds' gender specifiers; an article or *of* in the same unit is likewise never the head. Ask which of the two the English noun is before choosing, and say in the note that the other reading was available. Where the phrase also carries an article, *of*, or *a*, rules 19 and 29 place those; they are never the head. **But read whether a second source nominal stands in the phrase before sending that *of* to rule 19.** What that rule places is the relation between two nominals the Hebrew writes, and an *of* the ASV writes inside a phrase it has supplied for a single substantive is not that. Where the ASV completes the substantive with a generic head noun and writes an *of* between that noun and the word carrying the lexeme — `זָהָב` → ***things of*** *gold*, `עֵץ` → ***things of*** *wood* — the supplied noun and its *of* both join that substantive's unit, neither is `added`, and the head stays on the word carrying the lexeme, so those two head on *gold* and *wood*. This is what rule 46 already gives a bare participle or adjective in giving its supplied noun phrase *whatever article, relative or* of *the phrase needs*, holding for a substantive of any kind. Decide it by the subtraction test above: drop the English *of* and ask whether a relation between two source nouns has gone unstated, or whether only a phrase the ASV supplied for one noun has been broken in two. Where a second source nominal does stand, rule 19 governs as before and the *of* is the rectum's, whatever generic the ASV supplied to expand the first noun — `כָּל־עֵץ` → *any kind* ***of*** *tree* gives *of tree* to the rectum while `כֹּל` keeps *any kind* — and an ASV italic on the supplied words corroborates the supply and decides nothing, by rule 7. Two riders. An article morpheme standing before such a phrase takes the written *the* under rules 29 and 47, a present morpheme taking its English before a supplied reading does, so `לָעֵצִים` → *for* ***the*** *things of wood* gives the *the* to the article and *things of wood* to the noun. And a preposition the ASV writes before the whole phrase to state what it contributes to its clause is its own morpheme's, or rule 33's where no preposition morpheme stands, and is no part of the supplied expression. **A substantival numeral is such a substantive.** Where the ASV completes a numeral standing for what it counts with a generic noun the Hebrew does not write, that noun joins the numeral's unit and the head stays on the numeral, which carries the lexeme: `אֶחָד` → *one* ***portion*** in a list of tribal allotments, `אֶחָד` → *the* ***first day***. Rule 33's `added` case is the other one, and what the supplied noun does tells them apart: there the numeral is a quantity whose English is already complete and the noun names the unit it is counted in — the italic ***reeds*** after *five and twenty thousand* — so the numeral's own rendering loses nothing when it is dropped. **And a head noun the ASV supplies for a written noun to stand on as a possessive is such a completion too.** Where Hebrew writes an elliptical nominal whose fuller form names a head noun it leaves out — `אָבוֹת` for `בֵּית אָבוֹת` — and the ASV supplies that head noun, writing the source noun before it as an English possessive, the supplied noun joins the written noun's unit, is never `added`, and the head stays on the word carrying the lexeme: `רָאשֵׁי הָאָבוֹת` → *the heads of the* ***fathers houses*** gives *of fathers houses* to `אָב`, headed on *fathers*, while the construct noun and the article are placed by rule 29 as usual. Rule 18's closing sentence does not reach it: what that sentence marks `added` is a supplied *possessive* standing where no suffix or other possessive morpheme is written, and here the possessive is the very noun the Hebrew wrote while what the ASV supplies is the noun it stands on. Rule 33's supplied-phrase sentence does not reach it either, since that governs a preposition and a noun the Hebrew writes neither of, and a written noun governs this one; nor is it rule 20's supplied lexical verb, which predicates an event the Hebrew never wrote, while a head noun predicates nothing. Test by the subtraction this rule already runs: drop the supplied noun and ask whether a phrase the ASV supplied to complete one written noun has been broken, or whether what has gone is a thing the Hebrew never names at all. An ASV italic on such a noun corroborates the supply and decides nothing, by rule 7. But read what the Hebrew writes before reaching for this sentence, since the same phrase written in full is two nouns and rule 2 splits it — `לְבֵית אֲבוֹתָם` → *their fathers houses* gives *houses* to `בַּיִת` and *fathers* to `אָב` — and one chapter may write both shapes, as 1CH.26.13 and 1CH.26.21 do. The corpus places the supplied noun inside the written noun's unit, headed on *fathers*, in twenty-four units across the finished chapters and marks it `added` in none.
32. A paragogic or cohortative *he*, and a paragogic *nun*, have no English realization and are each their own `unrepresented` unit. **The directive or locative *he* is not one of these.** MACULA tags it `Sd`, it carries a motion or a place its host does not, and it takes whatever English the ASV spends on that. Where the ASV writes a word of its own for it — *to*, *unto*, *into*, *toward*, *in*, *upon* — it is its own aligned unit by rule 11 and heads on that word. Where the ASV instead writes a single English word for the host and the *he* together, the two share a unit under rule 30, headed on the morpheme carrying the lexeme: `שָׁמָּה` → *thither*, `הַחוּצָה` → *out*, `הַבַּיְתָה` → *home*, `קֵדְמָה` → *eastward*, `בַּיְתָה` → *inward*, `מַעְלָה` → *upward*, *above*. Mark it `unrepresented` only where the ASV writes nothing for it, which is usually where another morpheme has already taken the English that would have carried the direction, as where `עַד` takes the *to* before a bare place name. Do not reach for this rule's first sentence because the token is a *he*: read the morphology, and where the tag is `Sd` read what the ASV writes.
33. An English function word the ASV supplies for an adverbial accusative belongs to the noun whose case-function licenses it — *as a bird*, *to your mountain* — and is never `added`. **An appositional or measure nominal the ASV introduces with a supplied preposition is read the same way.** Where a noun or numeral stands in the clause with no preposition and no object marker on it, and the ASV writes an *of* or a *for* before it to state what that nominal contributes, the word is that nominal's own rendering: `אֹרֶךְ חֲמִשָּׁה וְעֶשְׂרִים אֶלֶף` → *the length ... of five and twenty thousand*, `תֹּכֶן אֶחָד` → *of one measure*, `לַאֲחֻזָּה עֶשְׂרִים לְשָׁכֹת` → *for a possession ..., for twenty chambers*, `חַטָּאת שְׂעִיר עִזִּים` → *a he goat ... for a sin offering*. It goes to the word it stands before, which is the noun or numeral opening that phrase, and the unit still heads on the word carrying the lexeme under rules 24 and 31; the supplied preposition is never the head. **Read *an *of* or a *for** as any preposition the ASV writes for the nominal's own contribution, and not as a closed list.** What licenses the word is that a bare nominal stands in the clause and the English needs a word to say what it contributes there, so read the condition and not the lemma, exactly as rule 40 does for the preposition it silences: a comparative *as* before a nominal the ASV construes as a comparison — `פַּלְגֵי מַיִם` → ***as*** *the watercourses* — an agent *by* before a bare nominal the ASV construes with a passive participle — `מְבַקְשֵׁי מָוֶת` → *driven to and fro* ***by*** *them that seek death* — and a *with* or an *in* before a nominal standing in a `מִן` comparison — `אֵשֶׁת מִדְיָנִים` → ***with*** *a contentious woman*, `בֵּית חָבֶר` → ***in*** *a wide house* — all go to the nominal on the same terms as an *of* or a *for*, and this rule's first sentence already writes *as* and *to* for the adverbial accusative. Three limits, each stated where it belongs. A word an object marker stands to take is the marker's under rule 16, and an *as for* the ASV writes before a topicalized phrase no marker and no preposition stands on is `added` under that rule rather than this one, since a nominal's fronting is not a case-function. A preposition the English verb's valency needs to reach its own bare object is the verb's under rule 44, and this rule's own closing paragraph disposes of one a predicate's valency needs. And a coordinating or subordinating connective the ASV writes beside such a phrase is not a word spent on the nominal at all: rules 14 and 55 count those against the morphemes that could carry them. **A preposition standing elsewhere in the clause that has already taken an English word of its own does not make such a word a surplus.** Rule 52's count governs two English words offered to one morpheme, and here each phrase has a rendering of its own, so a written `לְ` rendered *for a possession* does not silence the *for* the ASV writes before the phrase standing beside it. **But an English preposition the ASV writes again over a member the Hebrew coordinates under one written preposition is that preposition's, and not the bare nominal's.** Hebrew gaps a preposition in the later members of a coordination where English rewrites it, and the word the ASV writes there is the distributed scope of the one written morpheme: it joins that morpheme's possibly discontinuous unit under rule 25, however much stands between and whatever word the ASV chose for the members in between. `בְּכֹל מַלְכֵי יְהוּדָה וַאֲשֶׁר הָיוּ לְפָנָיו` → *among all the kings of Judah, nor* ***among*** *them that were before him* gives the second *among* to the one `בְּ`, and `בַּחְלַח וּבְחָבוֹר נְהַר גּוֹזָן וְעָרֵי מָדָי` → *in Halah and on the Habor the river of Gozan and* ***in*** *the cities of the Medes* gives the third preposition to the first `בְּ`, across the second member's own `בְּ` holding the *on* written for it. What tells this from the preceding sentences is order and repetition together, not that the nominal is bare: this sentence governs a member coordinated after a written preposition of its own phrase whose English the ASV has simply written again, and the earlier sentences govern a nominal standing before any such preposition, or in a phrase where none is written at all. So read whether a preposition of that phrase stands earlier in the coordination before giving the word to the noun — `הַנִּמְצָא בֵית־יְהוָה וּבְאֹצְרוֹת בֵּית הַמֶּלֶךְ` → *that was found* ***in*** *the house of Jehovah, and in the treasures of the king's house* leaves its first *in* to `בַּיִת` under this rule's first sentence, because the written `בְּ` stands after it and nothing is being repeated. Test by subtraction as elsewhere: ask whether the English word states what a bare nominal contributes to its clause, or restates a relation a written preposition already carries over that member. **But the noun must be present.** Where the ASV supplies the head noun as well as the function word, no morpheme is there to carry either, and the whole supplied phrase is its own `added` unit under rule 30: `חֲמֵשׁ מֵאוֹת בַּחֲמֵשׁ מֵאוֹת` → *five hundred* ***in length*** *by five hundred* ***in breadth*** supplies both the preposition and the dimension noun in a clause whose Hebrew names neither, exactly as the italic ***reeds*** of `חֲמִשָּׁה וְעֶשְׂרִים אֶלֶף` supplies a measure noun. Test by subtraction as rules 16 and 31 do: drop the English and ask whether a Hebrew noun standing in the clause has lost the relation it bears, or whether what has gone is a noun the Hebrew never wrote. An ASV italic on such a word corroborates it either way and decides nothing, by rule 7. **And a bare noun that completes a predicate's own valency is not an adverbial accusative at all.** Where a verb or a predicate adjective governs a bare noun as its complement — no preposition and no object marker on it — and the English predicate can reach that complement only through a preposition the ASV has written, that word answers to the predicate's valency and joins the predicate's unit under rule 44, while the noun keeps its own English: `מָלֵאתָ` → *thou art full* ***of*** *the judgment* and `מָלֵא דָשֶׁן` → *would be full* ***of*** *fatness* both give the *of* to `מָלֵא`, headed on *full*, and leave `דִין` and `דֶשֶׁן` their own words. Decide it by asking whether the ASV had a relation to choose: where the English predicate admits no other word before such a complement — *full of* is the only way English writes it — the preposition is the predicate's, and where the word states what the noun contributes to the clause and another would have served, this rule governs and the word is the noun's, as it does for `יָזֹקּוּ מָטָר` → *distil* ***in*** *rain* and `כַּבִּיר כֹּחַ` → *mighty* ***in*** *strength*. Read the English predicate rather than where the noun stands, since Hebrew writes such a complement before its predicate as readily as after it, and JOB.36.16 and JOB.36.17 are the two shapes in one chapter. **But run the direct-object question before that test, because the test cannot answer it.** Where the bare noun is the direct object of a verb standing in the clause, rule 44's supplied-preposition clause governs and the word joins that verb's unit, headed on the lexical verb, whatever preposition the ASV chose and whether or not another would have served. A recasting that makes a transitive Hebrew verb intransitive in English always leaves the translator a relation to choose, so the availability of another word says nothing about which morpheme licenses the one written, and only what governs the noun in the Hebrew can tell the two apart. `וְיוֹסֶף לֶקַח` → *he will increase* ***in*** *learning* and `מְיַשְּׁרִים אֹרְחוֹתָם` → *go right* ***on*** *their ways* both give the word to the verb, because `לֶקַח` and `אֹרַח` are what those verbs govern, and rule 44's own examples — *have done violence to my law*, *hast greedily gained of thy neighbors* — are the same construction written without the recasting. What the preceding sentence governs is a complement no verb takes as its object: the accusative of manner of `יָזֹקּוּ מָטָר`, the accusative of specification of `כַּבִּיר כֹּחַ`, and the `מָלֵא` case, where an adjective and not a verb is the predicate. Read MACULA's clause for what governs the noun, as this rule's third limit already directs in deferring to rule 44, and where no verb governs it the preceding sentences stand as before. **But that deferral reaches a governing verb the ASV has itself rendered as an English verb, and no other.** Rule 44's clause places a preposition the English verb's valency needs to reach its own object, so it presupposes an English verb doing the reaching. Where instead the ASV dissolves the governing verb into the noun's own English phrase — writing it as an attributive adjective, as a participle, or as any word that takes no object of its own — no English verb reaches that noun at all, rule 44's clause has nothing to govern, and the preceding sentences stand: the supplied preposition states what the whole recast phrase contributes to the clause the ASV has left standing, and it belongs to the noun. `הֵעֵזָה פָנֶיהָ וַתֹּאמַר לוֹ` → *And* ***with*** *an impudent face she said unto him* gives *with an face* to `פָּנֶה`, the indefinite article the noun's own under rule 15, and leaves `עָזַז` the bare *impudent*, though `פָּנֶה` is what that verb governs in the Hebrew. Test by subtraction as elsewhere — drop the English preposition and ask whether an English verb has lost the reach to its object, or whether a phrase standing beside the clause's own verb has lost the relation it bears to it. Read what the ASV made of the verb before running the direct-object question, since a recasting that keeps the verb a verb is what the preceding paragraph governs, and PRO.7.16 → *I have spread my couch* ***with*** *carpets* and PRO.7.13 → ***with*** *an impudent face* are the two shapes in one chapter.
40. Where the ASV's recasting leaves a preposition with no English word — the impersonal `קרא לְ` + suffix rendered as a personal passive, `וְקֹרָא לָךְ` → *and thou shalt be called*, or a dative `לְ` + suffix rendered as a bare English indirect object, `תְּנָה לִּי` → *give me* — the preposition is its own `unrepresented` unit and its suffix takes the English pronoun that realizes it, including a subject pronoun. This is rule 21's recasting clause outside the resultative `הָיָה לְ`, and it is the case where rule 18's *possessive or object* is not the whole range. **The `לְ` is the commonest case, not the condition.** The same recasting silences any preposition it leaves nothing to say: `צוּר עַל` → *besieged Tirzah* and `צִוִּיתִי עָלֶיךָ` → *commanded thee* leave `עַל` bare, `אֶל` goes the same way before a bare English object, and a `בְּ` whose object the ASV promotes to a direct object — *blew the trumpet* — keeps nothing either. Read the condition, not the lemma: ask whether the ASV spends an English word on what that preposition contributes. **And a word the ASV writes as part of a neighbouring morpheme's own compound rendering is not a word spent on the preposition.** Where a Hebrew adverb or noun standing beside the preposition is rendered by an English expression written in two or three words, that expression belongs whole to the morpheme it renders, exactly as rule 30 reads its one-English-word condition as one rendering rather than one token, and the preposition takes a word only where the ASV has written one for its own relation. `הֶחָצֵר סָבִיב לַמִּשְׁכָּן` → *the court round about the tabernacle* gives *round about* whole to `סָבִיב`, headed on *round* under rule 24, and leaves the `לְ` `unrepresented`, exactly as the coordinated `וְלַמִּזְבֵּחַ` beside it is left. Do not divide such an expression to give a present preposition something to hold: rule 11 gives each morpheme the English it licenses, and half of another morpheme's fixed rendering is not English this one licenses. Test by subtraction as elsewhere — drop the word and ask whether the preposition's own relation has gone unstated, or whether only the neighbouring expression has been broken in two. The corpus renders `סָבִיב` by *round about* headed on *round* in 144 units and by a bare *round* in two, one of them hedged and the other a doubled adverb for which the ASV writes a separate word for each occurrence, and it reads the whole compound as the adverb's own in four of the five units where a determined preposition follows it. **But read whether the preposition's own object is rendered at all before silencing it.** Every case above is one where the ASV writes English for what the preposition governs and leaves only the relation unstated — *besieged Tirzah*, *commanded thee*, *blew the trumpet*, *give me*, *round about the tabernacle*. Where instead the governed noun is itself left with no English of its own, and a single English word carries the predicate, the preposition and that noun together, rule 30's one-rendering condition is met and this rule does not reach it: the contiguous morphemes share one unit headed on the one carrying the lexeme, rather than one rendered morpheme standing beside others marked `unrepresented`, which is what rule 30 forbids for a fixed expression and what rule 22 forbids for a morpheme whose sense the ASV has written inside a fused word. `אִם־רַע בְּעֵינֶיךָ` → *if it displease thee* gives *it displease* to `רַע`, `בְּ` and `עַיִן` in one unit headed on the verb, while the suffix keeps the *thee* written for it under rule 18, exactly as `הֵשִׁיב פָּנִים` → *deny* fuses under rule 30. **And the single English word need not carry a predicate, nor its noun be a construct.** Where a preposition stands on an absolute noun and the ASV recasts the whole phrase as one English adverb — `בֶּאֱמֶת` → *faithfully*, `בְּאָחוֹר` → *back* — no word is written for the preposition apart from the noun or for the noun apart from the preposition, so rule 30's condition is met on the same terms and the two share one unit headed on the noun, which carries the lexeme. Do not mark the preposition `unrepresented` on the ground that the one English word is the noun's ordinary rendering: what tells this case from the ones above is that a single English word stands for the pair, and the subtraction test below finds the noun's own rendering gone with it. Contiguity is rule 30's condition and it is the limit here too: `גָּדַל ... מִן` → *exceeded*, where the subject or the object stands between, is split by rule 2 and its preposition is `unrepresented` as before. Test by subtraction — drop the English word and ask whether the preposition's noun as well as its relation has gone unsaid; where the noun keeps a word of its own, this rule governs and the preposition is `unrepresented`. **And where the ASV writes no pronoun for the suffix either, that suffix is its own `unrepresented` unit beside its preposition.** This rule's first sentence places a pronoun the ASV has written; a reflexive or ethical dative the ASV renders by nothing at all — `קוּמִי לָךְ` → *Rise up*, `וּלְכִי־לָךְ` → *and come away*, `הָלַךְ לוֹ` → *is over and gone* — leaves neither morpheme an English word, and each goes `unrepresented` on its own merits under rule 11. Do not read a phrasal particle standing beside the verb as the dative's rendering: *up*, *away* and *forth* are the English verb's own under rule 44, and the subtraction test rules 16 and 43 use finds the verb's rendering diminished when they are dropped while the dative's relation is left unstated either way. Read what the ASV writes rather than the lemma, since the same construction is rendered both ways: where a pronoun is written for the suffix — `לֶךְ־לְךָ` → *Get thee out* — this rule's first sentence governs as before and the suffix takes it. **And the case of that pronoun is no part of the condition.** Rule 18 gives a suffix whatever English pronoun realizes it, a possessive as readily as an object one, so a dative, benefactive or reflexive `לְ` whose relation the ASV leaves unstated is `unrepresented` whichever the ASV writes: `יַגִּיד לוֹ` → *tell* ***him*** and `נָתַן לוֹ` → *given* ***him*** leave the preposition nothing, and so do `לְרַע לוֹ` → *to* ***his*** *hurt* and `מַאֲרִיךְ לוֹ` → *prolong* ***his*** *days*, where the ASV recasts the dative as a possessive determiner on the noun standing beside it. Do not fuse such a pair under rule 30 on the ground that a possessive states the belonging the preposition carries: the subtraction test this rule already runs finds that *his* is the very word a bare suffix takes in any possessive context, so no English word is spent on the preposition's own relation, and a noun the ASV supplies for that possessive to stand on is `added` under rule 20 as before. Rule 49's fused genitive is the other case and its own closing sentence marks the boundary: there the possessive marker is written on a word that would otherwise stand bare — `לַיהוָה` → *is Jehovah's* — and the `לְ` is itself the verbless clause's predicate, whereas an adjunct dative hanging on a finite verb or a participle is this rule's.

41. The **substantival article** — `הַ` on a participle or adjective used as a noun — takes the pronoun the ASV supplies to make it substantival, and heads on that pronoun rather than on the relative that follows it. `הָרַע` → *that which was evil* is the article with *that which*, headed on *that*; `הָעֹשֶׂה` → *him that doeth* is the article with *him that*, headed on *him*. The supplied pronoun is the article's own rendering and is never `added`. The predicate word keeps its own English, including any copula the ASV supplies, which stays with it under rule 20: in *that which is right*, *is* and *right* belong to the adjective and only *that which* belongs to the article. **An attributive article the ASV renders by a relative goes the same way.** Where `הַ` stands on a participle or adjective modifying an explicit noun and the ASV writes a relative instead of a *the* — `אֹיְבֶיךָ הַקָּמִים עָלֶיךָ` → *thine enemies that rise up against thee*, `הָאִישׁ הָרַךְ` → *The man that is tender*, `הַכְּתוּבִים` → *that are written*, `הַכֹּהֵן הַמְחַטֵּא` → *The priest that offereth* — the article takes that relative and heads on it, while the participle or adjective keeps its own English together with any supplied copula, exactly as above. **But read where the ASV has put the attributive before reaching for that sentence.** Where it renders the attributive prenominally instead, no relative stands before the attributive's own word and rule 47 governs the count: the phrase's single *the* is the first article's and the agreement article is `unrepresented`, as `הַכֹּהֵן הַמָּשִׁיחַ` → *the anointed priest* is. **A demonstrative, possessive or quantifying determiner the ASV writes in the article's place goes the same way, whatever the article stands on.** Where the ASV writes no *the* for the phrase but a *this*, *that*, *these*, *those*, a possessive *his*, *her*, *thy*, *thine*, or a quantifier such as *any* or *every*, standing where the phrase's *the* would stand — `הַזִּמָּה` → *this lewdness*, `הַגְּדֹלוֹת` → *thine elder*, `הַבֶּגֶד` → *any garment* — that word is what the ASV has written for the article: the article takes it and heads on it, and it is neither `added` nor a reason to mark the article `unrepresented`. A quantifying determiner is read on exactly the terms of the others and is not left to the noun under rule 15: that rule gives a supplied *any* to the nominal expression only where no determiner morpheme stands before the word it determines, and an article standing on that word is such a morpheme. **Read that as no *the* standing before the article's own word, not as no *the* anywhere in the phrase.** Where the article sits on the *nomen rectum* of a construct chain and the ASV writes a *the* before the construct and a demonstrative or possessive before the rectum's own word, rule 29's arthrous case governs the count: nothing is fronted, the article takes the determiner standing before its own word, and the construct takes the supplied *the*. `יְמֵי הַמִּשְׁתֶּה` → *the days of* ***their*** *feasting* gives *their* to the article and *the days* to the construct, exactly as `שְׂדֵה הָעִיר` → *the fields of the city* gives the second *the* to the article there. Do not mark such an article `unrepresented` and the determiner `added` under rule 18's closing sentence: that sentence governs a supplied possessive with no determiner morpheme standing before the word it determines, and here one does. **But only where no other morpheme licenses that determiner.** A pronominal suffix standing in the same phrase is rendered by a possessive of its own under rule 18, a `כֹּל` standing on the noun is itself what an *any* or an *every* renders under rule 15, an `אֶחָד` standing in the phrase is itself what a distributive *each* or *every* renders, and a demonstrative pronoun standing in the phrase is itself what a written *this*, *that*, *these* or *those* renders; where the ASV writes one determiner for the whole phrase that morpheme takes it and the article is `unrepresented`: `אֲחוֹתֵךְ הַגְּדוֹלָה` → *thine elder sister* gives *thine* to the suffix on `אָחוֹת` and leaves the article nothing, while `אֲחוֹתַיִךְ הַגְּדֹלוֹת` → *thy sisters, thine elder sisters* writes a determiner for each and the article takes the second; so too `אֲחִיהֶם הַבְּכוֹר` → *their eldest brother's*, where the suffix takes *their* and the article has nothing, and `הַמָּקוֹם הַהוּא` → *that place*, where `הוּא` takes the written *that* while the noun's article and the agreement article beside it are both `unrepresented` under rule 47. **And the morpheme that licenses the determiner need not stand inside the noun phrase.** What the preceding sentences ask is which morpheme the written determiner answers to, so read the clause rather than the phrase's edges: where a dative or possessive `לְ` with a pronominal suffix stands beside the noun and the ASV recasts it as a possessive determiner on that noun, the suffix is what the determiner realizes under rules 18 and 40, the `לְ` is `unrepresented` under rule 40, and the article is `unrepresented` exactly as it is beside a suffix written on the noun itself. `וַיְהִי לָהֶם הַגְּבוּל` → *And* ***their*** *border was* gives *their* to the suffix, *was* to `הָיָה` under rule 43, and nothing to either the preposition or the article. Do not hand such a determiner to the article on the ground that the suffix stands outside the phrase: that would leave the morpheme the ASV wrote the word for holding nothing, against rules 2 and 18. Count the determiners and read which word each stands before, as rule 29 counts the ASV's *the*s. **The `אֶחָד` case is counted on exactly those terms, and it turns on the one word the ASV wrote rather than on where that word stands.** A distributive determiner stands precisely where the phrase's *the* would stand, so the preceding sentences alone would hand it to the article; the numeral takes it because the numeral is itself what the word says. Where the ASV writes one such determiner, it is the numeral's and every article in the phrase — the noun's own and the agreement article on the numeral alike — is `unrepresented`: `לַפָּר הָאֶחָד` → *for* ***each*** *bullock* and `לַכֶּבֶשׂ הָאֶחָד` → *for* ***every*** *lamb*. Where it instead writes a *the* before the noun and an *one* for the numeral, two determiners are written and each morpheme takes the one standing before its own word: `לָאַיִל הָאֶחָד` → *for* ***the one*** *ram* gives *the* to the noun's article and *one* to the numeral, the agreement article still `unrepresented` under rule 47. One verse may write both shapes, and NUM.28.12 and NUM.28.28 do. The corpus aligns `אֶחָד` to *each* in 18 units, to *every* in four and to *every one* in five, so a numeral standing in the phrase is a morpheme that licenses such a determiner; the article's own units of *every* are the case this sentence excludes, where no such numeral stands. **And a determiner another morpheme licenses is still a determiner standing before the article's own word.** The count above is unchanged where the preceding sentences have left the article with nothing: still nothing is fronted, and where such a phrase is the *nomen rectum* of a construct chain the construct takes a supplied *the* of its own. `שֵׁם הַמָּקוֹם הַהוּא` → *the name of that place* gives *the name* to the construct, *that* to the demonstrative pronoun, and nothing to either article. Do not read the article's silence as an anarthrous rendering and hand the construct's *the* back to it as a fronted determiner: what forces fronting under rule 29 is a rectum the ASV renders with no determiner at all — `לֻחֹת הָאֲבָנִים` → *the two tables of stone* — and a rectum whose own word carries a *this*, a *that* or a possessive is not that, whichever morpheme in the phrase the word answers to. Read what stands before the rectum's word, not which morpheme was left with nothing. Read what the ASV writes for the article and let that decide which rule governs: a bare *the* is rule 15's ordinary attributive article, a relative or a supplied pronoun is this rule's, a demonstrative, possessive or quantifying determiner is this rule's too, and where the ASV writes none of them the article is `unrepresented` under rule 29. Where coordinated attributives share a single written relative, the first article takes it and the rest are `unrepresented` under rules 22 and 47. **An articleless attributive that the ASV recasts as a relative clause keeps the supplied relative with the attributive itself.** Where an adjective or participle without an article modifies an explicit noun and the ASV writes a relative frame — `אֲדָמָה טְמֵאָה` → *a land that is unclean* — the attributive takes the relative together with its own English and any copula rule 20 supplies, headed on the word carrying its lexeme. The relative realizes the Hebrew attributive relation and is not `added`; rule 55's supplied clause subordinator is a different construction. **But read whether the Hebrew writes an attributive or a predication before reaching for that sentence.** What it governs is an adjective or participle standing with the noun it modifies inside one nominal expression, as `אֲדָמָה טְמֵאָה` is. Where the adjective or participle is instead the predicate of a verbless clause and the ASV subordinates that clause to the noun standing as its subject, the English relative subordinates a clause rather than realizing an attributive relation, so rules 46 and 55 govern it: the relative answers to no morpheme and is its own `added` unit, while the copula and the predicate's own English stay with the adjective or participle under rule 20. `דּוֹר טָהוֹר בְּעֵינָיו` → *a generation* ***that*** *are pure in their own eyes* and `לַיִשׁ גִּבּוֹר בַּבְּהֵמָה` → *The lion,* ***which*** *is mightiest among beasts* both leave the relative `added` and give *are pure* and *is mightiest* to the adjective. Read the Hebrew clause rather than the English noun phrase the ASV has built around it, since a translation that subordinates a predication writes exactly what an attributive relation writes. Four things mark a predication. A subject or resumptive pronoun standing in the clause is one, as the `הֵם` of `אַרְבָּעָה הֵם קְטַנֵּי־אָרֶץ` → *four things* ***which*** *are little upon the earth* is. A further finite clause coordinated under the same English relative is another, since no attributive frame carries one. A clause of the same shape elsewhere in the passage that the ASV renders as a main clause with a copula is the third, as `הַנְּמָלִים עַם לֹא־עָז` → *The ants are a people not strong* stands beside the `לַיִשׁ גִּבּוֹר` above. **And the Hebrew's own word order is the fourth, which is the plainest of them.** An attributive follows the noun it modifies, so a participle or adjective standing before the noun the ASV has made the head of its English noun phrase is that clause's predicate and not an attributive, whatever order the English writes: `מִתְהַלֵּךְ בְּתֻמּוֹ צַדִּיק` → *A righteous man* ***that*** *walketh in his integrity* opens with the participle and stands the participle's own prepositional phrase between it and the noun, so the *that* is `added` and the participle keeps only its own *walketh*, while `מֶלֶךְ יוֹשֵׁב עַל־כִּסֵּא דִין` → *A king* ***that*** *sitteth on the throne of judgment* writes the participle after its noun and the preceding sentence governs. Read the order of the source tokens rather than the ASV's, since a translation that subordinates a fronted predicate writes exactly what an attributive writes, and one chapter may write both shapes, as PRO.20.7 and PRO.20.8 do; a fronted participle the ASV renders as a finite predicate of its own — `מְזָרֶה רְשָׁעִים מֶלֶךְ חָכָם` → *A wise king winnoweth the wicked* — is the same shape read without a relative and needs nothing placed. But read what the fronted word governs before applying this mark: a participle in construct on the noun that follows it is a substantive with its own dependent, and rules 46 and 19 govern it, as `יִרְאֵי יְהֵוָה` → *them that feared Jehovah* is. Where none of these four is present and the Hebrew writes an attributive inside a nominal expression, the preceding sentence governs as before.

**An articleless attributive that the ASV recasts as a relative clause keeps the supplied relative with the attributive itself.** Where an adjective or participle without an article modifies an explicit noun and the ASV writes a relative frame — `אֲדָמָה טְמֵאָה` → *a land that is unclean* — the attributive takes the relative together with its own English and any copula rule 20 supplies, headed on the word carrying its lexeme. The relative realizes the Hebrew attributive relation and is not `added`; rule 55's supplied clause subordinator is a different construction.

42. **A numeral phrase may hold more waws than the ASV renders.** Align the ASV's *and* to the waw standing between the same two numerals the English *and* stands between, and mark any other waw in the phrase `unrepresented`. In `חָמֵשׁ וְתִשְׁעִים שָׁנָה וּשְׁמֹנֶה מֵאוֹת שָׁנָה` → *eight hundred ninety and five years* the English *and* joins ninety to five, so the units-to-tens waw takes it and the waw at the group boundary has nothing; in `תְּשַׁע מֵאוֹת שָׁנָה וּשְׁלֹשִׁים שָׁנָה` → *nine hundred and thirty years* the same English *and* joins the two groups, so there the group-boundary waw is the one that takes it. This is determined rather than a tie-break: the ASV's *and* stands between specific numerals, so read which, rather than preferring a position. **The surplus runs the other way just as often.** Where the phrase holds more English *and*s than waws, an *and* standing between two numerals no waw joins is `added` under rule 30, because no morpheme licenses it: `מֵאָה שְׁנֵים עָשָׂר` → *a hundred and twelve*, `אֶלֶף שִׁבְעָה עָשָׂר` → *a thousand and seventeen*, `שְׁמֹנֶה מֵאוֹת שְׁמֹנָה עָשָׂר` → *eight hundred and eighteen*, `אַרְבַּע רִבּוֹא אַלְפַּיִם` → *forty and two thousand*. Do not fold such an *and* into a neighbouring numeral's unit to avoid the `added`: rule 30's fusion wants one English word, and this *and* is written beside the numerals rather than inside one of them. Count the waws, count the *and*s, and set them against each other by the pair of numerals each stands between; whichever side is in surplus is what the two halves of this rule dispose of. An ASV italic on such an *and* corroborates it, and the absence of one is not evidence against it, by rule 7. **But the numerals the count runs between are the ones the Hebrew writes, not the ones the English writes.** Where a single Hebrew numeral is rendered by a fixed English compound that contains an *and* of its own — `שִׁבְעִים` → *threescore and ten* — that word coordinates the parts of one morpheme's rendering rather than two source items, so it joins that numeral's unit under rule 14's closing sentence, is neither `added` nor any waw's, and does not move the unit's head. Decide it by the subtraction test rules 14, 16 and 31 use: ask what stands on either side of the English *and* in the Hebrew. Where a source numeral stands on each side, the sentences above govern and the surplus is marked; where the same one numeral's rendering runs on both sides, no source item is coordinated at all and there is nothing to mark. One list may write both, and 2CH.29.32 does: `שִׁבְעִים בָּקָר` → *threescore and ten bullocks* keeps its *and* inside the numeral's unit, while the *and* of *a hundred rams and two hundred lambs* stands between two numerals the Hebrew writes separately with no waw between them and is `added`.

43. **Hebrew has no verb *to have*, so an English *have* is a recasting and belongs to whatever made the clause possessive.** Where `הָיָה` is present it is the existential and takes the *have* itself, its preposition goes `unrepresented` under rule 40, and the suffix takes the subject pronoun: `וַיְהִי לוֹ נָשִׁים` → *he had* … *wives* puts *had* on `הָיָה` and *he* on the suffix. **The existential particle `יֵשׁ` is read on exactly those terms.** Where `יֵשׁ` stands in the clause it is what the *have* renders, exactly as a written `הָיָה` is: the particle takes the word and heads on it, its preposition goes `unrepresented` under rule 40, and the suffix takes the subject pronoun — `יֶשׁ־לִי תִקְוָה` → ***I have*** *hope* gives *have* to `יֵשׁ`, *I* to the suffix and nothing to the `לְ`. This is rule 50's reading of the Aramaic positive existential `אִיתַי`, which takes whatever overt finite frame the ASV writes for it, a *have* among them, and of the negative `אַיִן`, which keeps the frame and heads on its own word in all twenty-eight of its corpus units rather than leaving it to a morpheme standing beside it. Read the condition and not the lemma, as rule 40 directs: what makes such a clause possessive is the existential, and a written `יֵשׁ` left `unrepresented` beside a preposition holding the *have* would leave the clause's one predicating morpheme with nothing, against rule 2. Where the clause is verbless and no existential particle stands in it, the possessive preposition is what makes it possessive and carries the *have*: `לָהֶם מִסְגְּרוֹת` → *they had panels* puts *had* on `לְ` and *they* on the suffix, and `שָׁפְרָה עָלָי` → *I have a goodly heritage* puts *have* on `עַל` and *I* on the suffix. Where neither is present, a possessive suffix can be the possession by itself and takes the supplied verb with its pronoun: `טוֹבָתִי בַּל עָלֶיךָ` → *I have no good beyond thee* gives the suffix *I have*, headed on the pronoun. Another finite verb in the clause does not qualify merely by being finite. `שָׁפְרָה` is not a copula: the ASV renders it by the attributive *goodly*, which is its lexeme, so the *have* is not its. **But a verb the ASV has itself rendered by the *have* does qualify, and the same subtraction decides it.** Where the Hebrew writes a verb of falling or allotment and the English writes *have* in its place — `יִפְּלוּ בְנַחֲלָה` → *they shall have inheritance* — dropping the word leaves that verb with no rendering at all, so the *have* is the verb's own together with the subject pronoun rule 17 keeps there, and a preposition beside it whose relation the ASV has left unstated goes `unrepresented` under rule 40. **And where nothing in the clause makes it possessive, the word is not to be placed at all.** A clause that writes no `הָיָה`, no existential particle, no possessive preposition, no suffix and no verb the *have* renders has no morpheme to carry it: `יוֹסֵף חֲבָלִים` → *Joseph* ***shall have two*** *portions* leaves the supplied verb its own `added` unit under rule 20's supplied-lexical-verb clause, together with any numeral, subject or object the ASV supplies alongside it, while the possessed noun keeps only its own word. Do not fold such a word into that noun, into a neighbouring clause's verb, or into a preposition standing elsewhere in the verse to avoid the `added`, for the reason rule 42 gives about a surplus *and*: it is written beside those renderings rather than inside one of them. **But that count runs over the morphemes of a clause the Hebrew wrote, and does not reach a *have* the ASV writes inside its recasting of a single nominal.** Where the Hebrew writes a noun of possession — `בַּעַל`, or another noun naming the owner or master of what follows — and the ASV recasts that noun together with its construct dependents as an English head noun and a relative clause of having, rule 46's closing paragraph governs: the supplied antecedent, the relative and the *have* all belong to that noun's own unit, because the *have* is what the noun's own lexeme names, and the unit heads on the *have* under rule 31, while the dependent keeps only its own English. `בַּעַל כְּנָפַיִם` → *that which* ***hath*** *wings* is one unit on `בַּעַל`, headed on *hath*, with *wings* left to `כָּנָף`. So ask whether what the ASV recast is a clause or a nominal before counting a *have* as unlicensed, and do not mark such a word `added` on the ground that the morpheme carrying it is not a verb. Test by subtraction, as in rule 16 — drop the *have* and ask which morpheme has lost its rendering. If only the possession relation is gone, the *have* is the preposition's or the suffix's; if a written verb, a written existential particle, or a written noun of possession is left with nothing, it is that morpheme's; and if nothing in the clause is diminished but a possession the English has supplied, it is `added`. **Read *no possessive preposition* and *no suffix* in that count as none the ASV has left an English word for.** A preposition or a suffix on which the ASV has already spent its own word stands in the same position as an absent one: it is not what makes the clause possessive, and it is not a home for a second English word, exactly as rule 52 counts a preposition's function words against the morphemes that could carry them and rule 51 says a waw holding an English word of its own is not a home for a second one. What this rule's earlier sentences govern is a `בְּ` or a `לְ` that is itself the clause's predicate, for which the ASV writes no preposition at all — `אֲשֶׁר בּוֹ מוּם` → *that hath a blemish* — and not a locative or other phrase the ASV has rendered in full. `אוֹ תְבַלֻּל בְּעֵינוֹ` → *or* ***that hath*** *a blemish in his eye* writes *in* for the `בְּ`, *his* for the suffix, *eye* for the noun and *a blemish* for `תְּבַלֻּל`, so nothing is left for the *hath* to complete and it is its own `added` unit; `מְרוֹחַ אָשֶׁךְ` → ***hath his*** *stones broken* goes the same way, the adjective keeping *broken* and the noun *stones*, with the supplied *his* `added` besides under rule 18's closing sentence. Count the clause's English words against the morphemes that could carry them before reaching for one that is already spent. **And where the possessive clause is a relative clause, count the ASV's words against the morphemes that could carry them before placing the English subject.** Where the ASV writes one word for the relative and the resumption together, that word is the relative's and it supplies the subject: `אֲשֶׁר בּוֹ מוּם` → *that hath a blemish* gives *that* to `אֲשֶׁר` and *hath* to the `בְּ`, and the suffix is `unrepresented` under rule 22, since two morphemes point at the antecedent and the ASV writes one word for it. **But where the ASV writes a subject pronoun besides the relative, two English words answer to the two morphemes and neither is a surplus.** `כֹּל אֲשֶׁר לוֹ` → *all* ***that he hath*** gives *that* to `אֲשֶׁר`, *hath* to the possessive `לְ` and *he* to the suffix, which is the subject pronoun this rule's earlier sentences give it; the same clause written without a relative gives the suffix that pronoun in any case, as `מוּם בּוֹ` → *he hath a blemish* does. Count as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s, and do not mark the suffix `unrepresented` beside a written pronoun no other morpheme answers to, which would leave that pronoun `added` against rule 30 while the morpheme it realizes held nothing. A written `הָיָה` in such a clause still takes the *have* itself and leaves both the preposition and the suffix `unrepresented`: `אֲשֶׁר יִהְיֶה בּוֹ מוּם` → *that hath a blemish*. **And an interrogative particle standing in such a clause does not take the *have* either.** Where `הֲ`, or the `אִם` opening a disjunctive question, stands in a possessive clause and the ASV marks the question by fronting the possessive verb itself, that word is the possessive morpheme's under this rule's earlier sentences and the particle is `unrepresented` under rule 17's sentence on a question marked by inversion alone: `וְאִם־זְרוֹעַ כָּאֵל לָךְ` → *Or* ***hast*** *thou an arm like God* gives *hast* to the `לְ` and *thou* to its suffix, and leaves the `אִם` nothing. Rule 20's fronted-copula sentence and rule 50's reading of an interrogative existential clause reach a copula the construction supplies and an existential frame, which are words no other morpheme licenses; a *have* is neither, since this rule assigns it to the `הָיָה`, the existential particle, the preposition, the suffix, the verb or the noun of possession that makes the clause possessive, and handing it to the particle would leave that morpheme holding nothing while the relation it alone carries went unrendered, against rule 2. Read what the fronted word is rather than that the clause is a question: where the ASV writes a separate question auxiliary besides the *have* — a *Do*, a *Shall* or a *Will* at the clause head — that word is the particle's under rule 17 as before, and the *have* still goes where this rule sends it.

44. **A verb the ASV renders as a light verb plus a complement heads on the complement, not on the light verb.** This is rule 31's test applied to a verb: drop each word in turn, and the head is the one whose loss loses the lexeme. `הַאֲזִין` → *Give ear unto* heads on *ear*, because *give* alone gives anything; so too `הִשְׁתַּחֲוָה` → *did obeisance*, `נוֹעַץ` → *took counsel*, `הִמְלִיךְ` → *make king*, `הוֹדָה` → *give thanks*, `כִּלָּה` → *made an end*, `עוּד` → *bear witness*, `חָנַן` → *make supplication*, `בּוֹשׁ` → *put to shame*. The complement need not be a noun: an adjective (`הִכְבִּיד` → *made heavy*, `גָּדַל` → *make greater*) and a second verb (`חָטָא` hiphil → *made to sin*, `אָכַל` → *did eat*) are read the same way. Rule 24's *lexical predicate over subjects and auxiliaries* is about `was gathered`, and must not be read as a preference for the English verb — that reasoning would head *make king* on *make*. **A determiner or possessive the English idiom writes on such a complement is part of that one rendering.** Where the complement carries the source verb's lexeme and English writes it with an article or a possessive the idiom requires — `הֶחֱרִישׁ` → *held his peace*, `כִּלָּה` → *made an end*, `גָּוַע` → *give up the ghost* — that word joins the verb's unit with the complement, is never `added`, and does not move the head, which stays on the complement. Rule 18's closing sentence does not reach it: what that sentence marks `added` is a possessive standing on a noun the Hebrew writes with no suffix, or on a supplied object noun the sentences below leave `added` — `הָלַךְ` → *go* ***your way*** — and a possessive inside a complement that is itself the verb's rendering is neither. Test by subtraction as rule 31 does: drop the complement and the possessive has nothing to stand on, so the two go together, whereas dropping *your way* leaves *go* whole. The corpus reads `חָרֵשׁ` hiphil as *hold thy peace*, *held his peace*, *held their peace* and *hold my peace* in one unit headed on *peace* throughout its forty-two units and marks the possessive `added` in none. **A phrasal particle is not a complement.** `שָׁלַח` → *put forth*, `סוּר` → *take away*, and `נָתַן` → *put away* are one lexical verb plus its particle, and the head stays on the verb; the same subtraction says so, since *forth* and *away* alone name nothing. **But run that same subtraction on the particle before folding it in.** Where the bare English verb already writes the source verb's whole lexeme **and** the particle adds a direction, a result or a repetition the Hebrew does not write, no morpheme licenses that word: it is its own `added` unit under rule 30 and does not join the verb merely because it stands inside its rendering, exactly as this rule leaves a supplied object noun `added` and rule 17 a supplied lexical subject noun. `שׁוּב` → *turn ... again* keeps its *again* with the verb, discontinuous under rule 25, because *turn* alone does not write the returning, and `כָּנַס` → *gather ... together* and `מָצָא` → *find out* keep theirs on the same ground; but `בּוֹא` hiphil → *bring him* ***back*** leaves the *back* `added`, because the hiphil is the bringing and *bring* has already written it. **Both conditions must hold, and the second is read against the whole Hebrew clause and not against the verb alone.** What marks a particle surplus is that it states something the Hebrew has nowhere written, as the return of *bring him back* is: the particles this rule keeps — *out*, *together*, *away*, *forth* — stand beside verbs whose bare English word writes the lexeme quite as plainly as *bring* writes the bringing, so the first condition cannot decide a particle by itself. Where the direction the particle states is written in the clause by a preposition, an adverb or another morpheme, the particle restates what the Hebrew has written rather than supplying what it has not, and it is part of the English phrasal verb by which the ASV renders that verb: it joins the verb's unit, discontinuous around an object under rule 25, and the head is unaffected. `הָלַךְ` with `מִן` → *went* ***away*** *from him* and `לָקַח` with `עַל` → *took her* ***up*** *upon the ass* both keep their particle with the verb, on the same footing as `מָצָא` → *find out*. That the other morpheme holds an English word of its own is not a reason to mark the particle `added`, and it is not an invitation to move the particle onto that morpheme either: the word belongs to the verb's rendering, and a preposition already holding its own English is not a home for a second one, as rule 51 says of a waw. Read the source verb's lexeme against the bare English verb rather than the shape of the English verb phrase, since the particle of *put forth* and *take away* is what completes a lexeme neither *put* nor *take* writes, and do not fold a surplus particle into the verb, the object or a preposition standing beside it, for the reason rule 42 gives about a surplus *and* and rule 51 about a supplied *also*. An ASV italic on such a particle corroborates it and decides nothing, by rule 7. **A supplied object is not a complement either, and that holds of any verb's rendering and not only a light verb's.** Where the ASV writes an object pronoun inside or beside a Hebrew verb's rendering — `וַיַּפֵּל` → *he let* ***it*** *fall*, `וַיַּשְׁקְ` → *gave* ***them*** *drink*, `אֲנִי שָׁמָעְתִּי` → *I have heard* ***it*** — read whether an object morpheme is present. Where one is, it takes the pronoun under rule 18 and the verb keeps the rest, as in `וַיַּעֲבִירֵם` → *caused* ***them*** *to pass through*. Where none is, the pronoun answers to no morpheme, is its own `added` unit under rule 30, and does not join the verb merely because it stands inside or beside its rendering; rule 17's permission reaches a pronoun realizing the verb's own person and number, and never an object the Hebrew does not write, exactly as that rule leaves a supplied lexical subject noun `added`. The verb's unit is discontinuous around such a pronoun under rule 25. **This applies only to a pronoun that truly supplies an object.** A reflexive pronoun the ASV uses to render the Hebrew verb's own voice or its elative sense is part of that verb's unit, because the source verb licenses it: `אִכַּף` → *bow myself* keeps *myself* with the Niphal verb and heads on *bow*, and `הִגְדִּיל עַל` → *magnified yourselves against* keeps *yourselves* with the Hiphil on the same ground. Position inside the English verb phrase is not what makes a word part of the verb's rendering — the complement, or the source verb's own voice or sense, must license it, and an unsupported object does not. **And a pronoun realizing the source verb's own person and number is not a supplied object either, whatever case the English recasting puts it in.** Where the ASV recasts a finite verb impersonally — writing a dummy or anticipatory *it* as the English subject and demoting the verb's own subject to the English object — both words belong to that verb's unit, headed on the lexical verb, exactly as rule 17 keeps a presentative dummy with the finite verb it recasts and rule 21 keeps *it was* with `הָיָה`: `נִלְאָה לַהֲשִׁיבָהּ אֶל־פִּיו` → ***It wearieth him*** *to bring it again to his mouth* gives *It wearieth him* to `נִלְאָה`, while the infinitive and the preposition keep their own English. What the preceding sentences govern is a pronoun denoting a participant the Hebrew never wrote, as the *it* of `וַיַּפֵּל` → *he let it fall* and the *them* of `וַיַּשְׁקְ` → *gave them drink* are; a pronoun denoting the very participant the verb's own person and number denote is not that, and marking it `added` would leave the verb's subject unrendered against rule 2 while an English word plainly realizing it stood unaligned. Test by subtraction as elsewhere — drop the English pronoun and ask whether a participant the Hebrew wrote has lost its rendering, or only one the ASV supplied. Read whom the pronoun denotes rather than the case English puts it in, and read the Hebrew clause before reaching for this sentence: where an object morpheme stands in the clause it takes the pronoun under rule 18 as before, where the ASV writes the verb's subject as an English subject this sentence does not arise, and rule 20's ruling that a cleft or impersonal *it* is `added` governs a verbless clause, where no verb stands to be recast. **A supplied object noun is read on those same terms, and what decides it is whether the English noun writes the verb's own lexeme.** Where the ASV completes a Hebrew verb with an object noun the Hebrew does not write, run rule 31's subtraction on that noun. Where it does not carry the verb's lexeme, it answers to no morpheme, is its own `added` unit under rule 30, and does not join the verb merely because it stands inside its rendering: `לֹא שָׁאָלוּ` → *asked* ***counsel*** *not* leaves *counsel* `added`, because `שָׁאַל` is the asking and *asked* has already written it, and `הָלַךְ` → *go* ***your way*** leaves the supplied noun and its possessive `added` on the same ground, because *go* has written the going and *way* names a path the Hebrew does not write, exactly as rule 17 leaves a supplied lexical subject noun `added` and rule 20 a supplied lexical verb. A supplied noun is not a particle, and the clause-level reading above does not reach it: what that reading keeps with the verb is a word stating a relation the clause writes, and a noun naming a thing the clause never names is not that. Where the noun does carry the lexeme, it is part of that verb's one rendering, joins its unit, and the head goes by the same subtraction to whichever word carries the lexeme: `וַיַּעֲשׂוּ` → *did work* is one unit on the verb, headed on *did*, because `עָשָׂה` is the doing and the working alike and neither English word is a complement the other lacks. Read the source verb's lexeme rather than the English phrase's shape, since this rule's light-verb clause governs where the supplied noun is what carries that lexeme — `נוֹעַץ` → *took counsel* heads on *counsel* — and this sentence governs where the verb's own English word has written it already. A supplied possessive standing on such a noun is disposed of by rule 18's closing sentence and is not made the verb's by the noun beside it. **But a preposition the ASV supplies to reach the verb's own object is part of that rendering.** Where the Hebrew writes a bare direct object — no object marker on it and no preposition of its own — and the English verb reaches that object through a preposition the ASV has written, the preposition belongs to the verb's unit and not to the object noun's: `חָמְסוּ תוֹרָתִי` → *have done violence* ***to*** *my law*, `תְּבַצְּעִי רֵעַיִךְ` → *hast greedily gained* ***of*** *thy neighbors*, `הֵקַלּוּ אָב וָאֵם` → *have set light* ***by*** *father and mother*, `אֲבַקֵּשׁ אִישׁ` → *sought* ***for*** *a man*, `טָחוּ תָּפֵל` → *have daubed ... with untempered mortar*. This is rule 16's reasoning where no marker stands to take the word: the same subtraction test finds that dropping it diminishes nothing but the object relation, and the object noun's own English is the noun. The unit is discontinuous under rule 25 where other material stands between the verb and the preposition, and the head is unaffected — it stays on the lexical verb, or on the complement where this rule's light-verb clause puts it. **And the object so reached may be a pronominal suffix on the verb rather than a noun.** Where a verbal object suffix stands with no preposition of its own and the English rendering reaches it through a supplied preposition — `יִבְּמָהּ` → *perform the duty of a husband's brother* ***unto*** *her*, `יַבְּמִי` → *perform the duty of a husband's brother* ***unto*** *me* — that preposition belongs to the verb's unit on exactly the terms just given, and the suffix keeps only the pronoun rule 18 writes for it. Do not read rule 18's *the English pronoun that realizes it* as reaching the preposition standing beside that pronoun: what that rule places is the pronoun, and a preposition the English verb's valency licenses is the verb's whether its object is a noun or a suffix. Rule 40 does not reach such a suffix either, since no preposition morpheme stands in the clause for the recasting to silence. Test by subtraction as before — drop the English preposition and ask whether anything but the object relation is diminished. Two limits. Where an object marker or a preposition of the object's own is present, that morpheme takes the word under rules 16 and 11, a present morpheme taking its English before a supplied reading does. And this does not touch rule 33: a function word supplied for an adverbial accusative is licensed by that noun's own case-function and stays with the noun, whereas a word supplied for a direct object is licensed by the English verb's valency, so read which the Hebrew noun is before placing it.

45. **The *that* of a presentative `הָיָה` belongs to that verb, and is not `added`.** The ASV renders `וַיְהִי` and `וְהָיָה` with a frame — *And it came to pass … that*, *And it shall come to pass … that* — and the frame's complementizer is part of what the verb is rendered by, exactly as rule 21's dummy subject is. It joins the `הָיָה` unit, headed as that unit already heads. The unit may be discontinuous under rule 25 where a temporal phrase stands inside the frame: `וְהָיָה אַחֲרֵי כֵן` → *And it shall come to pass afterward, that* gives `הָיָה` both *it shall come to pass* and *that*, while `אַחֲרֵי כֵן` keeps *afterward*. **Where the apodosis opens with its own waw, that waw carries the *that* instead** and the verb keeps only *it came to pass*; and where the ASV writes no *that* at all there is nothing to place. Read which of the three the verse is rather than reaching for the verb every time. **But read where the ASV has written the word before handing it to that waw.** The waw carries the *that* only where the ASV writes it at the head of the apodosis; where the ASV instead writes it immediately after the frame, ahead of the temporal phrase or of a temporal clause carrying a subordinator of its own, the word stands inside the frame rather than at the apodosis's head, the frame keeps it under this rule's first sentence, and the apodosis waw, for which the ASV has then written no connective, is `unrepresented` under rule 14. `וַיְהִי כֶּאֱמֹר יְהוֹשֻׁעַ אֶל־הָעָם וְשִׁבְעָה הַכֹּהֲנִים ... עָבְרוּ` → *And it was so,* ***that*** *when Joshua had spoken unto the people, the seven priests ... passed on* gives *it was so that* to `הָיָה` and *when* to the `כְּ` under rule 52, leaving that waw nothing, while `וַיְהִי כִשְׁמֹעַ הָעָם ... וַיָּרִיעוּ הָעָם` → *And it came to pass, when the people heard ...,* ***that*** *the people shouted* writes its one *that* at the head of the apodosis and gives it to the waw that opens it. Read the English word order, since what tells the two apart is where the ASV has put the word and not that a waw stands in the apodosis at all. **And where the ASV writes no complementizer at the apodosis head, the frame has none to place.** A subordinator the ASV supplies for a temporal clause the Hebrew writes asyndetically is not the frame's: no morpheme stands in that clause to carry it, so it is its own `added` unit under rule 55, and the apodosis waw is `unrepresented` as before. `וַיְהִי בַּפַּעַם הַשְּׁבִיעִית תָּקְעוּ הַכֹּהֲנִים בַּשּׁוֹפָרוֹת וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוֹשֻׁעַ` → *And it came to pass at the seventh time,* ***when*** *the priests blew the trumpets, Joshua said* leaves that *when* `added`, while the same word is the preposition's wherever a `כְּ` or a `בְּ` stands to carry it, as in the two examples above. Count the ASV's subordinators against the morphemes that could carry them, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s. **And where what stands inside the frame is not a phrase but a temporal clause the ASV writes with a relative or complementizer of its own, the frame's *that* is the one standing at the head of the apodosis, never the first one written.** Read the Hebrew for where the apodosis begins and count the ASV's *that*s against the clauses that license them, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s: a *that* introducing the temporal clause answers to whatever morpheme rules 52 and 19 put it on, and only the *that* the ASV writes before the apodosis is the frame's. `וְהָיָה בְּיוֹם הַנְחִילוֹ אֶת־בָּנָיו ... לֹא יוּכַל לְבַכֵּר` → *then it shall be in the day* ***that*** *he causeth his sons to inherit that which he hath,* ***that*** *he may not make the son of the beloved the first born* gives the second *that* to `הָיָה`, discontinuous across the whole temporal clause under rule 25, and the first to the infinitive construct, exactly as rule 52 leaves *when they shall make* to the infinitive of `בְּיוֹם הֵעָשׂוֹתוֹ` where the `בְּ` and the construct noun each already hold an English word of their own. Do not mark the apodosis *that* `added` under rule 55 on the ground that no conjunction stands in its clause: the frame licenses it, and a frame whose complementizer has been spent on the temporal clause will always leave one over.

46. **An anarthrous substantive takes the English the ASV supplies to make it one.** A participle or adjective used as a noun with no article morpheme present still needs an English noun phrase, and the ASV supplies it: a bare *the*, or a pronoun with a relative — *him that*, *them that*, *they that*, *those that*. No article is present to take that English and nothing else licenses it, so it joins the substantive's own unit and is never `added`. `זֵדִים` → *the proud*, `צַדִּיק` → *the righteous*, `יִרְאֵי יְהֵוָה` → *them that feared Jehovah*, `עֹשְׁקֵי` → *those that oppress*, `עֹבֵד` → *him that serveth*. **Head on the word carrying the lexeme**, by rule 31's subtraction: *proud*, *righteous*, *feared*, *oppress*, *serveth*. This does not contradict rule 41's heading on the pronoun. There the supplied pronoun is the whole of what the article is rendered by, so it is the only candidate; here the substantive's own lexeme sits in the same unit and outranks a word the ASV supplied. Read whether an article morpheme is present, and let that decide which rule governs. **And the supplied noun phrase may be built on a generic English noun as readily as on a pronoun.** Where the ASV writes *man*, *men*, *one*, *ones*, *thing*, *things* or another generic noun to make the anarthrous substantive an English noun phrase — `חָכָם` → *the wise man*, `נָבוֹן` → *the man of understanding*, `מַקְשִׁיב` → *man hath regarded* — that noun, together with whatever article, relative or *of* the phrase needs, joins the substantive's own unit on exactly the terms above and is never `added`, and the head stays on the word carrying the lexeme under rules 24 and 31, which already read a substantival adjective's *living creature* as one unit headed on *living*. Rule 17's ruling that a supplied lexical subject noun is `added` does not reach such a noun: that ruling governs a noun the ASV supplies where no source nominal stands at all, as `יָשְׁבוּ בָהּ` → *men shall dwell therein* is, and an anarthrous participle or adjective standing in the clause is itself the nominal the English noun phrase is built on. So read whether a source substantive stands for the phrase to realize before marking the noun `added`, exactly as this rule's own first sentence directs for a supplied pronoun. Two riders. Where the Hebrew supplies a relative of its own, that relative takes the *that* and the supplied antecedent goes with it — `לַאֲשֶׁר לֹא עֲבָדוֹ` → *him that serveth him not* puts *him that* on `אֲשֶׁר` — since a present morpheme takes its English before a bare participle does. And where the ASV writes the antecedent once across coordinated substantives, the later ones take only what is written: `וְחֹשְׁבֵי שְׁמוֹ` → *and that thought upon his name* leaves the participle *that thought upon*, under rule 22. **A headless relative clause is read the same way, and its verb may be finite.** Where the Hebrew writes a relative clause with no antecedent noun and no `אֲשֶׁר`, the whole clause stands in a nominal slot and the ASV must supply a noun phrase for it: `אַחֲרֵי לֹא יוֹעִילוּ הָלָכוּ` → *walked after* ***things that do*** *not* ***profit***, `בְלוֹא יוֹעִיל` → *for* ***that which doth*** *not* ***profit***. The supplied antecedent and relative join the clause's own unit and head on the word carrying the lexeme, exactly as *them that* joins a bare participle above and for the same reason: no article and no relative morpheme is present to take them, and nothing else licenses them, so neither is `added`. The unit is discontinuous around the negative or any other intervening word under rule 25, and a Hebrew noun the ASV recasts the same way — `מִבְטַחַיִךְ` → *those in whom thou trustest* — goes with it. **This governs only a clause with no antecedent of its own.** Where the Hebrew writes the antecedent and the ASV supplies a relative for a finite clause juxtaposed to it, nothing in this rule licenses that word: it subordinates a clause rather than making one a noun, and rules 30 and 55 are what decide it. **And a substantive the Hebrew writes is such an antecedent, whatever English this rule supplied to make it one.** An anarthrous participle or adjective for which the ASV has written *him that*, *they that* or *He who* is a written antecedent exactly as a common noun is, so a further relative the ASV writes over a finite clause juxtaposed to it answers to no morpheme and is `added` under rule 55: `נְאֻם שֹׁמֵעַ אִמְרֵי אֵל ... מַחֲזֵה שַׁדַּי יֶחֱזֶה` → *He saith who heareth the words of God ...* ***Who*** *seeth the vision of the Almighty* leaves that *Who* `added`, while the same clause written with a relative of its own — `אֲשֶׁר מַחֲזֵה שַׁדַּי יֶחֱזֶה` → ***Who*** *seeth the vision of the Almighty* — gives the word to the `אֲשֶׁר` under this rule's first rider. Do not read the supplied noun phrase as evidence that what follows is headless: ask whether a substantive stands before the clause, not whether that substantive's own English was written or supplied. Read what the ASV supplied, too, since the headless case writes an antecedent and a relative together — *things that*, *that which* — while a bare relative with no antecedent beside it subordinates a clause to what precedes and is rule 55's. Rule 41's articleless attributive is a third case again, and is decided there. **But those two sentences govern a clause the Hebrew writes, and no more.** Where instead the ASV recasts one Hebrew nominal — the noun together with whatever construct dependents it governs — as an English head noun and a relative clause, and no clause stands in the Hebrew for that relative to subordinate, the relative is part of the noun's own recasting: it joins that noun's unit and is never `added`, exactly as `מִבְטַחַיִךְ` → *those in whom thou trustest* is above. `מִמַּצַּב רַגְלֵי הַכֹּהֲנִים` → *out of the place* ***where*** *the priests' feet stood firm* gives *the place where* to `מַצָּב`, and `אֶל־הַמָּלוֹן` → *unto the place* ***where*** *they lodged* gives the whole phrase to `מָלוֹן`. Head on the word carrying the noun's lexeme under rule 31, which is the English verb where the recasting writes one for it and the head noun where a written Hebrew verb already holds the predicate. **A lexical English verb standing inside such a recasting is not rule 20's supplied lexical verb either**, where that verb is what the noun's own lexeme names — a lodging place is where one lodges and a standing place where one stands — since dropping it leaves that noun's rendering short rather than removing a predication the Hebrew never wrote. Test by subtraction as rules 16 and 31 do: drop the English relative and ask whether the noun's own rendering is left incomplete, or whether a clause the Hebrew wrote has merely lost a subordinator. Where a Hebrew relative or resumptive morpheme does stand in the phrase it takes the English first, under this rule's first rider and rule 22, and the noun keeps only its own words: `בַּמָּלוֹן אֲשֶׁר תָּלִינוּ בוֹ` → *in the lodging place* ***where*** *ye shall lodge* leaves `מָלוֹן` the bare *lodging place*.

47. **An attributive phrase may hold more article morphemes than the ASV renders.** Where coordinated attributives each carry their own `הַ` and the ASV writes a single *the* — `יוֹם יְהוָה הַגָּדוֹל וְהַנּוֹרָא` → *the great and terrible day of Jehovah* — the first article takes it and the rest are `unrepresented` under rule 22, because the English *the* stands at the left edge of the phrase the first attributive opens. This is rule 42's reasoning about waws applied to articles: read which word the ASV's *the* stands before rather than preferring a position. **The same first-article rule governs a definite noun whose attributive adjective repeats its article.** Where the ASV writes one *the* for `הָעִיר הַגְּדֹלָה` → *the great city*, the noun's first article takes it and the adjective's agreement article is `unrepresented`, even though English reorders the determiner before the adjective. **It holds even where the head noun is a construct that could take no article of its own.** A present article morpheme takes the English *the* before a supplied one does, exactly as in rule 29 and rule 46's first rider, so the *the* goes on the attributive's article and not on the construct noun. Where the ASV instead writes a *the* for each attributive — *the great and the terrible* — nothing is repeated and each article takes its own by rule 15.

48. **Where a privative `מִן` + infinitive construct is recast as a negative result clause, the preposition takes the frame's function words and the infinitive takes its supplied subject.** The ASV writes *that it cannot save*, *so that he will not hear*, *that I should give*. The complementizer and the negation are what the `מִן` contributes and stay with it, including a *cannot* that fuses that negation with the modal and so cannot be divided; the supplied subject pronoun, and any modal the ASV writes as a separate word, belong to the infinitive, exactly as rule 17 keeps a subject pronoun with its verb and as the corpus does after any preposition — `לָמוּת` → *that he should die*, `מִתִּתִּי` → *that* on the preposition beside *should give* on the infinitive. The two units may interleave — *that*(`מִן`) *it*(inf.) *cannot*(`מִן`) *save*(inf.) — which rule 25 allows; do not hand the preposition the subject pronoun merely to keep its unit contiguous. Where the ASV supplies no negation at all the `מִן` keeps only the complementizer. **And the negation is the `מִן`'s only where no negative morpheme stands in the clause to carry it.** Where the privative stands on an existential `אַיִן`, that particle is what the ASV's negation word realizes: it takes that word and heads on it under rule 50, while the `מִן` keeps only the complementizer the ASV has written — `מֵאֵין עוֹבֵר` → *so that none shall pass through* gives *so that* to the `מִן` and *none* to the `אַיִן`, a present morpheme taking its English before a supplied reading does, exactly as in rules 46 and 47. What the privative governs need not be an infinitive construct: an `אַיִן` with a participle or a noun after it is read the same way. **And read that as a negative morpheme, not as the lemma `אַיִן`.** Where the privative stands instead on `בְּלִי`, `בִּלְתִּי` or another negative substantive, that morpheme is what the ASV's negation word realizes and takes it, while the `מִן` keeps only the complementizer, exactly as the `אַיִן` divides above: `מִבְּלִי יְכֹלֶת יְהוָה` → ***Because*** *Jehovah was* ***not*** *able* gives *Because* to the `מִן` and *not* to `בְּלִי`, leaving the infinitive its own *was able*. What decides it is that a morpheme carrying the negation stands in the clause, which is the condition rule 40 reads in place of a lemma and the count rule 52 runs against the morphemes that could carry a function word; do not give the `מִן` a second English word on the ground that its own lemma is the privative one. **And the complementizer the `מִן` takes need not be a result one.** Where the ASV recasts the privative clause as a causal one it writes *because* or *for* in that same slot, and that word is the preposition's on the same terms as a *that* or a *so that*. But where the ASV spends a single English word on the preposition and the negative together — `מֵאֵין` → *without* — the one-English-word condition of rule 30 is met and the two share one unit instead, headed on the negation, and that sentence governs a `מִבְּלִי` the ASV renders by one word just as it governs a `מֵאֵין`.

49. **A verbless clause with a prepositional predicate takes its supplied copula on the preposition.** Rule 20 supplies the copula from the construction; where the predicate is a prepositional phrase, the copula joins the preposition heading that phrase and the unit heads on the preposition — `בְכַפֵּיהֶם` → *is in their hands*, `בִמְסִלּוֹתָם` → *are in their paths*, `אִתָּנוּ` → *are with us*, `עָלֶיךָ` → *is upon thee*. **And a modal or volitive auxiliary the ASV writes with that copula is part of it.** Where the clause carries jussive or optative force and the ASV writes *Let ... be*, *shall be*, *let there be* or another modal frame in the slot where the copula would stand, the whole frame is the construction-supplied copula and joins the preposition's unit, headed on the preposition as before, and no part of it is `added`: `רוֹמְמוֹת אֵל בִּגְרוֹנָם` → *Let the high praises of God* ***be in*** *their mouth* gives *Let ... be in* to the `בְּ`, discontinuous around the subject under rule 25, while the fronted construct nominal keeps its own English as the clause's subject. This is rule 17's ruling that a volitive frame belongs to the verb whose mood it renders, read for a clause that writes no verb for the frame to join, and rule 20's ruling that the copula is whatever verb the ASV writes where a copula would stand, read for the auxiliary written beside it; the directional case below already gives a modal *shall be* to the morpheme heading the predicate on the same terms. Do not divide such a frame, giving the *be* to the preposition and marking the *Let* `added` under rule 20's supplied-lexical-verb clause: what that clause governs is a verb introducing an event and a participant the Hebrew never wrote, as *were given* is, and a volitive auxiliary introduces neither. Read whether a verb stands in the clause before placing the word, since where one does rule 17 governs and the frame joins that verb; an ASV italic on the frame corroborates the supply and decides nothing, by rule 7. **A directional predicate whose relation is carried by a directive `he` rather than by a preposition is read on the same terms.** Rule 32 makes such a `he` its own aligned unit by rule 11 wherever the ASV writes a word of its own for it, so where that directional phrase is the predicate of a verbless clause the construction-supplied copula joins the `he`'s unit and that unit heads on the word written for the direction, discontinuous around the noun under rule 25: `דֶּגֶל מַחֲנֵה רְאוּבֵן תֵּימָנָה` → *On the south side shall be the standard of the camp of Reuben* gives *On … shall be* to the `he`, while `תֵּימָן` keeps *the south side*, headed on *south* under rule 31, and the construct chain keeps only its own English. Read the Hebrew for which side is the predicate, as this rule's closing paragraph and rule 20 both direct: a definite construct chain standing in such a clause is its subject, and the ASV's inversion of the English is not evidence against that. Two limits. Where the ASV writes no separate word for the `he` and the two share one unit under rule 30, there is no separate unit to carry the copula and it joins that one fused unit. And where a participial or other nominal phrase is the clause's subject, so that the directional phrase is not the predicate at all, rule 20 governs and the copula joins the nominal predicate instead — `וְהַחֹנִים קֵדְמָה מִזְרָחָה דֶּגֶל מַחֲנֵה יְהוּדָה` → *And those that encamp on the east side toward the sunrising shall be they of the standard of the camp of Judah* leaves the directive `he` its bare *on*. **A resultative prepositional predicate takes the ASV's supplied result or change frame on the same terms.** The preposition carries the relation that English expresses with the frame: `לְ` → *to be*, *shall be turned into*, *will be unto*, or *shalt become*. This is not rule 20's wholly supplied lexical verb, because removing the English frame removes the preposition's own result relation; any explicit Hebrew subject still takes the English subject pronoun separately. Head the preposition's unit on the English word carrying that relation — *to* in *to be*, *into* in *shall be turned into*, *unto* in *will be unto*, and *become* where no English preposition remains. The ordinary copular case is settled rather than a tie: the corpus reads it this way in 103 units across 39 chapters and never moves the copula onto the object noun instead. It is settled; the note need not hedge it. **A relational verb the ASV writes where a copula would stand goes the same way.** Where a possessive or belonging `לְ` heads the predicate and the ASV renders the clause with a verb of belonging rather than a bare copula — `אֲשֶׁר לַבָּיִת` → *which belonged to the house* — that verb is part of the preposition's own rendering and joins its unit, headed on the English preposition written for it. It is not rule 20's wholly supplied lexical verb: drop the verb and the preposition's relation is what has gone and nothing else, which is the subtraction test rule 43 applies to the same clause written with *had*. Where the ASV does write *had* or *have*, rule 43 governs; where it writes a bare copula, the first sentence of this rule does. **But every sentence above governs a phrase for which the ASV has written an English preposition, and says nothing about one for which it has written none.** Where the ASV spends no word on the preposition's own relation and instead writes that relation inside the following word — a possessive or belonging `לְ` rendered as an English genitive, `לַיהוָה` → *is Jehovah's* — rule 30's one-rendering condition is met: one English word carries both the relation and the lexeme, and the preposition shares that word's unit, headed on the word carrying the lexeme. The construction's copula then joins that one fused unit, exactly as it joins `לִפְנֵי` → *was before* under rule 30, which states that a copula the construction hands to the whole of an expression does not defeat the condition. Test by the subtraction rules 16, 30 and 43 use: drop the copula and ask whether the preposition has lost a rendering of its own or only the clause has lost the tense English writes for it. Where only the clause is diminished, the preposition has no separate English and fuses; where an English preposition of its own remains — *is in their hands* — the first sentence of this rule governs and the copula stays with it. Do not reach for rule 2 to split the fused pair on the ground that the noun or name holds an English word of its own: that reasoning is rule 57's, where each of two nouns holds a word, and here the preposition holds none. The corpus reads `לְ` + the divine name this way in six units across five chapters — *is Jehovah's*, *shall be Jehovah's*, *am Jehovah's*, and a bare *Jehovah's* — and never splits a copula onto the preposition beside a separately aligned name. **And every sentence above governs a preposition that is itself the clause's predicate, never one heading an adjunct the ASV has merely placed the copula beside.** Where a nominal predicate stands in the verbless clause and a prepositional phrase modifies it, the copula and any dummy subject are rule 20's and join that nominal, whichever order the ASV writes the two in: `חֻקַּת עוֹלָם לְדֹרֹתֵיכֶם` → *it shall be a statute for ever throughout your generations* and `מֵאֵת בְּנֵי־יִשְׂרָאֵל בְּרִית עוֹלָם` → *it is on the behalf of the children of Israel an everlasting covenant* are one construction, and both give the copula to the construct nominal, the second unit discontinuous around the fronted phrase under rule 25 while the preposition keeps only the English written for its own relation. Read the Hebrew clause for which side is the predicate rather than the English word order, exactly as rule 20 directs, since a phrase the ASV fronts puts the copula beside something that predicates nothing; and do not let a fused compound preposition draw the copula in under rule 30's one-rendering condition, which reaches a copula the construction hands to that expression and not one another morpheme is there to take. Where the clause writes no nominal predicate at all, nothing competes and the first sentence of this rule governs as before. **But a nominal standing beside a prepositional phrase is not a nominal predicate merely because the ASV writes the copula next to it.** Where the clause writes no subject but that one nominal, and the ASV writes no separate English subject for the clause either, the nominal is the clause's subject and the prepositional phrase is its predicate, so the copula stays on the preposition under this rule's first sentence — whichever order the ASV writes the two in, and however indefinite the English nominal is. `בְּפֶשַׁע שְׂפָתַיִם מוֹקֵשׁ רָע` → *In the transgression of the lips* ***is*** *a snare*, `בְּאֹרַח־צְדָקָה חַיִּים` → *In the way of righteousness* ***is*** *life*, `מִרְמָה בְּלֶב־חֹרְשֵׁי רָע` → *Deceit* ***is in*** *the heart of them that devise evil* and `וּלְיֹעֲצֵי שָׁלוֹם שִׂמְחָה` → *But* ***to*** *the counsellors of peace* ***is*** *joy* all put the copula on the preposition, and an indefinite *a snare* is no more a predicate than the definite construct chain the paragraph above calls a subject. What the preceding paragraph governs is a clause that has a subject of its own for the nominal to predicate of, which the ASV writes as a separate English subject — the *it* of *it shall be a statute for ever throughout your generations* and of *it is on the behalf of the children of Israel an everlasting covenant* — and a nominal that is itself the English subject of the copula in question is not that. So count the clause's nominals against the subjects the ASV writes, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s, before reading a phrase as an adjunct: where the only candidate subject is the nominal beside the preposition, the prepositional phrase is what predicates of it.

50. **The existential `אַיִן` heads on the negation word, not on the supplied *there*.** Its dummy subject and copula belong to the `אַיִן` unit exactly as rule 21's belong to `הָיָה`, but the head is the word carrying the negation — *no*, *none*, *not*, *neither*, *nothing* — since that is what the morpheme means, while *there* is supplied by English syntax. **The same analysis governs existential `אֶפֶס`.** Where the ASV writes *there is none* or *there is no*, the presentative frame belongs to `אֶפֶס` and the unit heads on *none* or *no*; uses meaning *ends*, *nought*, or another nonexistential lexeme are not governed by this sentence. **Only a supplied dummy belongs to the existential unit.** Where `אַיִן` bears a pronominal suffix and the ASV writes a referential subject pronoun, the suffix takes that pronoun under rule 18 while `אַיִן` keeps the copula and negation: `אֵינֶנּוּ` → *it was not* gives *it* to the suffix and *was not* to the existential. A suffix the ASV does not separately render remains `unrepresented` under rule 22. All 28 corpus occurrences head the existential this way and none heads on *there*, so this too is settled and not a tie to hedge. **But an interrogative particle in the clause takes the fronted question auxiliary, and the existential keeps only what is left.** Where `הֲ`, or the `אִם` opening a following disjunctive question, stands in an existential clause, the ASV's fronted *Is*, *Was* or *Shall* is what it has written for that particle under rule 17, exactly as rule 17's `הֲלוֹא לֶאֱמוּנָה` → *do not thine eyes look upon truth* gives *do* to `הֲ` in a clause with no verb to take it: `הַצֳרִי אֵין בְּגִלְעָד` → *Is there no balm in Gilead* gives *Is* to `הַ` and *there no* to `אַיִן`, and `הַיהוָה אֵין בְּצִיּוֹן` → *Is not Jehovah in Zion* gives *Is* to `הַ` and *not* to `אַיִן`. A dummy *there* the ASV writes still stays with the existential, and where it writes none there is nothing to place; the existential's head is the negation word in either case. **The Aramaic positive existential/copular `אִיתַי` takes whatever overt finite frame the ASV writes for it.** It takes *there is*, *there are*, *be*, *have*, or a finite auxiliary such as the *will* of `אִיתַ נָא פָלְחִין` → *we will serve*, while an explicit pronoun keeps its own English and a participial predicate keeps its lexical verb. Where the ASV writes no separate frame, as `אִיתֵי כוֹן פָלְחִין` → *ye serve*, the particle is `unrepresented`; do not move part of the lexical verb onto it merely because another occurrence carries an auxiliary.

51. **A waw the ASV renders by *also* takes it, wherever in the clause the ASV puts it.** This is rule 14's list — *but*, *then*, *or*, *now* — with the one member that keeps tempting an `added`. English writes *also* postpositively, after the fronted subject or between subject and verb — *Thy gates* ***also*** *shall be open*, *Thou shalt* ***also*** *suck the milk of the nations*, *I will* ***also*** *make thy officers peace* — so the word does not stand where the waw stands, and the unit is discontinuous under rule 25 rather than broken. Distance from the waw is not evidence against it, exactly as in rule 35. The corpus aligns a waw to *also* in 33 units across 21 chapters, so where a waw is present this is settled and the note need not hedge it. **But read whether a waw is present at all before placing the word.** Where no waw stands in the clause, no morpheme licenses the *also* and it is its own `added` unit under rule 30: `אֲשֶׁר לֹא יַשְׁאִיר לְךָ` → *that also shall not leave thee* has only the relative, the negative and the verb, and the *also* answers to none of them. Do not fold such an *also* into the relative, the negative or the verb to avoid the `added`, for the reason rule 42 gives about a surplus *and* — the word is written beside those renderings rather than inside one of them. **And a waw on which the ASV has already spent a connective of its own is in the same position as an absent one.** Where the ASV recasts the waw's clause and writes a coordinator or a subordinator for it at that clause's left edge, that word is the waw's rendering under rules 14 and 55, and a further *also* standing in the same clause answers to no morpheme and is `added`: `וְלֹא־תָקִיא הָאָרֶץ אֶתְכֶם` → ***that*** *the land vomit not you out* ***also*** gives *that* to the waw and leaves the *also* `added`. Count the clause's connectives against the morphemes that could carry them, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s and rule 52 counts its function words: a waw holding an English word of its own is not a home for a second one. Two limits. This does not reach the pair rule 55 governs, where a coordinator and a subordinator written together at the clause's left edge are one rendering of a single waw, nor rule 14's correlative pair, where *both … and*, *either … or* or *neither … nor* is one connective the ASV has written in two words; in each of those the two English words are one rendering rather than a second one. And it does not reach rule 25's distributed repetition, where the ASV rewrites one and the same connective over coordinated clauses.

52. **A purpose `לְ` + infinitive construct recast as a finite clause gives the complementizer to the preposition and the supplied subject and modal to the infinitive.** The ASV writes *that men may bring*, *that I may be glorified*. This is rule 48's division outside the privative `מִן`, and for the same reason: the complementizer is what the preposition contributes, while a supplied subject pronoun and a separately written modal belong to the verb, as rule 17 keeps a subject pronoun with its verb. The corpus puts the *that* on the preposition in 28 units across 17 chapters and never on the infinitive, so this too is settled. Where the ASV writes no English word at all for the preposition there is nothing to place, and the `לְ` goes `unrepresented` under rule 40. **Read that as no word, not as no complementizer.** The complementizer is what a finite recasting spends on the preposition, but a recasting that makes the infinitive an English gerund or a bare verbal noun writes an ordinary preposition in that same position, and that word is the `לְ`'s on the same terms: `לִשְׁמֹר אֶת־בְּרִיתוֹ` → *by keeping his covenant*, `לִשְׁלֹחַ מַלְאָכָיו` → *in sending his ambassadors*, `לְהָפֵר בְּרִית` → *by breaking the covenant*, `לָצֶקֶת` → *for casting*, `לֶאֱכֹל` → *an end of eating*. The corpus aligns a `לְ` before an infinitive construct to a written *to* in 583 units, to *that* in 81, and to *for*, *in*, *of*, *by*, *after* or *when* in 53 more, against 527 marked `unrepresented`; the two groups do not disagree, because every unrepresented one is a recasting that writes no function word at all — *left off building*, *the building of the house*. So ask whether a word is written before reaching for rule 40, and do not read the gerund itself as the recasting that silences the preposition. **Where two such `לְ` + infinitive phrases stand in one clause and the ASV writes one complementizer and one preposition, count them as this rule's last sentence directs rather than taking them in linear order.** `לִשְׁמֹר אֶת־בְּרִיתוֹ לְעָמְדָהּ` → *that by keeping his covenant it might stand* gives *by* to the `לְ` of the gerund and *that* to the `לְ` whose infinitive the finite clause renders: the ASV fronts the complementizer ahead of the gerund adjunct, and that reordering says nothing about which preposition it answers to. **A temporal preposition before an infinitive construct is read the same way.** Where `בְּ` or `כְּ` + infinitive is recast as a finite temporal clause — `בְּחֻמָּם` → *When they are heated*, `בְּלֶכְתּוֹ` → *when he went*, `כְּבֹאֲךָ` → *When thou comest*, `כְּכַלֹּתְךָ` → *when thou hast made an end* — the preposition takes the *when*, the infinitive keeps the verb, and the suffix takes the subject pronoun the ASV writes for it under rule 18. The corpus aligns `בְּ` to *when* in 45 units, so the temporal case is settled on the same footing as the purpose case. Read the preposition that is present rather than its lemma: a `לְ` heading such a phrase goes the same way even where its object is the noun rather than the infinitive, as in `לְקוֹל תִּתּוֹ` → *When he uttereth his voice*. **But only a preposition that still has an English word to take can take it.** Where no preposition is present and the construct relation itself is what the English *when* realizes, rule 19 puts that word on the *nomen rectum*, which is the infinitive: `עֵת הִדְרִיכָהּ` → *at the time when it is trodden* gives *at the time* to `עֵת` and *when ... is trodden* to the infinitive. **And a preposition on which the ASV has already spent its own English word is in the same position as an absent one.** Where the preposition heads a construct noun, the ASV writes a word for that preposition and a second temporal word besides, the second answers to the construct relation and not to the preposition, so rule 19 puts it on the *nomen rectum*: `בְּיוֹם הֵעָשׂוֹתוֹ` → *in the day when they shall make it* gives *in* to the `בְּ` and *the day* to `יוֹם`, leaving *when they shall make* to the infinitive, whose own suffix takes the pronoun rule 18 gives it. Count the ASV's function words against the morphemes that could carry them, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s and rule 29 counts the ASV's *the*s: a preposition holding an English word of its own is not a home for a second one, and giving it both would leave the construct's relation unrendered against rule 2. **But that count places the second word on a morpheme that is there to take it, and a noun the projection writes as an absolute has no rectum standing under it.** Where the preposition heads a noun MACULA tags absolute — as it does where a finite clause rather than an infinitive construct or another nominal follows the noun — no construct relation is written in the phrase at all, so rule 19 has nothing to place the second temporal word on: that word answers to no morpheme in the clause and is its own `added` unit under rule 55, while the preposition and the noun each keep the English written for them. `בְּיוֹם קָרָאתִי` → *In* ***the day*** ***that*** *I called* gives *In* to the `בְּ` and *the day* to `יוֹם`, the determiner supplied under rule 15, leaves *I called* to the finite verb, and marks the *that* `added`. Read MACULA's morphology rather than the pointing, exactly as rule 29 directs, and read what stands under the noun: an infinitive construct or another nominal under a construct noun is a rectum and the preceding sentences govern, and a finite clause under an absolute noun is not one. Rule 46 does not reach such a word either, since the noun the ASV writes before the clause is a written antecedent and that rule's headless case governs a clause with none. And where an apodosis follows whose waw the ASV leaves unrendered, that waw is `unrepresented` under rule 14 and is not a home for the supplied word, which stands ahead of the temporal clause rather than at the apodosis's head, exactly as rule 45 reads a *that* written inside its frame. **Where no preposition stands before the infinitive construct at all, the English infinitive marker *to* belongs to the infinitive's own unit and is never `added`.** English writes no bare infinitive complement, so that word is what the infinitive itself licenses, exactly as rule 15 gives a supplied *a* to the noun that licenses it and rule 46 gives a supplied *them that* to a bare participle: `מֵאֵן בִּלְעָם הֲלֹךְ עִמָּנוּ` → *Balaam refuseth* ***to come*** *with us* and `הֲיָכוֹל אוּכַל דַּבֵּר מְאוּמָה` → *have I now any power at all* ***to speak*** *anything* each give the *to* to the infinitive, headed on the lexical verb under rule 24. Read whether a preposition is present before reaching for this sentence, since one and the same infinitive is read both ways in one chapter: `לַהֲלֹךְ עִמָּכֶם` → *to go with you* gives the written *to* to the `לְ` and only *go* to `הֲלֹךְ`, while the bare `הֲלֹךְ` above holds both words. And this reaches only a *to* the ASV has written: a modal's bare complement — *cannot go beyond* — has none to place, and a preposition the recasting leaves unrendered is `unrepresented` under rule 40 as before. **And an infinitive construct with an overt subject takes the whole *for … to* frame the English clause requires.** English writes no infinitival clause with an overt subject without a complementizing *for* standing before that subject, so where the Hebrew writes a subject for a preposition-less infinitive construct and the ASV renders the pair as *for X to V*, both words are what the infinitive itself licenses on exactly the terms of the preceding sentences: they belong to that infinitive's one discontinuous unit under rule 25, wrapped around the subject, which keeps only its own English, and neither is `added`. `שֶׁבֶת אַחִים` → ***For*** *brethren* ***to dwell*** gives *For to dwell* to `שֶׁבֶת` and *brethren* to `אָח`, headed on the lexical verb under rule 24. Do not give the *for* to the subject noun under rule 33: what that rule places is a word stating what a bare nominal's own case-function contributes to its clause, and standing as the subject of an infinitive is not such a case-function, exactly as rule 16 says of a topicalized nominal's fronting. Do not mark it `added` under rule 55 either, since that rule governs a subordinator the ASV has written over a clause the Hebrew writes finite, and here the word is part of the frame the ASV's own infinitival recasting requires. Test by subtraction as elsewhere — drop the *for* and ask whether a nominal has lost the relation it bears to its clause, or whether the infinitival clause the ASV built has been left ungrammatical. Two limits. Where a preposition stands on the infinitive it takes the ASV's word first, on the terms above, a present morpheme taking its English before a supplied reading does. And an anticipatory *it* the ASV writes in the matrix clause for such an extraposed infinitival subject is no part of the frame: it is rule 25's expletive and is its own `added` unit, while the matrix predicate keeps the copula rule 20 gives it. **And the verb that takes such a bare *to* need not be an infinitive in the Hebrew.** Where a second Hebrew verb of any form — an imperative, a perfect, an imperfect — stands with no preposition on it and the ASV subordinates it to a governing verb as an English infinitive complement, the written *to* is what that English infinitive licenses and it joins the subordinated verb's own unit, headed on the lexical verb under rule 24, exactly as the bare `הֲלֹךְ` above holds it: `מַהֵר עֲנֵנִי` → *Make haste* ***to answer*** *me* gives *to answer* to `עָנָה` and *Make haste* to `מַהֵר`, and `אֵלֶיךָ כִסִּתִי` → *I flee unto thee* ***to hide*** *me* gives *to hide me* to `כָּסָה`. Read what the ASV made of the verb rather than what MACULA tags it, exactly as rule 19 reads a participle or infinitive the ASV has written as an English verb and rule 33 defers only to a governing verb the ASV has itself rendered as one. Do not give such a *to* to the governing verb: a verb already holding an English word of its own is not a home for a second one, as rule 51 says of a waw, and rule 44's supplied-preposition clause places a preposition the English verb's valency needs to reach its own object, which a complementizing *to* standing before a second verb is not. Two limits. Where a preposition morpheme does stand before that verb it takes the word first, on the terms above, a present morpheme taking its English before a supplied reading does. And where the governing verb and the subordinated verb are one and the same morpheme — a causative or other verb the ASV renders by a light verb and a second verb, as `הַשְׁמִיעֵנִי` → *Cause me* ***to hear*** and `הוֹשִׁיבַנִי` → *hath made me* ***to dwell*** — rule 44 keeps the whole phrase in that morpheme's one unit, headed on the lexical complement, and there is nothing to allocate.

## Poetry needs less than it looks

34. Gapping is not a new case. When a parallel line omits its verb, the copula the ASV supplies joins the predicate that licenses it, exactly as in rule 20, and is never `added`. **But that covers the copula and nothing more, exactly as rule 20's own boundary does.** Where the line's missing verb is a lexical one and the ASV writes a lexical verb for it, no morpheme in that line is there to carry the word, and it is its own `added` unit under rule 20's supplied-lexical-verb clause: `כִּי־מַפְרִיס פַּרְסָה הוּא וְלֹא גֵרָה` → *because he parteth the hoof but* ***cheweth*** *not the cud* gives *but* to the waw under rule 14, *not* to the negative, and *the cud* to the noun with its supplied *the* under rule 15, leaving the supplied *cheweth* `added`. Do not read *omits its verb* above as a licence to fold such a word into the negative, the connective or the noun standing beside it, for the reason rule 42 gives about a surplus *and* and rule 51 about a supplied *also*: the word is written beside those renderings rather than inside one of them. Test by subtraction as rules 16, 31 and 43 do — drop the English verb and ask whether a morpheme standing in that line has lost its rendering, or whether what has gone is a predication the Hebrew never wrote there. **But read whether the Hebrew writes that verb anywhere in the coordination before counting.** Where a verb the Hebrew writes once is realized twice across coordinated clauses, both occurrences are that verb's one possibly discontinuous unit under rule 25, exactly as a distributed `מִן` takes both of its *from*s there; what the preceding sentences govern is a line whose verb the Hebrew leaves unwritten altogether. **And the second realization need not repeat the first's English words.** English answers a gapped verb with a pro-verb as readily as with a repetition, so where the ASV writes *doth*, *do* or *did* for the colon whose verb the Hebrew gaps, or rewrites that verb's clause in other words, the word realizes the verb the Hebrew wrote once and joins its one discontinuous unit on exactly the terms above, headed on the first occurrence's lexical verb: `שֶׁמֶן וּקְטֹרֶת יְשַׂמַּח־לֵב וּמֶתֶק רֵעֵהוּ` → *Oil and perfume* ***rejoice*** *the heart, So* ***doth*** *the sweetness of a man's friend* gives both *rejoice* and *doth* to the one `יְשַׂמַּח`, while the waw keeps the *So* rule 14 writes for it. What tells such a word from the supplied lexical verb of the preceding sentences is whether the Hebrew writes the verb it stands for anywhere in the coordination, and not whether the ASV wrote the same English twice: a pro-verb names the very verb the first colon writes, while *cheweth* above names an action the Hebrew never wrote in that verse at all. An ASV italic on such a word corroborates it either way and decides nothing, by rule 7.
35. Expect more fusion rather than new rules: *Nor* for waw plus a negative, *But* for `כִּי אִם`, *whatsoever* for `כֹּל אֲשֶׁר`. The same *Nor* may rest on a present negative in one line and a gapped one in the next; align what is actually there. **Distance does not break these fusions.** Where the ASV's single word still says what every morpheme in it says, the unit stands however far apart they stand: `וְ ... לֹא` → *Nor* across a fronted prepositional phrase or object, `לֹא ... לְעוֹלָם` → *never* across the verb it negates. This is the far side of rule 30's contiguity condition, and the difference is what the English word carries. `גָּדַל ... מִן` → *exceeded* drops the preposition's own sense, so rule 2 splits it and the preposition is `unrepresented`; *Nor* still carries both the coordination and the negation and *never* both the negation and the time, so nothing is left over to mark. Head a *Nor* on the negative, which is what distinguishes it from a bare *and*. A *never* is a genuine tie between the negative and the temporal noun — the corpus heads it both ways — so choose, and say in the note which reading you took and that the corpus heads it both ways. **A single negative word written for a negative and a universal or indefinite quantifier standing in the same clause is one of these fusions.** Where Hebrew writes `כָל` on the noun, or an indefinite such as `מְאוּמָה` in the clause, and `לֹא`, or a prohibitive `אַל`, on its verb, and the ASV spends one word on the pair — `כָל־אָדָם לֹא־יִהְיֶה` → *there shall be* ***no*** *man*, `וְכָל־מְלָאכָה לֹא תַעֲשׂוּ` → *shall do* ***no*** *manner of work*, `לֹא־יִדְבַּק בְּיָדְךָ מְאוּמָה` → *there shall cleave* ***nought*** *to thy hand* — that word carries both the quantification and the negation, exactly as *whatsoever* carries both morphemes of `כֹּל אֲשֶׁר`, so the two share one unit, discontinuous around the noun and the verb under rule 25, and headed on the negative for the reason a *Nor* is: the negation is what distinguishes *no* from *all* and *nought* from *aught*. Do not read rule 15's determiner sentences as a direction to give the word to one of them and mark the other `unrepresented`; that rule counts one determiner against one morpheme, and here one English word answers to two. **But read whether the quantifier keeps an English word of its own before fusing.** What rule 15 governs is a quantifier the ASV still writes — a `כֹּל` rendered *any* or *all* beside a *no* or a *not* elsewhere — and what this sentence governs is one where the single negative word is the whole of what both morphemes are rendered by. The noun the quantifier stands on keeps its own English either way, including any generic the ASV supplies to expand it under rule 31, so *manner of work* is `מְלָאכָה`'s, headed on *work*, and the `מִן־הַחֵרֶם` of the `מְאוּמָה` clause above keeps its own *of the devoted thing*. Where the ASV instead writes a word for each — a *not* on the verb beside an *any* or an *all* on the noun — nothing is indivisible, rule 2 splits them, and each morpheme takes the word written for it. **A *cannot* is not one of these fusions, because the verb's lexeme stands outside it.** Where the ASV writes *cannot* for a negated verb, the word carries the negation together with a modal English has supplied, and it goes whole to the negative while the verb keeps its subject pronoun and its lexical verb under rule 17: `לֹא יִצְעָדוּ` → *they* ***cannot*** *go* gives *cannot* to `לֹא` and *they go* to the verb, exactly as rule 48 gives such a word to the privative `מִן` that carries the negation there. The corpus aligns `לֹא` alone to *cannot*, headed on it, in fourteen units. **But where `יָכֹל` stands in the clause, that verb is what the modal renders**, and the two morphemes share the one indivisible word — `לֹא אוּכַל` → *I cannot*, `לֹא יוּכְלוּ` → *they cannot*, `לֹא נוּכַל` → *we cannot* — headed on *cannot*, which is how the corpus reads all eleven of those units. And where the ASV writes the negation and the modal as two words instead — *are not able*, *shall not be able* — nothing is indivisible, rule 2 splits them, the negative takes only its own *not*, and the rest belongs to the verb, its complement headed by rule 44. Read the English the ASV wrote and the verb the Hebrew wrote, not the modality the Hebrew implies.
36. A vocative noun takes a supplied *O*, *ye*, or *thou* into its own unit, never `added`. **An article on the vocative does not take that pronoun.** The article takes only what the ASV actually writes for it, which for a vocative is usually nothing: `הַכֹּהֲנִים` → *ye priests* and `הַזְּקֵנִים` → *ye old men* are an `unrepresented` article beside a noun holding the whole *ye priests*, exactly as the far commoner anarthrous vocative is handled, and `הַמֶּלֶךְ` → *O king* goes the same way. Where the ASV instead builds a relative around the vocative — `הַחֲרֵדִים` → *ye that tremble* — the article takes the *that* under rule 41, and the *ye* still stays with the noun. Rule 41's supplied pronoun is the substantival article's; the vocative *ye* is not, since the anarthrous vocative supplies it with no article present at all. **But an imperative standing in the same clause has its own claim on such a pronoun, and the ASV's word order is what allocates it.** English writes an imperative's subject pronoun immediately after the verb and a vocative's immediately before the noun, so where a verb and a vocative both stand in the clause and the ASV writes a single *ye* or *thou*, read which word it stands beside: a pronoun written after the imperative is that verb's subject pronoun under rule 17, and one written before the vocative noun is the noun's under this rule. `הַלְלוּהוּ שֶׁמֶשׁ וְיָרֵחַ` → *Praise* ***ye*** *him, sun and moon* gives the *ye* to the verb and leaves the vocative its bare *sun*, while `הַלְלוּהוּ כָּל־כּוֹכְבֵי אוֹר` → *Praise him, all* ***ye*** *stars of light* gives it to `כּוֹכָב` and leaves the imperative its bare *Praise*; one verse may write both shapes, as PSA.148.3 does. Count the ASV's pronouns against the morphemes that could carry them, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s: where the ASV writes one for each — *Praise ye him, ye heavens* — each morpheme takes the one standing beside its own word, and where it writes none at all neither takes anything, the verb keeping only its own word under rule 17 and the vocative under this rule's first sentence. Position is what decides this and nothing else, because the two readings differ in no other respect: rule 14 reads a `וְעַתָּה` pair by which morpheme's own lexeme each English word writes, and rule 51 disregards a postposed *also*'s distance from the waw that licenses it, but a supplied subject pronoun writes no lexeme at all and both morphemes license it, so adjacency is the only evidence there is. An *O* the ASV writes before the vocative is that noun's on the same terms and is never the imperative's, since English writes no *O* for a verb's subject.

## Not evidence

37. The romanization shown beside each source token is a display aid. It never determines a unit, an edge, or a head.
38. Strong's numbers are secondary evidence, and neither an ASV italic nor any reference data overrides direct analysis of the Hebrew. **An ASV marginal note is read on exactly those terms.** The packet prints the edition's own notes beneath each verse — an alternative rendering (*Or, Send forth*, *Or, in*, *Or, spirit*, *Or, For*) and a literal gloss of the Hebrew (*Hebrew upon the face of the waters*) — and what is aligned is the text as printed, never the margin. A word the margin would have written is not a target token and licenses nothing; a construction the margin prefers does not move a unit, an edge or a head; and a preposition the margin renders one way keeps the word the printed text renders it by, so `בַּדָּרוֹם` → *toward the south* aligns *toward* to the `בְּ` whatever the margin offers. Such a note may corroborate an analysis the Hebrew already carries, exactly as an ASV italic may under rule 7: a literal gloss showing that a preposition and its construct noun were rendered by a single English word supports the fusion rule 30 finds there, and the absence of a note is not evidence against anything. But it decides nothing, and where the note and the printed text point different ways the printed text governs. Where a reader might otherwise wonder, say in the unit's note that the margin was read and set aside.
39. The gloss shown beside each source token can be wrong. Where MACULA segments a suffixed word, it sometimes distributes that word's gloss across the segments in reverse order: `כֻּלָּנוּ` is glossed *of.us* on `כֹּל` and *all* on the suffix, in six places across the vendored lowfat files. The surface, lemma, and morphology are correctly paired and are what to read; a gloss that contradicts them is a display defect, not a reading.

53. Where a ketiv/qere unit's decision leaves the projection carrying a reading the ASV **excludes**, English that answers to the missing source word is `added`, and the note must carry the unit id, the readings that remained, and why the fallback dropped the word. The packet marks such a unit `FALLBACK EXCLUDED BY EVIDENCE` beneath its variant line; an underdetermined unit whose qere fallback is still among the readings the English permits carries no such mark and is not this case. The reverse, where the retained reading is the longer one, makes the surplus tokens their own `unrepresented` unit and owes the same note. No Hebrew chapter has yet produced either — the ketiv and its qere are usually the same word differently written, so the two rarely differ in token count — and the rule stands so that the first one is recorded rather than explained away as a free English supply. Do not fold such a word onto a neighbouring token as a helper: the word that would have licensed it is not in the projection. This is `nt-greek-verse-v61` rule 97 holding for a ketiv/qere pair, and PHP.3.12 and JHN.9.28 are what it looks like when it happens. **But every sentence above governs a slot the packet has marked, and no other.** Where the ASV's English answers to a Hebrew word the projection does not carry and the packet records no variant unit in that slot at all, no textual decision has been taken for the alignment to honour and rule 9 forbids taking one here: align the English as printed against the token the projection does carry, on the terms the construction it stands in would otherwise take, and say in the unit's note that the English answers to a different reading of that word. `שֹׁפְטֵי צֶדֶק` → *the judges of the earth* keeps *of the earth* on `צֶדֶק` as the chain's rectum under rule 19, headed on *earth*, rather than marking the noun `unrepresented` and the English `added`. Rule 24's direction to head on the token most directly realizing the source head's lexeme is not defeated by this: where the divergence leaves no such token in the unit, head where the construction puts it, and name the divergence in the note under rule 8. An ASV marginal note reporting the other reading corroborates the divergence and decides nothing, by rule 38. Read the packet's variant lines before reaching for this paragraph, since a marked unit is what the sentences above govern, and do not read a lexical mismatch as licence to mark one side `unrepresented` and the other `added` where the pipeline has recorded no decision to record.
54. **A verse the pinned source edition does not contain is resolved before the packet is built, and the briefing says which way.** The ASV's Nehemiah 7:68 — the horses and mules of the returning company — stands in the Hebrew the ASV was translated from and not in the Leningrad Codex, which is why WLC numbers the rest of that chapter one lower. Where the chapter records a parallel verse of the same edition that supplies it, the packet carries that verse's Hebrew and marks it `(supplied from ...)` under `-- source`: it is ordinary source text and is aligned by every other rule here, and the verse's `review_notes` name the verse it is taken from. Where the chapter records instead that nothing supplies it, the packet has no source tokens at all: put every target token into a single `added` unit with a null target head and say in its note that the source edition omits the verse, which is the one place `added` covers a whole verse. Either way nothing is missing from the packet and nothing is to be hunted for in a neighbouring verse. **A verse boundary the two editions draw in different places is resolved inside each verse, on those same terms.** Where the ASV writes at the end of one verse a clause the pinned source edition carries at the head of the next, each verse's packet holds the tokens its own edition assigns to it and neither verse is short of anything. In the verse where the ASV writes the English, that English answers to no token the verse carries: it is one `added` unit with a null target head, its words standing together and doing one office in the clause, exactly as rule 2 places any run of supplied English, and it is not folded into a neighbouring morpheme's unit to avoid the mark. In the verse where the source edition carries the tokens, each of those morphemes is its own `unrepresented` unit, since no correspondence stands between them to be tested and rule 2 prefers the smallest unit. Say in the `review_notes` of both verses which verse holds the other side, so that a later reader finds the whole clause. Do not reach into the neighbouring verse for the missing half and do not read the divergence as a textual decision: rule 10 makes the verse the whole unit of work, and rule 9 forbids taking a new textual decision inside the alignment file, while the two editions' versification is settled before the packet is built. 1CH.27.30-31 is what it looks like when it happens, the ASV closing verse 30 with *and over the flocks was Jaziz the Hagrite* where WLC opens verse 31 with `וְעַל־הַצֹּאן יָזִיז הַהַגְרִי`.
55. **A subordinating conjunction the ASV supplies for a clause whose Hebrew writes none is `added`.** Casuistic law opens its protasis with אִם, with כִּי, or with a waw, and where one of these stands in the clause it takes the ASV's *if*, *when* or *that* by rules 14 and 30: the corpus aligns a waw to *if* and to *when*, and כִּי takes whichever of them the ASV has written for it. But the ASV also writes such a word for a clause that has no conjunction in it at all — *and, behold,* ***if*** *the plague be spread* for וְהִנֵּה פָּשָׂה הַנֶּגַע, and the italic ***yet*** of *he is bald,* ***yet*** *is he clean* for קֵרֵחַ הוּא טָהוֹר הוּא — and there no morpheme licenses it: it is its own `added` unit under rule 30. Do not fold it into the waw that opens the clause, into הִנֵּה, or into the verb, for the reason rule 42 gives about a surplus *and* and rule 51 about a supplied *also* — the word is written beside those renderings rather than inside one of them, and the waw and הִנֵּה already have the *and* and the *behold* the ASV wrote for them. Count the clause's conjunctions against the ASV's subordinators, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s, and mark whichever side is in surplus. **But a coordinator and a subordinator the ASV writes together for one connective morpheme are one rendering of that morpheme, and neither word is a surplus.** Where the ASV recasts that connective's own clause as a subordinate one and writes the pair at that clause's left edge — *And when I looked* for וָאֶרְאֶה, *And when I see the blood* for וְרָאִיתִי, and likewise *and that* and *so that* — both English words belong to that connective's single unit, and the count above marks nothing `added` unless no conjunction stands in the clause at all. Contiguity is the test, exactly as in rule 30: what makes the Leviticus *if* above a surplus is that הִנֵּה's *behold* stands between it and the waw's *and*, so the *if* is written beside those renderings rather than as part of one connective. **Read the connective that is there and not its lemma.** A disjunctive אוֹ whose clause the ASV recasts the same way takes the pair on the same terms as a waw — `אוֹ־עָבַר עָלָיו רוּחַ־קִנְאָה` → ***or if*** *the spirit of jealousy come upon him* is one unit on the אוֹ, headed on *or* — since what licenses the pair is the one connective the ASV has rendered by it and not the lemma, exactly as rule 14 reads אוֹ and a waw alike where one connective is written in two English words. But where a morpheme of its own stands in the clause for the subordinator, that morpheme takes it and there is no pair to place: `אוֹ אִישׁ אֲשֶׁר תַּעֲבֹר עָלָיו` → *or* ***when*** *the spirit of jealousy cometh upon a man* gives *or* to the אוֹ and *when* to the אֲשֶׁר, a present morpheme taking its English before a fused reading does. Head such a unit on the coordinator, which is what the connective itself is; the corpus aligns a waw to *and when* in twenty-seven units, to *so that* in thirty-eight and to *and that* in eight, heading eleven of the *and when* units on *when* instead, so name that competing reading in the note under rule 8. **But read that head directive by what the written connective is, and not by which English word stands first.** What it says is that the head goes on the word the connective itself is, and the connective of every case above is a coordinator. Where instead the morpheme the pair renders is itself a subordinator — a כִּי or an אִם the ASV answers with *for though*, *and though*, *and if* or *and when* — the subordinating word is what realizes that morpheme's own lexeme and the coordinator standing beside it realizes nothing of it, so head there, exactly as rule 24 puts a target head on the token most directly realizing the source head's lexeme and rule 23 heads a fused unit on the morpheme carrying the content. `כִּי יַעַר הוּא` → ***for though*** *it is a forest* and `כִּי חָזָק הוּא` → ***and though*** *they are strong* both head on *though*, and the bare `כִּי` → *though* the ASV writes beside them in that verse is what identifies the word. Read the Hebrew morpheme rather than the English order, since the coordinator stands first in either case and one verse may write both pairs, as JOS.17.18 does; where the pair's two words both render senses the connective itself carries, choose the one subordinating the clause the unit heads and name the other in the note under rule 8. **And a subordinator the ASV writes a second time for a clause coordinated under one the Hebrew has already written is that morpheme's own repeated realization, not a surplus.** Where a single אֲשֶׁר or כִּי governs coordinated clauses and the ASV rewrites its *that* at the head of a later one, every occurrence belongs to that morpheme's one possibly discontinuous unit under rule 25, exactly as a distributed `מִן` takes both of its *from*s there and a paraphrased suffix takes both of its pronouns under rule 18; head on the first, and mark nothing `added`. `לְמַעַן אֲשֶׁר לֹא־יִגְבְּהוּ ... וְלֹא־יִתְּנוּ ... וְלֹא־יַעַמְדוּ` → *to the end* ***that*** *none ... exalt themselves ..., neither set their top ..., nor* ***that*** *their mighty ones stand up* gives both *that*s to the one אֲשֶׁר, the waws keeping only the *neither* and *nor* they are fused into. So read whether the later clause is coordinated under an earlier subordinator's scope before counting: what the count governs is a subordinator standing over a clause no written subordinator reaches, as the Leviticus protasis above has no conjunction in it at all. **And read that scope before reading a coordinator and a subordinator as a pair.** Where a written subordinator governs coordinated clauses and the ASV opens a later one with both a connective for its waw and a rewritten copy of that subordinator, the two English words answer to two morphemes and not to one: the waw takes only the coordinator written for it, and the subordinator's second occurrence joins the written אִם, כִּי, אֲשֶׁר or לְמַעַן as that morpheme's discontinuous unit, headed on the first. `וְאִם־לֹא קָרוֹב אָחִיךָ אֵלֶיךָ וְלֹא יְדַעְתּוֹ` → *And* ***if*** *thy brother be not nigh unto thee,* ***or if*** *thou know him not* gives *or* to the second waw and both *if*s to the one אִם, which opens the protasis and governs both of its members, exactly as לְמַעַן takes both *that*s of `לְמַעַן יִיטַב לָךְ וְהַאֲרַכְתָּ יָמִים` → *that it may be well with thee, and* ***that*** *thou mayest prolong thy days* while its waw keeps only the *and*. This is the carve-out the preceding paragraph makes for a morpheme standing in the clause itself, read for a subordinator whose scope reaches that clause from an earlier member of the coordination: a present morpheme takes its English before a fused reading does, whichever member of the coordination it stands in, and a waw already holding a connective of its own is not a home for a second word, as rule 51 says. So ask whether the subordinator the ASV has written a second time is one the Hebrew has already written for that same coordination before giving the pair to the connective. What the pair sentence governs is a clause no written subordinator reaches, which is what `וָאֶרְאֶה` → *And when I looked* is, and reading the two shapes apart is a matter of the Hebrew's scope rather than of which English words stand side by side. **And a word that belongs to a formula's own fixed English rendering is not a subordinator supplied for the clause: it belongs to the morpheme the formula rests on.** The oath חַי־אָנִי is written *As I live*, and the *As* is what English spends on the adverbial force of that predicate adjective, exactly as rule 20 takes the copula from the construction and rule 30 takes the pronouns of נָא's *I beseech thee* from the particle's one idiomatic rendering: the adjective takes *As … live* in one unit, discontinuous around the pronoun under rule 25 and headed on *live*, while an explicit אָנִי or אֲנִי takes only its own *I*. The corpus reads the formula this way in six chapters and never marks the *As* `added`. So before counting an English subordinator as a surplus, read whether it is one the formula always writes; the count above governs a word the ASV has supplied for the clause, not a word the formula's rendering contains.
56. **A cognate infinitive absolute the ASV renders with a distinct adverbial word takes that word as its own unit.** The finite cognate verb retains its subject, auxiliaries and lexical verb phrase under rule 17, while the infinitive absolute takes the English word spent on its emphasis and heads on that word: `פָּקֹד יִפְקֹד` → *will surely visit* gives *surely* to the infinitive absolute and *will visit* to the finite verb, and `הָשֵׁב יָשִׁיב` → *will fully requite* gives *fully* and *will requite* on the same terms. This is rule 2's smallest defensible unit rather than rule 30 fusion, because the ASV has written a separate English word for the infinitive absolute's contribution. The finished corpus has 34 Qal infinitive-absolute units across 26 chapters independently aligned to *surely*, *utterly*, *indeed*, *diligently* or *certainly*, all headed on the intensifier. **Read that word as whatever the ASV has spent on the emphasis, and not as a class of intensifying adverbs.** Where the clause is a rhetorical, concessive or conditional one and the ASV writes a minimizing or restrictive adverb in that same slot, the word is the infinitive absolute's on exactly the same terms and heads its unit: `וְאָבִיהָ יָרֹק יָרַק בְּפָנֶיהָ` → *If her father had* ***but*** *spit in her face* gives *but* to the infinitive absolute and *had spit* to the finite verb. **And read *word* as one English rendering rather than one English token, exactly as rule 30 reads its own one-word condition.** Where the ASV spends a fixed multi-word adverbial expression on that emphasis, the whole expression is the infinitive absolute's one unit: no part of it is `added`, no part of it is a neighbouring morpheme's, and the unit heads under rules 24 and 31 on the content word the expression turns on rather than on one of its function words. `הֲיָכוֹל יָכְלוּ אֱלֹהֵי גּוֹיֵי הָאֲרָצוֹת` → *Were the gods of the nations of the lands* ***in any wise*** *able* gives *in any wise* to the infinitive absolute, headed on *wise*, while the finite verb keeps its bare *able* and the interrogative particle keeps the fronted copula rule 20 gives it; the *in* and the *any* state nothing on their own and no morpheme in the clause answers to either, so rule 15's supplied determiner and rule 11's function-word priority do not reach them. But read what stands in the clause before taking the whole expression: where a negative, a `כֹּל` or another morpheme is itself what a word of that expression says, that morpheme takes it under rules 35, 41 and 48 and the infinitive absolute takes only the rest. Test by subtraction as rules 16, 31 and 43 do: drop the English word and ask whether the infinitive absolute has lost its whole rendering or only the clause has lost a shade of force. Do not mark such a word `added` under rule 30 or fold it into the finite verb, into a conjunction or into a negative standing beside it, for the reason rule 42 gives about a surplus *and* and rule 51 about a supplied *also*: a morpheme stands in the clause for it, no other morpheme answers to it, and the word is written beside those renderings rather than inside one of them. Where the ASV writes no distinct adverbial word at all for the infinitive absolute, the ordinary rules decide what, if anything, realizes it. **And a finite auxiliary verb whose whole contribution the ASV writes as an adverb is read on exactly those terms.** Hebrew writes a second verb where English writes an adverb of repetition, continuation or degree — `שׁוּב` for *again*, `יָסַף` for *any more*, `רָבָה` hiphil for *much* — and where the ASV spends its English on that verb's contribution while another verb in the clause holds the lexical predicate, the auxiliary verb takes that word as its own unit and heads on it, exactly as an infinitive absolute takes the word spent on its emphasis. `וַיָּשָׁב וַיִּבֶן אֶת־הַבָּמוֹת` → *he built* ***again*** *the high places* gives *again* to `שׁוּב` and *he built* to `בָּנָה`; `לֹא אֹסִיף לְהָנִיד` → *neither will I cause ... to wander* ***any more*** gives *will I any more* to `יָסַף`, headed on *more*, and *cause ... to wander* to the infinitive; `הִרְבָּה לַעֲשׂוֹת הָרַע` → *he wrought* ***much*** *evil* gives *much* to `רָבָה`. The subject pronoun, the auxiliaries and the infinitive marker are placed as they otherwise would be by rules 17, 48 and 52 — on the auxiliary verb where it is the clause's finite verb and on the other verb where the ASV has made that one the English predicate — and the unit is discontinuous under rule 25 where the adverb stands apart from them. Do not mark such an adverb `added`, and do not fold it into the lexical verb's unit as a phrasal particle under rule 44: what that rule's surplus-particle sentence governs is a word no morpheme licenses, and here a written verb is what the word renders, which the subtraction test rules 16, 31 and 43 use finds gone with it while the lexical verb keeps its whole rendering either way. Read the source verbs against the English words, as rule 42 counts waws against *and*s: where the ASV writes a verb for each — *returned and built* — rule 2 splits them and each takes its own, and where it writes nothing at all for the auxiliary verb the ordinary rules decide what, if anything, realizes it. **And a connective the ASV leaves unwritten between two such verbs is counted as rule 14 directs.** Hebrew coordinates the pair with a waw that English does not write, so where two conjunctions stand and the ASV writes one connective, the surplus waw is `unrepresented`; do not fold it into either verb's unit to avoid the mark.
57. **The 1cs suffix of the divine title `אֲדֹנָי` is lexicalized in the name and takes no English of its own.** In `אֲדֹנָי יְהוִה` → *the Lord Jehovah* the ASV writes no possessive anywhere in the title, so the suffix is its own `unrepresented` unit while `אֲדֹנָ` takes *the Lord*, the supplied *the* joining that noun under rule 15 and the copula of *I am the Lord Jehovah* joining it under rule 20. This is not rule 18: that rule places a suffix the ASV has rendered, and here the ASV has rendered none, so do not reach for a *my* the title does not write. The corpus marks a suffix standing between a `Ncmpc` noun and a proper name `unrepresented` in 250 units across 85 chapters, and aligns `אֲדֹנָ` itself to *the Lord* in 249 units, to *O Lord* in 19, to *of the Lord* in 10 and to *am the Lord* in 4, each time as its own unit. Eleven units in Isaiah instead fuse the pair as one `noncompositional` unit under rule 6; that rule reaches a genuinely indivisible phrase, and rule 2 prefers the split wherever the base noun holds an English word of its own, which in this title it always does. **The order in which the title's two elements stand is no part of the condition.** Where the divine name is written first — `יְהוִה אֲדֹנָי` → *O Jehovah the Lord* — the suffix stands at the end of the title rather than between the construct noun and the name, and nothing changes: the name takes its own English together with any supplied *O* under rule 36, `אֲדֹנָ` takes *the Lord* with the supplied *the* under rule 15, and the suffix is `unrepresented` as before. The corpus signature quoted above describes the commoner order and is not the test; what the test asks is whether the ASV has written a possessive anywhere in the title, which is the subtraction rules 16 and 43 run. **Where the ASV does write a possessive for the suffix, this rule does not reach it.** Outside the fixed title — *O Lord my God*, and the like — rule 18 governs as usual and the suffix takes the word written for it.
58. **An age written with `בֶּן` gives the English *old* to that noun.** Hebrew states an age as `בֶּן` with a numeral, usually with `שָׁנָה` standing after it, and the ASV writes *X years old*: the `בֵּן` takes the *old*, the numeral takes its own word, and `שָׁנָה` takes the *years* as the counted noun, which rule 22 places where a compound numeral distributes it. Do not mark the `בֵּן` `unrepresented` on the ground that no English word writes *son*, and do not fuse the phrase under rule 30: each morpheme has an English word of its own, so rule 2 splits them, and the subtraction test rules 16, 31 and 43 use finds the statement of age gone with the *old* while the numeral and the counted noun are left saying only how many years. The unit is discontinuous under rule 25 wherever the counted noun stands between, which in English is always, since Hebrew writes the `בֵּן` before its numeral and the ASV writes the *old* after the years. The corpus aligns `בֵּן` to *old* in a hundred and four units, to *was old* in twenty-eight, to *a old* in ten and to *being old* in five, and heads every one of them on the word carrying the age, so this is settled rather than a tie to hedge. Three riders. Where the clause is verbless and the ASV writes a copula, that word is the predicate's and joins the `בֵּן` unit under rule 20, which is what those *was old* units are. Where a preposition stands before the `בֵּן` it takes whatever the ASV writes for it under rule 11, as `מִבֶּן עֶשְׂרִים שָׁנָה` → ***from*** *twenty years old* does, and where the ASV leaves that relation unstated the preposition is `unrepresented` under rule 40 as usual. And this governs the age construction only: a `בֵּן` naming a son, and the `בָּנִים` of a bird's young that the ASV writes *young*, are read by the ordinary rules. **And where the Hebrew writes no counted noun at all, the *years* the ASV supplies completes the numeral and is never `added`.** Hebrew states the same age without `שָׁנָה` as readily as with it — `בֶּן־שְׁלֹשִׁים וּשְׁתַּיִם הָיָה בְמָלְכוֹ` → *Thirty and two* ***years*** *old was he when he began to reign* — and there the numeral stands for what it counts, so the supplied noun joins that numeral's unit under rule 31's substantival numeral and the head stays on the numeral, which carries the lexeme. Read which numeral it joins as rule 22 reads a counted noun distributed across a compound figure: English writes the noun once after the whole figure, so the supplied *years* joins the last numeral of the Hebrew one, standing exactly where a written `שָׁנָה` would have stood. Do not mark it `added` under rule 20's supplied-lexical-verb clause or rule 33's supplied-phrase sentence, and do not fold it into the `בֵּן` beside it: what those clauses govern is English answering to no morpheme at all, and here the numeral the word completes is written. And read the Hebrew before reaching for this sentence, since a written `שָׁנָה` takes the *years* itself under this rule's first sentence, and one chapter may write both shapes, as 2CH.21.5 and 2CH.21.20 do.
